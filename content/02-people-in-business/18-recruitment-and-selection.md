## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Recruitment | The process of identifying that a job vacancy exists and attracting suitable candidates to apply for it | A supermarket advertising for a new checkout assistant |
| Selection | The process of choosing the most suitable candidate from those who have applied | A panel interviewing three shortlisted candidates and choosing one |
| Job analysis | The process of examining a job to identify its duties, tasks and requirements | Observing a role to understand what tasks it involves before hiring |
| Job description | A document outlining the duties, responsibilities and conditions of a specific job | A document stating "responsible for daily stock checks and customer service" |
| Person specification | A document outlining the qualifications, skills, experience and personal qualities required of the ideal candidate | "Must hold a full driving licence and have 2 years' customer service experience" |
| Internal recruitment | Filling a vacancy with an existing employee of the business | Promoting a shop floor supervisor to store manager |
| External recruitment | Filling a vacancy with someone from outside the business | Advertising a new marketing role on a jobs website |
| Job advertisement | A notice, internal or external, informing potential candidates of a vacancy | A LinkedIn post advertising an accountant role |
| Curriculum vitae (CV) | A document prepared by a candidate summarising their qualifications, skills and work history | A candidate's CV listing their degree and previous jobs |
| Application form | A standardised form provided by the employer for candidates to complete with relevant information | An online form asking for education history and references |
| Shortlisting | The process of selecting a smaller number of the most suitable applicants to interview | Narrowing 50 applicants down to 5 for interview |
| Interview | A formal meeting (face-to-face, phone, or online) used to assess a candidate's suitability | A panel interview with the HR manager and department head |
| Reference | A statement from a previous employer or other credible source about a candidate's character and ability | A former manager confirming a candidate's reliability |
| Recruitment agency | An external organisation that helps businesses find and select suitable candidates | A business using a recruitment agency to headhunt a senior executive |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Getting recruitment right is one of the highest-stakes decisions a business makes: a poor hire can cost a business far more than a salary in wasted training, lost productivity, and team disruption, while the right hire can transform a department. Fast-growing companies such as Amazon and Netflix are famous for treating recruitment as a core strategic function rather than an administrative task — Netflix, for instance, has openly stated that hiring, developing, and retaining "stunning colleagues" is central to its culture and performance, showing why every business, however small, must think carefully about how it finds and chooses its people.

## Core Content

### 1. Recruitment vs Selection
**Recruitment** is the process of identifying a vacancy and attracting a pool of suitable applicants. **Selection** is the subsequent process of assessing those applicants and choosing the best candidate. Students often conflate the two, but recruitment is about generating a strong field of candidates, while selection is about choosing between them — a business can have excellent selection tools but still hire poorly if its recruitment process attracted weak candidates in the first place.

### 2. The Recruitment and Selection Process
A typical, logical sequence is:
1. **Identify the vacancy** — arising from staff leaving, business expansion, or a new role being created.
2. **Job analysis** — examining what the job actually involves (tasks, responsibilities, reporting lines).
3. **Job description** — a formal document based on the job analysis, describing the duties, responsibilities, pay, and conditions of the role.
4. **Person specification** — a formal document describing the qualifications, experience, skills and personal attributes the ideal candidate should have.
5. **Advertising the vacancy** — internally (staff notice boards, intranet) and/or externally (job websites, newspapers, social media, recruitment agencies, job centres).
6. **Receiving applications** — via CVs and/or application forms.
7. **Shortlisting** — narrowing the applicant pool down against the person specification.
8. **Selection** — using interviews, tests, and references to choose the best candidate.
9. **Job offer and contract** — formally offering the role, subject to references/checks (links to Chapter 19, Legal Controls over Employment).

### 3. Internal vs External Recruitment
**Internal recruitment** fills a vacancy from within the existing workforce, e.g. through promotion or transfer.
- *Advantages:* cheaper and faster than external recruitment; the candidate is already known to the business and understands its culture; can motivate existing staff by offering career progression (links to Chapter 21, Motivation); less induction/training needed.
- *Disadvantages:* limits the pool of talent and fresh ideas entering the business; creates a new vacancy elsewhere that must then be filled; may cause resentment among unsuccessful internal candidates.

**External recruitment** fills a vacancy from outside the business.
- *Advantages:* brings in new skills, experience, and perspectives; widens the pool of potential candidates, increasing the likelihood of finding the best possible fit.
- *Disadvantages:* more expensive and time-consuming (advertising costs, agency fees, longer selection process); higher risk, as the candidate's true suitability and fit with the business is less certain; requires more induction and training.

### 4. Methods of Selection
- **Application forms:** standardised, allowing easier direct comparison between candidates, but less personal than a CV.
- **CVs:** allow candidates to present themselves in their own way, but make direct comparison between candidates harder and give applicants scope to omit weaknesses.
- **Interviews:** the most widely used selection method; allow two-way communication (see Chapter 16) and assessment of communication skills and personality, but can be subjective and are vulnerable to unconscious bias.
  - *One-to-one interviews* — a single interviewer and candidate.
  - *Panel interviews* — multiple interviewers, reducing individual bias and allowing multiple perspectives.
  - *Group interviews/assessment centres* — multiple candidates assessed together, often through tasks, useful for observing teamwork and leadership skills.
- **Tests:** aptitude tests, skills tests, or psychometric tests used to objectively assess a candidate's abilities relevant to the role.
- **References:** statements from previous employers, educators or other credible sources, used to verify a candidate's claims and assess character/reliability. References are usually taken up after a provisional offer, not before.

**Exam pitfall:** Students often assume interviews are always the "best" method. In fact, the most appropriate method depends on the role — e.g. a fast-food business hiring many part-time staff may rely more heavily on group interviews or straightforward application forms for speed and cost reasons, while a senior executive role may involve multiple interview rounds, psychometric testing, and detailed reference checks.

### 5. Costs of Recruitment
Recruitment is costly in both time and money: advertising costs, agency fees, staff time spent shortlisting and interviewing, and the eventual costs of inducting and training a new employee (see Chapter 20). A poor recruitment decision compounds these costs through low productivity, the expense of managing underperformance, and the cost of repeating the whole process if the employee leaves or is dismissed.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Netflix, media and entertainment streaming, United States.
**Situation:** Netflix has publicly built its culture (documented in its widely circulated "Netflix Culture" memo) around hiring highly capable, autonomous employees ("stunning colleagues"), being willing to pay top-of-market salaries to attract talent, and moving quickly to part ways with employees who are not a strong fit, treating recruitment and selection as a continuous strategic priority rather than a one-off HR task.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a large business can use a distinctive recruitment and selection philosophy (rigorous external hiring, high pay for high performance) as a source of competitive advantage.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss whether Netflix's approach to recruitment would be suitable for a small business."

**Company/context:** Jumia, e-commerce, Nigeria/pan-African.
**Situation:** As one of Africa's largest e-commerce platforms, Jumia has grown rapidly across multiple African countries and has had to build large-scale recruitment operations to hire logistics, technology, and customer service staff across diverse local labour markets, often combining external recruitment (to bring in specialist e-commerce/tech skills) with local hiring and training to build country-specific teams.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the recruitment challenges of a fast-growing business expanding across diverse emerging markets, and the balance between external specialist hiring and building local talent pipelines.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse the recruitment challenges a fast-growing e-commerce business might face when expanding into new international markets."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often recruits informally and internally where possible, relying on word of mouth, local advertising, or promoting a trusted existing employee, since it cannot afford lengthy or costly recruitment processes and needs new staff to fit into a small, close-knit team quickly. A large multinational business typically uses a more formal, structured process — dedicated HR departments, recruitment agencies, psychometric testing, and multiple interview stages — because it is filling many vacancies, often across different countries, and the cost of a poor hire in a specialist or senior role can be very high.
