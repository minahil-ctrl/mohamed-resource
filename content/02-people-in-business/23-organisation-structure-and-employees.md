## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
As a business grows from a single founder to a multinational employing thousands, the informal way decisions get made has to be replaced with a clear structure — otherwise nobody knows who is responsible for what, or who reports to whom. Amazon, for example, is famous for its deliberately flat, decentralised structure around individual "two-pizza teams" (teams small enough to be fed by two pizzas) that are given autonomy to make their own decisions quickly, while many large government bodies and traditional manufacturing firms retain tall, centralised hierarchies with many layers of management. How a business chooses to structure itself has a direct effect on the speed of decision-making, communication, cost, and employee motivation (linking back to Chapters 21–22).

## Core Content

### 1. Organisation Charts and Their Purpose
An **organisation chart** is a diagram that shows the formal structure of a business: the job roles that exist, how they relate to each other, which department each role sits in, and — critically — the lines of authority and reporting between them. Organisation charts are useful because they clarify who is responsible for what, show employees who they report to and who reports to them, help identify duplicated roles or gaps in responsibility, and make clear how communication should formally flow through the business (see Chapter 16, Good Communication). Organisation charts are typically drawn with the most senior position (e.g. the Managing Director or CEO) at the top, with each subsequent layer below representing a lower level of authority, connected by lines showing direct reporting relationships.

### 2. Hierarchy, Chain of Command, and Span of Control
The **hierarchy** of a business refers to the number of distinct levels of authority it has, from the most senior manager down to the most junior employee. Each level in the hierarchy is sometimes called a "layer." The **chain of command** is the line of authority running through these levels — the path an instruction takes as it passes from the top of the business down to the person who must carry it out, and the path information takes as it is reported back up. A **long chain of command** (many levels between the top and the bottom) can slow down communication and decision-making, as messages must pass through more people, and can distort messages if each layer interprets or summarises the instruction before passing it on. A **short chain of command** allows faster, more direct communication, but places more direct oversight demands on senior managers.

The **span of control** is the number of subordinates who report directly to a single manager. A manager with a **narrow span of control** oversees few subordinates directly, allowing closer supervision and support for each, but requiring more managers (and therefore more layers of hierarchy) to oversee a given total workforce. A manager with a **wide span of control** oversees many subordinates directly, which requires fewer managers and layers overall, but gives each subordinate less individual attention and support, and demands strong organisational and delegation skills from the manager.

**Key relationship (frequently examined):** For a business of a given total size (total number of employees), a **narrow** span of control at each level requires **more** levels of hierarchy to manage the same workforce, producing a **tall** structure; conversely, a **wide** span of control requires **fewer** levels, producing a **flat** structure. This inverse relationship between span of control and number of hierarchy levels is one of the most commonly tested ideas in this topic.

**Exam pitfall:** Students frequently confuse "span of control" (subordinates reporting to one manager, a *horizontal* measure) with "levels of hierarchy" (the number of layers, a *vertical* measure). A strong answer keeps these clearly distinct and states the inverse relationship between them explicitly.

### 3. Tall vs Flat Organisational Structures
A **tall structure** has many levels of hierarchy and, correspondingly, a narrow span of control at each level. Advantages include closer supervision and support for each employee, clearer and more defined promotion pathways, and more specialised management roles at each level. Disadvantages include slower communication and decision-making (due to the long chain of command), higher costs (more managers to employ and pay), and the risk that employees at the bottom feel distant from senior decision-makers.

A **flat structure** has few levels of hierarchy and a wide span of control at each level. Advantages include faster communication and decision-making (a short chain of command), lower management costs, and — often — greater employee empowerment and motivation, since staff typically have more autonomy and direct access to senior management (linking to Chapter 22's discussion of empowerment as a non-financial motivator). Disadvantages include managers being potentially overstretched trying to supervise a large number of direct subordinates, fewer opportunities for promotion, and the risk of managers lacking time to give each subordinate sufficient individual guidance.

**Exam pitfall:** A very common error is assuming a flat structure is always "better" for motivation. While flat structures often support empowerment, an excessively wide span of control can leave staff without adequate guidance or support, which can *reduce* motivation and increase errors — a valid evaluative counterpoint that examiners reward when raised.

### 4. Delegation
**Delegation** is the process by which a manager passes down authority and responsibility for a specific task to a subordinate, while the manager typically remains ultimately accountable for the outcome. Effective delegation can free up senior managers' time for higher-level strategic tasks, develop subordinates' skills and confidence, and — by giving employees greater responsibility — act as a form of job enrichment that supports motivation (Chapter 22). However, delegation requires trust in the subordinate's competence, clear instructions and appropriate authority to match the responsibility given, and carries risk if the subordinate is inadequately trained, supervised, or supported — a manager who delegates a task but does not delegate the corresponding authority to make necessary decisions often creates confusion and delay rather than efficiency.

### 5. Centralisation vs Decentralisation
**Centralisation** occurs when the authority to make key decisions is concentrated among senior managers or a head office, with individual branches, departments, or subordinates having little independent decision-making power. Advantages include consistency of decisions across the whole organisation (e.g. uniform pricing or branding), stronger central control, and the ability to make decisions based on a full overview of the whole business. Disadvantages include slower response to local conditions, decisions being made by people who may be distant from and less familiar with local circumstances, and reduced motivation for local managers and staff who have little autonomy.

**Decentralisation** occurs when decision-making authority is passed down to lower levels of the organisation — for example, to regional managers or individual branch/store managers. Advantages include faster decisions tailored to local circumstances, and — by giving lower-level staff genuine authority — increased motivation through empowerment (Chapter 22). Disadvantages include the risk of inconsistency between different parts of the business, potential loss of central control and quality standards, and the need for lower-level managers to be sufficiently trained and trusted to make good decisions.

**Business implication:** Centralisation and decentralisation exist on a spectrum rather than as a strict either/or choice, and most real businesses combine elements of both — for example, a retail chain may centralise decisions on branding, pricing, and supplier contracts (to maintain consistency) while decentralising decisions on local staffing rotas or in-store promotions (to respond to local conditions), directly reflecting the trade-off between control/consistency and speed/local responsiveness.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Amazon.com, Inc., e-commerce and technology, United States.
**Situation:** Amazon has publicly described organising much of its technology and product development work around small, autonomous "two-pizza teams" — teams kept deliberately small enough to be fed with two pizzas — each given significant decision-making authority over their own area of work, reflecting a deliberately flat and decentralised approach to structure in parts of the business.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates a flat, decentralised structure intended to speed up decision-making and increase employee autonomy and motivation (linking to empowerment, Chapter 22), even within a very large multinational corporation.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss whether a small-team, decentralised structure like Amazon's could be applied to every department of a very large business, such as its finance or legal functions."

**Company/context:** McDonald's Corporation, fast food, global (franchise model).
**Situation:** McDonald's operates with a highly standardised central model — menus, branding, food preparation processes, and quality standards are largely set centrally by McDonald's Corporation and applied consistently across its restaurants worldwide, including franchised outlets, while individual restaurant managers retain decentralised authority over day-to-day staffing and local operational scheduling.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates a business combining centralisation (of brand, menu, and quality standards, ensuring consistency across thousands of outlets) with decentralisation (of daily local operational decisions), showing that centralisation and decentralisation are not mutually exclusive.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the extent to which a global franchise business such as McDonald's should centralise more, or decentralise more, of its decision-making."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically has a naturally flat structure by necessity — often just the owner and a small number of staff — with a wide span of control, a short chain of command, and decisions usually centralised in the hands of the owner, since there are too few staff to justify additional layers of management. As a business grows into a large organisation, it typically develops a taller structure with more levels of hierarchy and narrower spans of control at each level, to maintain adequate supervision across a much larger workforce, and must make an explicit strategic choice about how far to centralise or decentralise decision-making across its (often geographically dispersed) branches or divisions — a choice a small, single-site business rarely has to make.
