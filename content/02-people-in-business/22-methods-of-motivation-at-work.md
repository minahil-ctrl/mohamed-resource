## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Wage | Payment to an employee calculated by the hour or by output, usually paid weekly | A part-time retail assistant paid $12 per hour worked |
| Salary | A fixed annual payment to an employee, usually divided into equal monthly payments, regardless of hours worked in a given week | A marketing manager paid $48,000 per year, paid $4,000 monthly |
| Time rate | A method of paying wages based on the number of hours worked | A cleaner paid for each hour clocked in |
| Piece rate | A method of paying wages based on the number of units produced | A textile worker paid per garment sewn |

| Performance-related pay (PRP) | A system in which part of an employee's pay is linked directly to their individual performance against set targets or appraisal outcomes | A teacher receiving a pay increase linked to appraisal ratings |
| Fringe benefits (perks) | Non-cash benefits given to employees in addition to their pay | Company car, private health insurance, subsidised meals, gym membership |
| Job enrichment | Giving employees more challenging, varied, and responsible tasks to increase satisfaction | An administrator given authority to resolve customer complaints directly |
| Job rotation | Moving employees between different tasks or jobs periodically to reduce monotony | A factory worker rotating between three different assembly stations each week |
| Job enlargement | Increasing the number and variety of tasks in a job at the same level of responsibility | A cashier also trained to restock shelves and manage returns |
| Empowerment | Giving employees greater authority and responsibility to make their own decisions about how they carry out their work | A team given control over its own work schedule and quality checks |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Knowing *that* people are motivated by more than money (Chapter 21) is only half the story — a business manager also needs practical tools to actually motivate staff day to day. Companies such as Toyota have built entire production philosophies around involving workers directly in improving their own jobs through quality circles and teamworking, while others, such as many UK retailers, rely heavily on commission and bonus schemes to drive sales performance. Choosing the right mix of financial and non-financial methods — matched to the job, the workforce, and the theory behind it — is one of the most practical decisions any manager makes.

## Core Content

### 1. Financial Methods of Motivation
Financial methods motivate employees primarily through pay and money-related rewards. They connect most directly to Taylor's view (Chapter 21) that employees are principally motivated by financial reward, and to Herzberg's classification of pay as a **hygiene factor** — necessary to prevent dissatisfaction, but limited in its power to create lasting satisfaction on its own.

**Wages** are payments calculated either by **time rate** (a fixed amount per hour worked) or **piece rate** (a fixed amount per unit produced), and are usually paid weekly. Time rate is simple to calculate and guarantees income regardless of output, but gives limited direct incentive to work faster; piece rate directly rewards higher output (consistent with Taylor's scientific management) but can encourage staff to sacrifice quality for speed, and provides no income if production is halted for reasons beyond the worker's control (e.g. a machine breakdown).

*Worked example (piece rate):* A worker is paid $2.50 per unit produced. In a week they produce 340 units.
Pay = rate per unit × units produced = $2.50 × 340 = **$850**.

**Salaries** are a fixed annual sum, usually divided into equal monthly payments, and are typically paid to office, managerial, and professional staff regardless of the exact hours worked in a given week. Salaries provide financial security and are simple to budget for both employee and employer, but do not directly reward extra effort or output in the way piece rate does.

**Commission** is a payment calculated as a percentage of the value of sales an employee generates, common in retail and sales roles. It gives a strong direct financial incentive to sell more, aligning employee effort with business revenue, but can create pressure to prioritise sales volume over customer needs, and gives employees an unstable income if sales are seasonal or slow.

*Worked example (commission):* A salesperson earns 4% commission on sales. In a month they sell $60,000 worth of goods.
Commission = percentage rate × value of sales = 0.04 × $60,000 = **$2,400**.

**Bonuses** are one-off extra payments, usually awarded for reaching or exceeding a specific target (e.g. an annual sales target or a project deadline). Bonuses can be a flexible, low-commitment way to reward good performance without permanently raising the wage bill, but if targets are seen as unfair or unachievable, or if a bonus is expected every year, it can lose its motivational power and instead be treated as an entitlement.

**Profit-sharing** distributes a share of the business's overall profit among employees, in addition to normal pay. It is intended to align employees' interests with the success of the whole business, encouraging cooperation and a sense of shared ownership, but the link between any one individual's effort and the resulting bonus is often weak (an individual's hard work has only a tiny effect on total company profit), which can dilute its motivational impact — a criticism frequently raised in evaluative answers.

**Performance-related pay (PRP)** links part of an employee's pay to their individual performance, typically assessed through appraisals or against specific targets. PRP directly rewards individual achievement, which can strongly motivate high performers, but requires a fair, transparent, and accurately measurable appraisal system — if targets are perceived as subjective or unfairly applied, PRP can cause resentment and demotivation rather than improving performance.

**Fringe benefits (perks)** are non-cash rewards such as company cars, private health insurance, subsidised canteens, discounted products, or gym membership. They can help attract and retain staff, particularly in competitive labour markets, and may address needs beyond pure income (e.g. health insurance addressing safety needs in Maslow's hierarchy), but are a cost to the business regardless of individual performance and may be valued very differently by different employees.

**Exam pitfall:** Students often list financial methods without stating *how each is calculated* or without linking back to Herzberg/Taylor. A strong answer always ties the financial method chosen to the theoretical justification (e.g. "this reflects Taylor's belief that money is the primary motivator") and notes Herzberg's caveat that money alone rarely sustains long-term motivation.

### 2. Non-Financial Methods of Motivation
Non-financial methods motivate through the design and organisation of the job itself, rather than pay. They connect directly to Herzberg's **motivators** and to Maslow's higher-level needs (belonging, esteem, self-actualisation).

**Job enrichment** involves giving an employee more challenging, varied, and responsible tasks — for example, allowing a customer service assistant to resolve complaints independently rather than referring every case to a supervisor. This directly targets Herzberg's motivators (responsibility, achievement, recognition) and Maslow's esteem and self-actualisation needs, but requires employees to be trained and willing to take on the additional responsibility, and is not equally suitable for every role or every employee.

**Job rotation** involves periodically moving employees between different tasks or workstations. This reduces the boredom associated with highly repetitive work (a criticism of Taylorist job design), develops a more flexible, multi-skilled workforce, and can improve engagement, but does not increase the level of responsibility or challenge in any individual task — it is a rotation of similar-level tasks rather than a genuine enrichment of the job.

**Job enlargement** involves adding more tasks of a similar level of responsibility to a job — for example, a cashier who is also trained to restock shelves and process returns. This can reduce monotony and add variety, but, like job rotation, does not increase the *level* of responsibility, and some workers may perceive it as simply "more work for the same pay" rather than as motivating in itself — an important evaluative point.

**Empowerment** gives employees genuine authority and responsibility to make decisions about how they carry out their own work, rather than simply following instructions from managers. This strongly targets self-actualisation and esteem needs, and can improve motivation, decision-making speed, and employee ownership of outcomes, but requires trust from management, adequate training, and carries the risk of inconsistent decisions if employees are not well prepared.

**Teamworking** organises employees into groups that share responsibility for a set of tasks or outcomes, often with some autonomy over how the work is organised (as pioneered in Japanese and, later, Western car manufacturing, replacing single-task assembly lines with team-based cell production). This targets Maslow's social/belonging needs and can improve cooperation, problem-solving, and flexibility, but can also create conflict within teams or allow free-riding by less motivated members if not well managed.

**Quality circles** are small groups of employees, often from the same work area, who meet regularly (e.g. monthly) to identify problems and suggest improvements, particularly relating to product or process quality. Quality circles give employees a genuine voice in improving their own work environment (targeting esteem and self-actualisation needs) and can generate practical, ground-level improvements that management might not otherwise identify, but they take employee time away from normal duties and are only effective if management genuinely listens to and acts on the group's suggestions — a quality circle that is ignored quickly becomes a source of frustration rather than motivation.

**Exam pitfall:** Students very commonly confuse job enrichment with job enlargement. The critical distinction is that enrichment increases the *level* of responsibility and challenge (a "vertical" change), while enlargement and rotation simply add more tasks or variety at the *same* level (a "horizontal" change). Examiners specifically reward answers that state this distinction explicitly.

### 3. Choosing an Appropriate Mix of Methods
No single method suits every business or every employee. A business employing largely low-skilled workers doing highly repetitive tasks (closer to Taylor's model) may find financial incentives such as piece rate or bonus schemes most effective, since the scope for enrichment may be limited. A business employing skilled, creative, or professional staff is more likely to benefit from non-financial methods such as empowerment and job enrichment, since these employees' lower-level needs (pay, security) are typically already well met, and Maslow's model predicts they will be motivated instead by esteem and self-actualisation. In practice, most businesses use a *combination* of financial and non-financial methods, since financial methods (as hygiene factors) prevent dissatisfaction while non-financial methods (as genuine motivators) build lasting engagement — directly reflecting the logic of Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory from Chapter 21.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Toyota Motor Corporation, automotive manufacturing, Japan.
**Situation:** Toyota is widely credited with pioneering the use of quality circles and teamworking within its production system, organising workers into small teams with responsibility for their own section of the production process and encouraging regular group meetings to identify and solve quality problems on the production line.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates non-financial methods (quality circles, teamworking) being used to target esteem and belonging needs and to generate genuine employee-driven improvements in a large manufacturing business.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss whether quality circles would be equally effective in a small business as in a large manufacturer such as Toyota."

**Company/context:** John Lewis Partnership, retail, United Kingdom.
**Situation:** The John Lewis Partnership is structured so that its employees (known as "Partners") receive an annual profit-share bonus, calculated as a percentage of salary and dependent on the company's overall annual profit, in addition to having a voice in the business through elected representative bodies.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates profit-sharing as a financial method intended to align staff interests with overall company performance, combined with elements of empowerment through partner representation.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the extent to which profit-sharing schemes like John Lewis's genuinely motivate individual employees, given that an individual's own effort has only a small effect on total company profit."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often has limited funds for extensive fringe benefits or profit-sharing schemes, but can more easily use low-cost non-financial methods such as empowerment and job enrichment, since the owner works closely with staff and can delegate real responsibility informally and quickly. A large business can typically afford wider financial packages — structured bonus schemes, profit-sharing, and extensive fringe benefits — but implementing non-financial methods such as teamworking or quality circles often requires more formal restructuring of how work is organised across a much larger and more hierarchical workforce (see Chapter 23, Organisation Structure and Employees).
