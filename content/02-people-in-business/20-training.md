## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Training | The process of developing the skills, knowledge and attitudes of employees to help them perform their job effectively | A new cashier learning how to use the till system |
| Induction training | Training given to a new employee to introduce them to the business, its policies, and their role | A new employee's first-day tour and health and safety briefing |
| On-the-job training | Training that takes place at the employee's normal place of work, while carrying out the job | An experienced worker showing a trainee how to operate machinery |
| Off-the-job training | Training that takes place away from the employee's normal place of work | An employee attending an external course or college |
| Mentoring | A form of on-the-job training where a more experienced employee guides and supports a less experienced one | A senior manager mentoring a graduate trainee |
| Shadowing | An on-the-job training method where a trainee observes an experienced employee performing their role | A new sales assistant following an experienced colleague for a day |
| Apprenticeship | A structured programme combining paid on-the-job work experience with off-the-job study, usually leading to a qualification | An 18-year-old completing an engineering apprenticeship |
| Skills gap | The difference between the skills a business needs and the skills its current workforce actually has | A business needing digital marketing skills its staff do not have |
| Appraisal | A formal review of an employee's performance, often used to identify training needs | A manager conducting an annual performance review |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
As technology, customer expectations, and working methods change ever faster, the skills a business needed five years ago are often not the skills it needs today — a challenge sometimes described as "reskilling." Global companies such as Amazon have committed enormous sums (Amazon's "Upskilling 2025" pledge committed over one billion dollars to retrain hundreds of thousands of employees for higher-skilled roles) to training their workforces, recognising that a business's ability to compete depends directly on the capability of its people.

## Core Content

### 1. What Is Training and Why Do Businesses Provide It?
**Training** is the deliberate process of improving employees' skills, knowledge, and attitudes so they can do their current job more effectively, prepare for future roles, or adapt to changes in technology, processes, or products. Businesses invest in training to:
- Improve productivity and the quality of work.
- Reduce accidents and errors, particularly in relation to health and safety (see Chapter 19).
- Improve staff motivation and morale by investing in employees' development (links to Chapter 21).
- Reduce staff turnover, as well-trained employees who see career development opportunities are more likely to stay.
- Enable the business to adapt to new technology, processes, or legal requirements.
- Close a **skills gap** between what the business needs and what current staff can do.

### 2. Induction Training
**Induction training** is given to new employees to familiarise them with the business — its layout, health and safety procedures, culture, policies, and their specific role and colleagues. It typically happens in the first days or weeks of employment.
- *Advantages:* helps new employees settle in quickly and become productive sooner; reduces early mistakes and accidents; helps new starters feel welcomed, supporting motivation and retention.
- *Disadvantages:* takes time away from productive work for both the new employee and those delivering the induction; poorly designed induction can overwhelm new starters with too much information at once (links to information overload, Chapter 17).

### 3. On-the-Job Training
**On-the-job training** takes place at the normal place of work while the employee continues (or begins) to carry out their duties. Methods include shadowing, mentoring, coaching by a supervisor, and direct instruction while performing tasks.
- *Advantages:* relatively cheap, as no external training provider or travel is needed; directly relevant to the specific job and business, since it uses the business's own equipment, processes and standards; the trainee can be at least partly productive while training; does not require the employee to be away from the workplace.
- *Disadvantages:* quality of training depends heavily on the skill and patience of whoever is training (an unskilled trainer risks passing on bad habits); can disrupt the productivity of the person delivering the training, and possibly the trainee's own output while learning; limited exposure to new ideas or methods used outside the business.

### 4. Off-the-Job Training
**Off-the-job training** takes place away from the normal workplace — e.g. at a college, training centre, university, or external course/conference — and may be delivered online or in person.
- *Advantages:* delivered by specialist trainers, often leading to a recognised, transferable qualification; exposes employees to new ideas, methods and best practice from outside the business; not disrupted by the day-to-day pressures of the workplace, allowing focused learning.
- *Disadvantages:* more expensive (course fees, travel, accommodation); the employee is unproductive at work while training (an "opportunity cost" of lost output); the trained employee may leave the business afterwards to work for a competitor, meaning the training investment benefits a rival business — sometimes called "poaching."

### 5. Apprenticeships
An **apprenticeship** combines both approaches: paid, structured on-the-job experience alongside formal off-the-job study, usually resulting in a recognised qualification. Apprenticeships are widely used in industries such as engineering, construction and skilled trades, and are seen by many governments as a key tool for developing a skilled workforce while allowing young people to earn while they learn.

### 6. Identifying Training Needs
Businesses identify training needs through methods including performance **appraisals**, changes in technology or legislation, feedback from customers or managers, and analysis of a **skills gap** between current staff capability and future business requirements. Effective training should be targeted at a genuine, identified need rather than provided generically, to ensure it represents good value for the cost involved.

### 7. Costs and Benefits of Training
Training is never free: direct costs include trainer/course fees, materials, and travel; indirect costs include lost productivity while employees are being trained (or are training others) and the risk that a trained employee leaves for a competitor. However, the benefits — higher productivity, fewer errors and accidents, improved staff morale and retention, and the ability to adapt to change — usually outweigh these costs over time, particularly where training is well-targeted and where a business takes steps (e.g. career progression opportunities, competitive pay) to retain trained staff.

**Exam pitfall:** Students often assume "more training is always better." In fact, a good evaluative answer weighs the cost of training (in money and lost output) against the specific benefit for that business and scenario, and recognises that the *type* of training must suit the situation — e.g. a highly standardised, low-skill task may need only brief on-the-job training, while a specialist, safety-critical or technical role may justify substantial off-the-job investment.

2. **Type:** Comparison table.
   **Purpose:** To compare on-the-job and off-the-job training directly.
   **Content spec:** Columns: On-the-Job / Off-the-Job. Rows: Cost, Disruption to normal work, Relevance to specific job, Quality/consistency, Risk of losing trained staff to competitors, Qualification gained (yes/no, typically).

3. **Type:** Process/cycle diagram.
   **Purpose:** To show how businesses identify and act on training needs.
   **Content spec:** Circular flow: "Identify skills gap (via appraisal, new technology, customer feedback) → Design training programme → Deliver training (on/off-the-job) → Evaluate effectiveness (performance improvement?) → back to Identify skills gap."

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Amazon, e-commerce and logistics, United States (global operations).
**Situation:** Amazon launched its "Upskilling 2025" pledge, committing more than one billion dollars to provide free training programmes to hundreds of thousands of its employees, aiming to help warehouse and other operational staff move into higher-skilled technical roles such as IT support and software development, in response to changing skill needs driven by automation and technology.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates large-scale, strategic investment in off-the-job (and blended) training by a multinational business to close a skills gap and support internal career progression.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the benefits and risks to a large business of investing heavily in training employees for higher-skilled roles."

**Company/context:** Safaricom, telecommunications, Kenya.
**Situation:** Safaricom, the largest telecommunications provider in Kenya (known for the M-Pesa mobile money service), has invested in structured graduate trainee and apprenticeship-style programmes to build technical and managerial talent locally, combining on-the-job placements across departments with structured off-the-job development, reflecting the importance of building local skilled talent pipelines in a fast-growing emerging-market technology business.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a large business in an emerging market uses a blended on-the-job and off-the-job training approach (similar in structure to an apprenticeship/graduate scheme) to build a skilled local workforce.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse the advantages to Safaricom of training staff internally rather than only recruiting already-skilled staff externally."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically relies heavily on informal on-the-job training and induction, delivered directly by the owner or an experienced employee, because it cannot afford the cost or lost productivity of sending staff off-the-job for extended periods. A large multinational business can afford to invest in structured, large-scale training programmes — including formal induction schemes, dedicated training centres, e-learning platforms, and apprenticeship or graduate schemes — as the cost can be spread across a much larger workforce and the long-term benefits (e.g. lower staff turnover, a pipeline of skilled future managers) justify the greater investment.
