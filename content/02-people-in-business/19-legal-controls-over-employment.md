## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Contract of employment | A legally binding agreement between employer and employee setting out the terms and conditions of the job | A document stating salary, hours, holiday entitlement and notice period |
| Discrimination | Treating a person unfairly because of a personal characteristic such as gender, age, race, religion or disability | Refusing to hire a qualified candidate because of their age |
| Equal opportunities | The principle that all employees and applicants should be treated fairly regardless of personal characteristics | A business using a standardised interview process for all candidates |
| National minimum wage | The legally required lowest hourly rate of pay an employer must pay an employee | An employer paying at least the legal minimum rate to a part-time worker |
| Health and safety legislation | Laws requiring employers to protect the health, safety and welfare of employees at work | Providing protective equipment to factory workers |
| Unfair dismissal | Dismissing an employee without a fair, legally valid reason or without following the correct procedure | Sacking an employee for complaining about pay without following proper process |
| Wrongful dismissal | Dismissing an employee in breach of the terms of their contract | Dismissing an employee without giving the contractual notice period |
| Redundancy | Dismissal of an employee because the job itself is no longer needed, not because of the employee's performance | Closing a factory and making all production staff redundant |
| Redundancy payment | Compensation legally owed to an employee dismissed due to redundancy, usually based on length of service | A worker with 5 years' service receiving a lump sum payment |
| Employment tribunal | A legal body that hears disputes between employers and employees, e.g. claims of unfair dismissal or discrimination | An employee taking a former employer to a tribunal for unfair dismissal |
| Trade union | An organisation of workers formed to protect and negotiate on behalf of employees' interests | Workers joining a union to negotiate better pay |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Employment law shapes almost every decision a business makes about its workforce — who it can hire, how much it must pay, how it can dismiss someone, and how it must keep them safe. High-profile legal cases, such as gig-economy workers at Uber successfully arguing in UK courts that they should be classified as "workers" entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay rather than self-employed contractors, show how employment law is a live, evolving battleground with major financial and reputational consequences for business.

## Core Content

### 1. Why Employment Law Exists
Governments introduce employment legislation to protect employees from exploitation and unfair treatment, while also providing a clear legal framework within which employers can operate. Employment law seeks to balance the interests of employees (fair pay, safe working conditions, protection from discrimination and unfair treatment) with the interests of employers (the ability to manage their workforce, dismiss underperforming staff fairly, and restructure the business when necessary). Note: as this is an international specification, exact legislation and minimum wage levels vary by country — students should understand the *principles* and *purpose* of each type of law, illustrated with real examples, rather than memorising one country's specific statutes as universal.

### 2. Contracts of Employment
A **contract of employment** is a legally binding agreement setting out the rights and obligations of both employer and employee, typically including: job title and duties, pay and how it is calculated, hours of work, holiday entitlement, notice period required to terminate employment, and grounds for dismissal. Both employer and employee are legally bound by its terms; breaching the contract (e.g. dismissing an employee without the agreed notice period) can lead to a claim of **wrongful dismissal**.

### 3. Discrimination and Equal Opportunities
Discrimination laws make it illegal for employers to treat job applicants or employees less favourably because of protected characteristics such as gender, age, race, ethnicity, religion, disability, or marital status. This applies throughout the employment relationship — in recruitment and selection (see Chapter 18), pay, promotion, training opportunities, and dismissal. Businesses are expected to operate **equal opportunities policies**, ensuring recruitment and management decisions are based on merit and job-relevant criteria rather than personal characteristics. Failure to comply can result in expensive legal claims, reputational damage, and loss of talent and customer trust.

### 4. National Minimum Wage
Most countries set a **national minimum wage** — the lowest hourly rate an employer is legally permitted to pay. This protects low-paid workers from exploitation, but businesses (particularly small businesses and those in labour-intensive industries such as retail and hospitality) must factor minimum wage costs into their pricing and staffing decisions. An increase in the minimum wage raises a business's labour costs, which may lead to price rises, reduced staffing levels, increased automation, or reduced profit margins, depending on the business's ability to absorb or pass on the cost.

### 5. Health and Safety Legislation
Health and safety law requires employers to take reasonable steps to protect the health, safety, and welfare of their employees (and often customers and visitors) while at work. This includes providing appropriate training, protective equipment, safe machinery and premises, and clear procedures for reporting hazards and accidents. Non-compliance can lead to injuries, legal penalties, compensation claims, and severe reputational damage — and in the most serious cases, loss of life, as seen in major industrial disasters. Health and safety compliance is a cost to business, but neglecting it can be far more costly in the long run.

### 6. Dismissal, Wrongful Dismissal, and Redundancy
It is essential that students can clearly distinguish between these three related but distinct concepts, as this is a frequent area of confusion:

- **Fair dismissal:** an employer ends an employee's contract for a legally valid reason (e.g. serious misconduct, persistent poor performance after warnings) and follows the correct legal procedure.
- **Unfair dismissal:** an employee is dismissed without a fair reason, or without the employer following the correct legal procedure (e.g. no warnings given, no chance to respond to allegations). An employee who believes they have been unfairly dismissed may bring a claim to an **employment tribunal**.
- **Wrongful dismissal:** dismissal that breaches the specific terms of the employment contract, most commonly by not giving the contractually required notice period. This is a breach of contract, distinct from unfair dismissal, which is about the fairness of the reason/process rather than the contract terms.
- **Redundancy:** dismissal that occurs not because of anything the employee did, but because the job itself is no longer required — e.g. due to automation, a factory closure, or falling demand. Employees made redundant are usually legally entitled to a **redundancy payment**, calculated according to length of service and pay.

**Exam pitfall:** Students very commonly write "redundancy" and "unfair dismissal" as if they were interchangeable. They are not: redundancy is a *legitimate* reason for dismissal related to the job, not the person, and is not inherently unfair — though a redundancy process itself can become unfair dismissal if it is not carried out following a fair, transparent procedure (e.g. failing to consult properly or using redundancy as a pretext to remove a specific individual).

### 7. Impact of Employment Law on Business
Employment legislation affects business decision-making in several ways:
- Increases **costs** of employing staff (compliance costs, minimum wage, health and safety measures, potential compensation payments).
- Requires investment in **HR expertise and administration** to ensure compliance.
- Can make it harder and slower to dismiss underperforming staff, requiring documented warnings and fair procedures.
- Protects business reputation when complied with, but risks severe reputational and financial damage when breached (legal costs, tribunal awards, negative publicity).
- Can influence workforce planning decisions, e.g. increased use of automation or reduced hiring in response to rising minimum wage costs.

## Diagrams Required
1. **Type:** Comparison/decision diagram.
   **Purpose:** To help students distinguish clearly between fair dismissal, unfair dismissal, wrongful dismissal, and redundancy.
   **Content spec:** A branching flowchart starting at "Employee's contract ends." First branch: "Is it because the job no longer exists?" → Yes → "Redundancy" (note: entitled to redundancy pay). No → "Was there a fair reason and fair process?" → Yes → "Fair dismissal." No → "Unfair dismissal" (can claim at employment tribunal). Separate box: "Was the contract's notice/terms breached?" → Yes → "Wrongful dismissal" (breach of contract, regardless of fairness of reason).

2. **Type:** Cost-impact table.
   **Purpose:** To show how each area of employment law creates a cost or obligation for business.
   **Content spec:** Rows: Minimum Wage Law, Discrimination Law, Health & Safety Law, Unfair Dismissal Law. Columns: What it requires of employers / Cost or resource implication / Risk if non-compliant.

3. **Type:** Timeline/process diagram.
   **Purpose:** To show the stages of a fair dismissal process.
   **Content spec:** Sequential boxes: "Issue identified → Verbal warning → Written warning → Final written warning → Dismissal (with correct notice)," with a side note "Employee has right to respond/appeal at each stage" running alongside the whole chain.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Uber, ride-hailing/technology, United Kingdom (global operations).
**Situation:** In a landmark 2021 UK Supreme Court ruling, Uber drivers were found to be "workers" rather than fully self-employed contractors, entitling them to rights including the national minimum wage, paid holiday, and rest breaks. This significantly increased Uber's labour costs and legal obligations in the UK and prompted debate about employment classification across the wider "gig economy" globally.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how employment law (minimum wage and worker classification) directly affects a large business's costs and operating model, and how legal definitions of "employee" can be contested and change over time.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the impact on a business like Uber of being required to treat its drivers as workers rather than self-employed contractors."

**Company/context:** Rana Plaza garment factory complex, textile manufacturing, Bangladesh.
**Situation:** The 2013 collapse of the Rana Plaza building, which housed garment factories supplying major international clothing brands, killed over a thousand workers and was linked to serious health and safety failures, including ignored structural warnings. The disaster led to significant reforms in factory safety standards in Bangladesh (including the Bangladesh Accord on Fire and Building Safety) and intense scrutiny of health and safety and supply-chain responsibility among the multinational retailers sourcing from the factories.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the catastrophic human and reputational consequences of failing to comply with health and safety legislation and standards, and the resulting pressure on both local employers and their multinational customers to enforce stronger legal and safety controls.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the responsibility of large multinational retailers for health and safety conditions in the factories of their overseas suppliers."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often has limited in-house HR expertise and may find the administrative and financial burden of complying with employment law (contracts, minimum wage, health and safety, fair dismissal procedures) proportionally heavier, sometimes needing to pay for external legal or HR advice. A large multinational business typically has dedicated HR and legal departments to manage compliance, but faces the added complexity of operating under different employment laws in each country it operates in, and the reputational stakes of a legal breach (e.g. a discrimination or safety scandal) are far higher given its public profile and scale of operations.
