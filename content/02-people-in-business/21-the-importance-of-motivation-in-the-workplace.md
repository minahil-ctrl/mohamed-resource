## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
A demotivated workforce is one of the most expensive problems a business can have — not because of any single dramatic event, but through a steady drip of lower productivity, higher absenteeism, poor customer service, and staff quitting. Google has become globally famous for the lengths it goes to in order to keep employees motivated (from flexible working and generous perks to giving engineers time for personal projects), precisely because technology companies compete fiercely for skilled staff and know that motivated employees are more innovative, productive, and loyal. Understanding what genuinely motivates people — rather than assuming money is the only answer — is one of the most practically important topics in the whole course.

## Core Content

### 1. What Is Motivation and Why Does It Matter?
**Motivation** is the internal drive that makes an employee want to work hard, engage fully with their job, and contribute to business goals. A motivated workforce tends to be more productive, produce higher-quality work, show greater creativity and initiative, and have lower **labour turnover** and **absenteeism**. A demotivated workforce, conversely, tends to work at a lower pace, make more errors, show less commitment to customer service, and leave the business more readily — all of which increase costs (recruitment, training, lost productivity) and can damage a business's reputation and competitiveness.

### 2. Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs
Psychologist Abraham Maslow proposed that human needs can be arranged into a **hierarchy of five levels**, and that a person is primarily motivated by the *lowest* level of unmet need — once a level is satisfied, it stops being a strong motivator, and the person becomes motivated by the next level up. The five levels, from the base of the pyramid to the top, are:

1. **Physiological needs** — the most basic survival needs: food, water, warmth, rest. In a business context, this is met through a wage sufficient to afford basic living costs.
2. **Safety needs** — the need for security and protection from harm, physical and economic. In business, this includes job security (e.g. a permanent contract rather than casual work), a safe working environment (see Chapter 19, health and safety law), and stable income.
3. **Social/belonging needs** — the need for friendship, acceptance, and a sense of belonging to a group. In business, this includes good relationships with colleagues, teamwork, and an inclusive workplace culture.
4. **Esteem needs** — the need for self-respect, recognition, and respect from others. In business, this includes praise, promotion, status (e.g. job title), and public recognition of achievement.
5. **Self-actualisation** — the need to realise one's full potential, achieve personal growth and take on meaningful challenges. In business, this includes being given autonomy, creative or challenging work, and opportunities for personal development.

**Business implication:** Maslow's theory suggests that once a business has met an employee's lower-level needs (e.g. through adequate pay and job security), further pay rises alone will have diminishing motivational effect — the business must then address social, esteem, and self-actualisation needs (teamwork, recognition, development opportunities, autonomy) to keep motivating that employee.

**Exam pitfall:** Students frequently list the five levels without explaining *why the order matters* — the key theoretical point is that a lower, unmet need dominates behaviour and must be addressed before a higher need can motivate someone. A common misconception is treating Maslow as simply "a list of things people want" rather than an ordered, sequential model.

### 3. Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory
Frederick Herzberg's research led him to conclude that the factors which cause job **dissatisfaction** are different from the factors which create job **satisfaction** — they are not simply opposite ends of the same scale. He divided workplace factors into two categories:

- **Hygiene factors** (also called "maintenance factors"): these include pay, company policy, working conditions, job security, and relationships with supervisors. If these are inadequate, employees become dissatisfied. However, Herzberg argued that improving hygiene factors beyond an adequate level does *not* create genuine motivation or satisfaction — it merely prevents dissatisfaction. For example, raising pay may stop an employee from being unhappy, but will not by itself make them love their job or work harder in the long run.
- **Motivators**: these include achievement, recognition, responsibility, the nature of the work itself, and opportunities for advancement. Herzberg argued these are the factors that create genuine, lasting job satisfaction and drive employees to perform at a higher level.

**Business implication:** Herzberg's theory suggests that a business must first ensure hygiene factors are adequate (to prevent dissatisfaction) but must then focus on motivators — e.g. through **job enrichment** (giving employees more challenging, varied and responsible tasks) — to genuinely motivate staff, rather than assuming pay rises alone will improve performance.

**Exam pitfall:** Students often incorrectly assume pay is always a "motivator." In Herzberg's model, pay is a **hygiene factor** — its absence/inadequacy causes dissatisfaction, but its presence does not create lasting motivation. This is one of the most heavily examined distinctions in this topic and is frequently misapplied.

### 4. Taylor's Theory of Scientific Management
F.W. Taylor, writing in the early twentieth century, took a very different view. Taylor believed that workers were motivated **primarily, if not exclusively, by money**, and that the way to maximise output was to apply "scientific" methods to work: break jobs down into simple, standardised, repetitive tasks; select and train workers specifically for those tasks; closely supervise and measure their performance; and pay them according to output, typically through **piece rate** payment (paying per unit produced) — providing a direct financial incentive to work faster.

Taylor's approach was highly influential in the early industrial era (e.g. in mass-production factories) and did increase productivity in many settings, but it has been heavily criticised for treating workers as purely economically motivated, ignoring social and psychological needs (in direct contrast to Maslow and Herzberg), and for the risk that highly repetitive, closely supervised work leads to boredom, alienation, and poor quality if not carefully managed.

**Comparing the theories:** Maslow and Herzberg both suggest that non-financial factors (belonging, recognition, achievement, meaningful work) are essential for genuine, lasting motivation, particularly once basic needs/hygiene factors are met. Taylor's model, by contrast, assumes money is the dominant motivator and that management's role is to design efficient systems and provide financial incentive, with less regard for psychological needs. A strong evaluative answer recognises that different theories may suit different contexts — e.g. Taylor's approach may still have relevance for simple, highly repetitive tasks where financial incentive is a powerful driver, while Maslow's and Herzberg's insights are more relevant for knowledge-based, skilled, or creative roles where intrinsic motivation matters more.

### 5. Consequences of Low Motivation
Low motivation can lead to: higher labour turnover (increasing recruitment and training costs, see Chapters 18 and 20), higher absenteeism, lower productivity and output per worker, poorer quality of work and customer service, increased conflict and grievances, and, in the most severe cases, industrial action such as strikes. These consequences directly affect a business's costs, revenue, and reputation, which is why motivation theory has such direct, practical relevance to business decision-making (links forward to Chapter 22, Methods of Motivation at Work).

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Google (Alphabet Inc.), technology, United States.
**Situation:** Google is widely known for its extensive employee benefits and workplace culture initiatives — including flexible working arrangements, on-site facilities, professional development opportunities, and its historical "20% time" policy allowing engineers to spend a portion of their time on self-directed projects (credited with contributing to products such as Gmail) — reflecting an approach aimed at addressing higher-level needs such as esteem, belonging, and self-actualisation, not just pay.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates a large technology business applying insights consistent with Maslow's higher-level needs and Herzberg's motivators (autonomy, achievement, meaningful work) rather than relying purely on financial incentives.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate whether Google's approach to motivation would work as effectively in a business with mostly low-skilled, repetitive jobs."

**Company/context:** Ford Motor Company, automotive manufacturing, United States (historical).
**Situation:** In the early twentieth century, Henry Ford applied mass-production methods heavily influenced by Taylorist thinking — breaking car assembly into simple, repetitive tasks on a moving production line — and famously introduced the "Five Dollar Day" in 1914, roughly doubling many workers' wages, which dramatically cut labour turnover and absenteeism on the assembly line at the time.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates Taylor's assumption that financial incentive is a powerful motivator, particularly for repetitive, low-autonomy manufacturing work, while also showing the limitations of highly repetitive work for long-term engagement.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the extent to which Taylor's scientific management theory is still relevant to businesses operating highly automated or repetitive processes today."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often relies on close personal relationships and informal recognition (e.g. the owner personally thanking staff, offering flexible hours) to meet employees' social and esteem needs, and may find it harder to offer the same career progression or self-actualisation opportunities (e.g. large-scale training, promotion ladders) that a bigger organisation can. A large multinational business can invest in structured motivation programmes — formal recognition schemes, career development pathways, staff benefits and well-being programmes — addressing higher-level needs (Maslow) and motivators (Herzberg) at scale, but risks employees feeling like "just a number" within a large, impersonal hierarchy unless deliberate effort is made to build belonging and recognition into the culture.
