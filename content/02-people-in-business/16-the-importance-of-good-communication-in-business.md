## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | The process of passing information from one person or group to another so that it is understood | A manager emailing staff about a new rota |
| Sender | The person or organisation who originates and sends the message | A team leader issuing an instruction |
| Receiver | The person or organisation who the message is intended for | An employee receiving a memo |
| Medium (channel) | The method used to transmit a message, e.g. face-to-face, phone, email, notice board | Video call used for a remote team meeting |
| Feedback | The response of the receiver, confirming the message has been received and understood | An employee replying "yes, understood" to an instruction |
| One-way communication | Communication that flows from sender to receiver with no feedback loop | A company-wide announcement email with no reply expected |
| Two-way communication | Communication where the receiver responds, allowing dialogue and clarification | A face-to-face meeting or a phone call |
| Formal communication | Official communication that follows the organisation's recognised structure and channels | A written contract of employment |
| Informal communication | Unofficial communication outside formal channels, often based on personal relationships | Colleagues chatting about a new policy at lunch |
| Verbal communication | Communication using spoken words | A briefing meeting |
| Written communication | Communication using text | A memo, report, or email |
| Visual communication | Communication using images, charts, or diagrams | A graph in a sales report |
| Electronic communication | Communication transmitted via digital technology | Email, video conferencing, instant messaging apps |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Every major business failure — a missed delivery, a product recall, a strike, a failed merger — can usually be traced back to a communication breakdown somewhere in the chain. Conversely, businesses like Toyota built entire competitive advantages (their famous "andon cord" system) on ensuring information flows instantly and accurately from the shop floor to management. In an age of remote work and global supply chains spanning continents and time zones, the ability to communicate clearly, quickly, and appropriately has become one of the most valuable — and most tested — skills in business.

## Core Content

### 1. The Communication Process
At its simplest, communication involves a **sender** encoding a **message** and transmitting it through a **medium** to a **receiver**, who decodes it and (ideally) provides **feedback** to confirm understanding. If any stage breaks down — the message is poorly worded, the wrong medium is chosen, or feedback is never given — the communication has failed, even if something was technically "said."

Effective communication requires the message to be:
- **Clear** — unambiguous and easy to understand
- **Accurate** — factually correct
- **Relevant** — appropriate to the audience and purpose
- **Timely** — delivered when it is still useful
- **Delivered via an appropriate medium** — matched to the urgency, formality, and complexity of the content

### 2. One-Way vs Two-Way Communication
**One-way communication** flows in a single direction — from sender to receiver — with no opportunity (or no requirement) for the receiver to respond. Examples include a company memo, a notice board announcement, or a pre-recorded training video. It is fast and efficient for spreading information to a large audience but carries the risk that the message is misunderstood, ignored, or not received at all, since there is no built-in check.

**Two-way communication** allows the receiver to respond, ask questions, and seek clarification, creating a dialogue. Examples include a face-to-face meeting, a phone call, or a video conference. Two-way communication is slower and more resource-intensive but is far more effective for complex, sensitive, or important messages because misunderstandings can be identified and corrected immediately.

**Exam tip:** Students frequently confuse "written" with "one-way" and "verbal" with "two-way." This is a misconception — a memo (written) is one-way, but a video call (verbal, and increasingly common) is two-way; equally, a letter can prompt a reply making an exchange two-way over time. The key distinguishing factor is whether feedback occurs, not the medium itself.

### 3. Methods of Communication
Businesses choose between several broad categories of method, often combining them:

- **Verbal communication:** spoken, e.g. team briefings, one-to-one meetings, telephone calls, video conferencing (Zoom, Microsoft Teams). Advantages: immediate feedback, personal tone, good for sensitive or complex matters. Disadvantages: no permanent record (unless recorded), can be forgotten or misquoted, difficult across time zones.
- **Written communication:** letters, memos, emails, reports, contracts, notices. Advantages: creates a permanent, referenceable record; can be precise and detailed; can be sent to many people at once. Disadvantages: slower feedback; tone can be misread; requires literacy and may be ignored.
- **Visual communication:** graphs, charts, diagrams, photographs, infographics, organisation charts. Advantages: can communicate complex data quickly and memorably; overcomes some language barriers. Disadvantages: can oversimplify; requires accompanying explanation for full understanding.
- **Electronic communication:** email, instant messaging (e.g. Slack, WhatsApp Business), video conferencing, intranets, social media. Advantages: fast, cheap, can reach global audiences instantly, often two-way. Disadvantages: risk of information overload, security/confidentiality risks, potential for technical failure, can reduce personal connection.

### 4. Formal vs Informal Communication
**Formal communication** follows the organisation's official structure — it moves through recognised channels such as management reports, official memos, board meetings, or contracts, and is usually documented. It is used for important, official, or legally significant information (e.g. disciplinary procedures, contracts of employment, board decisions) because it creates accountability and a clear record.

**Informal communication** occurs outside official channels — casual conversations, the "grapevine," personal messages between colleagues. It travels quickly and can boost morale and team cohesion, but it is unregulated, may distort information as it passes between people (the "grapevine effect" or "Chinese whispers" effect), and cannot be relied upon for accuracy or accountability.

Businesses generally rely on a mix of both: formal channels for direction, policy, and accountability; informal channels for building relationships and morale. A well-run business tries to ensure that informal communication doesn't have to fill gaps left by poor formal communication — e.g. rumours filling a vacuum when management fails to announce a restructuring officially.

### 5. Why Effective Communication Matters
Good communication:
- Improves **efficiency** — employees understand instructions correctly the first time, reducing wasted time and errors.
- Improves **motivation** — employees who are kept informed feel valued and trusted (links to Chapter 21, The Importance of Motivation in the Workplace).
- Reduces **conflict** — misunderstandings are a major source of workplace disputes.
- Improves **decision-making** — managers need accurate, timely information from all levels of the business to make good decisions.
- Supports good **customer relations** — clear communication with customers builds trust and repeat business.
- Enables **coordination** — larger businesses with many departments (see Chapter 23, Organisation Structure) rely on communication to keep everyone working toward the same objectives.

Poor communication, by contrast, causes duplicated work, missed deadlines, low morale, poor customer service, and — in the worst cases — safety failures or reputational damage.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Toyota Motor Corporation, automotive manufacturing, Japan.
**Situation:** Toyota's production system includes the "andon cord" (or andon system), which allows any assembly-line worker to stop the entire production line the moment they spot a defect or problem. This is a deliberate two-way communication mechanism ensuring information about quality issues travels instantly from the shop floor to management, rather than being filtered or delayed through layers of hierarchy.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates the value of fast, two-way, formal communication channels in improving efficiency and quality, and shows how communication structures can be deliberately designed into operations.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the advantages and disadvantages for a large manufacturer of giving every worker the power to stop production."

**Company/context:** Boeing, aerospace manufacturing, United States.
**Situation:** Investigations into the 737 MAX crashes (2018–2019) identified, among other factors, that critical safety information about a new flight-control system (MCAS) was not clearly communicated to airlines, regulators, and pilots, contributing to a loss of trust in the company and significant reputational and financial damage, including grounding of the entire 737 MAX fleet.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the severe consequences of poor communication between a business, its stakeholders, and regulators, including reputational damage, loss of consumer confidence, and financial cost.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the importance of transparent communication with external stakeholders (e.g. regulators and customers) for a large multinational business."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business owner can often communicate directly and informally with every employee face-to-face, since the team is small and the structure flat — formal written channels may feel unnecessary for day-to-day matters. A large multinational business, however, must rely heavily on formal, often electronic, communication systems (intranets, video conferencing, standardised reporting) to ensure consistent messages reach thousands of employees across multiple countries and time zones, and must invest more deliberately in preventing communication breakdown across its longer chain of command (see Chapter 23).
