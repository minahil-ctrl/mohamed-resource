# Chapter 24: Departmental Functions

**Unit:** Unit 2 — People in Business
**Specification reference:** 4BS1 Topic 2.5 — Organisational Structure / Departmental Functions
**Applies to:** Both (Paper 1 and Paper 2), with departmental separation becoming more distinct as a business grows (particularly relevant to Paper 2)
**Tracker code:** 2.9

## Learning Objectives
- Define the main functional departments found in a business: production, finance, marketing, and human resources (personnel).
- Explain the core responsibilities of each department.
- Explain why and how departments must communicate and coordinate with one another.
- Apply knowledge of departmental functions to a given business scenario (AO2).
- Analyse the consequences of poor coordination or communication between departments.
- Evaluate how a business should organise its departments as it grows in size (AO3/AO4).

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Department | A section of a business organised around a particular function or specialism | The marketing department of a supermarket chain |
| Production department | The department responsible for making the business's products or delivering its services, including managing operations and quality | A factory's production department scheduling machinery and staff to meet output targets |
| Finance department | The department responsible for managing the business's money, including recording transactions, preparing accounts, managing cash flow, and budgeting | The finance department preparing the annual profit and loss account |
| Marketing department | The department responsible for identifying customer needs and promoting, pricing, and distributing the business's products to meet them | The marketing department launching an advertising campaign for a new product |
| Human resources (personnel) department | The department responsible for recruiting, training, appraising, and looking after the welfare of employees, and for ensuring compliance with employment law | The HR department handling recruitment and disciplinary procedures |
| Interrelationship (between departments) | The way in which the work and decisions of one department affect, and depend on, the work of other departments | Production needing sales forecasts from marketing to plan output levels |
| Line of communication | The formal route through which information passes between departments and levels of an organisation | A weekly report passed from the production manager to the finance director |
| Functional structure | An organisational structure in which staff are grouped into departments based on the specialist function they perform | A business organised into separate production, finance, marketing, and HR departments, each reporting to a director |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Every product a customer buys — a phone from Apple, a meal from a restaurant chain, a car from Toyota — is the result of several departments working together: marketing researching what customers want and promoting the finished product, production actually making it, finance ensuring the business can afford to make it and tracking whether it is profitable, and human resources ensuring the right people are recruited, trained, and retained to do all of this. When these departments fail to communicate — for example, when marketing promises a delivery date that production cannot realistically meet — the result is exactly the kind of costly, reputation-damaging failure that recurs across real businesses of every size. Understanding what each department does, and how they depend on one another, is essential to understanding how a business actually functions as a whole system.

## Core Content

### 1. Why Businesses Organise into Departments
As a business grows beyond a size where one person (the owner) can manage every aspect of it, tasks are typically divided among specialist **departments**, each responsible for a particular function. This is a practical application of the division of labour: employees and managers can specialise in a particular area (finance, marketing, production, or people management), developing greater expertise and efficiency than a single generalist could achieve across all areas. A business organised this way is said to have a **functional structure** — one in which departments are defined by the specialist function they perform, and each is usually headed by a departmental manager or director who reports to the owner, managing director, or board.

### 2. The Production Department
The **production department** is responsible for actually making the business's goods or delivering its services. Its core responsibilities include: planning and scheduling production to meet expected demand, managing the resources needed for production (materials, machinery, labour), controlling the quality of output, and managing stock levels of raw materials, work-in-progress, and finished goods. The production department must work closely with marketing (to know how much to produce and when, based on sales forecasts and orders), with finance (to stay within budget for materials, labour, and equipment), and with human resources (to ensure sufficient trained staff are available for production schedules). Poor coordination here — for example, producing without accurate sales forecasts — can lead directly to costly problems: overproduction (excess stock, storage costs, potential wastage) or underproduction (unable to meet customer demand, lost sales, reputational damage).

### 3. The Finance Department
The **finance department** is responsible for managing the business's money. Its core responsibilities include: recording all financial transactions accurately, preparing financial statements (such as the income statement and statement of financial position — see Unit 4), managing cash flow to ensure the business can pay its debts as they fall due, preparing and monitoring budgets for other departments, and providing financial information to support management decision-making (e.g. whether a new product launch is affordable, or whether a piece of machinery should be purchased). Finance must work closely with every other department, since every department's activities have a financial cost or consequence: it sets and monitors production's materials budget, funds marketing's advertising campaigns, and pays for human resources' recruitment and training costs. If finance imposes budgets without proper consultation with the departments affected, or if other departments overspend without informing finance, cash flow problems and inaccurate financial planning can quickly follow.

### 4. The Marketing Department
The **marketing department** is responsible for identifying what customers want and ensuring the business's products meet those needs, and for promoting, pricing, and distributing those products effectively (the "marketing mix" — see Unit 3). Its core responsibilities include: conducting market research to understand customer needs and competitor activity, forecasting future sales and demand, deciding on branding, pricing, and promotional strategy, and managing distribution channels to get products to customers. Marketing must communicate closely with production (providing sales forecasts so production knows how much to make, and communicating any special features or specifications customers want), and with finance (agreeing an advertising and promotional budget, and providing sales revenue projections). A marketing department that promises features, prices, or delivery timelines without first consulting production and finance about what is actually feasible is a classic and heavily examined source of interdepartmental conflict.

### 5. The Human Resources (Personnel) Department
The **human resources department** (sometimes called the personnel department) is responsible for managing the people side of the business. Its core responsibilities include: workforce planning (forecasting future staffing needs), recruitment and selection (see Chapter 18), organising training and development (see Chapter 20), managing appraisal and performance systems, handling pay and employee benefits administration, ensuring compliance with employment law (see Chapter 19), and managing employee relations, including grievance and disciplinary procedures. Human resources must work closely with every department that needs staff, since it is responsible for ensuring each department has the right number of appropriately trained employees at the right time — for example, if production plans to expand output, HR must recruit and train sufficient staff in time to support that expansion, requiring early and accurate communication between the two departments.

### 6. Interrelationships and Communication Between Departments
No department in a business operates in isolation — each depends on information and resources from the others to do its own job effectively. Common interrelationships include: marketing needing production's input on what is realistically achievable before promising it to customers; production needing marketing's sales forecasts to plan output and materials purchasing; all departments needing finance to approve and monitor budgets for their activities; and every department needing human resources to ensure it is adequately staffed. Effective **lines of communication** between departments (see Chapter 16, Good Communication) — regular meetings, shared reporting systems, and clear reporting relationships defined by the organisation chart (Chapter 23) — are essential to keep departments coordinated. When communication between departments breaks down, the consequences can include: production making goods that do not match what marketing has actually sold or promised, financial overspending because departments were not aware of budget limits, understaffing or overstaffing because HR was not given accurate forecasts, and, ultimately, dissatisfied customers and reduced profitability.

**Exam pitfall:** A common weakness in student answers is describing each department's function in isolation without explaining *how* departments depend on one another. Top-level answers to "analyse" or "evaluate" questions on this topic almost always focus on a specific interrelationship (e.g. marketing and production) and trace through the practical consequence of a communication failure between them, rather than simply re-describing each department's job separately.

## Diagrams Required
1. **Type:** Functional organisation chart.
   **Purpose:** To show a typical functional structure with the four core departments.
   **Content spec:** Top box "Managing Director / Board." Four boxes below, connected to the top box: "Production Department," "Finance Department," "Marketing Department," "Human Resources Department." Beneath each departmental box, list 2–3 core responsibilities (e.g. under Production: "scheduling, quality control, stock management").

2. **Type:** Interrelationship / network diagram.
   **Purpose:** To show how the four departments depend on and communicate with one another (not just upward to the top of the hierarchy).
   **Content spec:** Four boxes arranged in a square or circle — Production, Finance, Marketing, Human Resources — with double-headed arrows connecting every pair of boxes. Label each arrow with the key information flow, e.g. Production ↔ Marketing: "sales forecasts / production capacity"; Production ↔ Finance: "materials budget / cost data"; Marketing ↔ Finance: "promotional budget / sales revenue"; HR ↔ all others: "staffing levels and training needs."

3. **Type:** Cause-and-effect flow diagram.
   **Purpose:** To illustrate the consequence of a communication breakdown between departments (worked scenario).
   **Content spec:** Flow: "Marketing promises a delivery date to a major customer without consulting Production → Production cannot meet the deadline with current staff/materials → Late delivery / rushed, lower-quality output → Customer dissatisfaction and possible lost future orders → Reduced revenue and reputational damage." Side note: "Could have been prevented by a shared line of communication between Marketing and Production before the promise was made."

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Airbus SE, aerospace manufacturing, multinational (headquartered in France, with major operations in the UK, Germany, and Spain).
**Situation:** Airbus's A380 programme experienced well-documented production delays in the mid-2000s, which were widely attributed in part to coordination problems between design/engineering teams in different countries using incompatible versions of design software for the aircraft's wiring, causing components built in different national factories not to fit together correctly when assembled.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the serious real-world consequences of poor coordination and communication between departments/teams (here, engineering and production coordination across sites) in a very large, complex organisation.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the steps a large, multi-site manufacturing business like Airbus could take to prevent departmental coordination failures of this kind."

**Company/context:** Zara (Inditex Group), fashion retail, Spain.
**Situation:** Zara is widely recognised for the exceptionally close, fast coordination between its design, production, and marketing/retail functions, enabling it to move a new design from concept to in-store availability in a period of a few weeks rather than the many months typical of the traditional fashion industry, by keeping much of its production in-house and geographically close to its design and distribution operations.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the competitive advantage that can result from strong interrelationships and fast communication between the marketing/design function and the production function.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the extent to which close coordination between departments, as at Zara, could be copied by a competitor that manufactures its products overseas through outside suppliers."

## Large vs Small Business Application
In a small business, one person — often the owner — may perform the roles of several "departments" simultaneously (for example, personally handling sales, bookkeeping, and hiring), so interdepartmental communication is informal and immediate because there is often no real separation between functions. In a large business, each function is usually managed by a separate, specialised department with its own manager or director, allowing greater expertise and efficiency in each area, but requiring formal systems of communication, reporting, and coordination (supported by the organisation structure covered in Chapter 23) to prevent the kind of costly misalignment between departments illustrated by the Airbus case study above.
