## Learning Objectives
- Define quality, quality control, quality assurance, and total quality management (TQM), and distinguish clearly between them.
- Explain the methods a business can use to monitor and improve quality.
- Identify the costs of poor quality to a business.
- Explain the purpose and significance of quality standards and certification such as ISO 9000.
- Analyse the advantages and disadvantages of quality control, quality assurance, and TQM as approaches to managing quality.
- Evaluate which approach to quality management is most appropriate for a given business scenario.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | The degree to which a product or service meets the specifications set for it and satisfies customer expectations, reliably and consistently | A smartphone that functions as advertised without defects, batch after batch |
| Quality control | A system of checking (usually by inspection) the quality of output at the end of the production process, or at set inspection points, to identify and remove defective items | A quality inspector checking a sample of finished bottles for cracks before packaging |
| Quality assurance | A system that builds quality checks into every stage of the production process, giving each worker responsibility for checking their own and the previous stage's work, so that defects are prevented rather than only detected at the end | Each worker on an assembly line signing off their sub-assembly as meeting standard before it passes to the next stage |
| Total quality management (TQM) | A company-wide philosophy in which every employee, at every level and in every department, takes responsibility for quality and is committed to continuous improvement, with the aim of "getting it right first time" | A hotel chain where housekeeping, reception, and management all treat guest satisfaction as their personal responsibility |
| Quality circles | Small groups of employees who meet regularly to identify quality problems and suggest improvements | A weekly meeting of production-line staff proposing fixes to a recurring defect |
| Zero defects | A target/philosophy of TQM aiming to eliminate errors and defects entirely, rather than accepting a "tolerable" defect rate | A pharmaceutical manufacturer aiming for no faulty tablets to reach a batch at all |
| Cost of quality | The total cost to a business of achieving good quality (prevention and appraisal costs) plus the cost of failing to achieve it (internal and external failure costs) | The combined cost of inspectors' wages, wasted materials from rejected units, and refunds to unhappy customers |
| Quality standard | A defined, documented level of quality or a set of processes that a product or business must meet, often assessed by an external body | A toy manufacturer meeting the EU's CE safety marking requirements |
| ISO 9000 | An internationally recognised family of quality management standards, developed by the International Organization for Standardization, certifying that a business has effective, documented quality management systems and processes in place | A supplier displaying ISO 9001 certification to reassure business customers of its quality processes |
| Benchmarking | Comparing a business's products, processes, or performance against the best in the industry (or against a direct competitor) to identify areas for improvement | A budget airline comparing its on-time performance against the industry leader |
| Kaizen | Continuous, incremental improvement, typically driven by employee suggestions (see Chapter 42, Lean Production) | Workers submitting small process-improvement ideas every week |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Quality failures can destroy a business almost overnight — Samsung's Galaxy Note 7 recall in 2016, triggered by batteries that could overheat and catch fire, cost the company an estimated several billion dollars and lasting reputational damage, all traceable to a quality-control failure in battery manufacturing. At the other end of the spectrum, Toyota built one of the most trusted brands in the world largely on a reputation for reliability, underpinned by decades of quality-assurance and continuous-improvement practices embedded throughout its production system. For any business, quality is not a cost to be minimised but an investment that determines customer trust, brand reputation, and ultimately survival.

## Core Content

### 1. What Is Quality?
**Quality** refers to the degree to which a good or service meets the specifications set for it and satisfies the expectations of the customer, reliably and consistently, every time it is produced. Quality is not the same as luxury or high specification — a budget product can be "high quality" if it consistently and reliably does exactly what it is designed and marketed to do, while an expensive product that frequently fails is low quality. The two dimensions typically assessed are: **fitness for purpose** (does the product do what it is meant to do?) and **consistency/reliability** (does it do this every single time, across every unit produced?).

Quality matters commercially because it affects: customer satisfaction and repeat purchase, brand reputation, the ability to charge a premium price, the level of wastage and rework costs, and (for businesses selling to other businesses) eligibility to be a supplier at all, since many large firms now require formal quality certification from their suppliers.

### 2. Quality Control
**Quality control (QC)** is the traditional approach to managing quality: output is inspected, usually by a dedicated quality-control team or department, at the end of the production process (or at defined checkpoints along it), and any items that do not meet specification are identified and removed — either scrapped, reworked, or sold as seconds/rejects.

**How it works:** Inspection can be done on every unit (100% inspection) or, more commonly for high-volume production, on a **sample** of units from each batch, using statistical sampling methods to estimate the defect rate of the whole batch.

**Advantages of quality control:**
- Simple to set up and understand; does not require every worker to change how they work.
- Provides a clear final check before products reach the customer.
- Useful where 100% reliability of a specific, testable specification is critical (e.g. safety testing).

**Disadvantages of quality control:**
- **Reactive, not preventive** — defects are only caught after they have already been produced, meaning the wasted materials, labour, and machine time invested in a defective unit are already lost.
- Relies on a separate inspection team/department, adding a direct cost without adding value to the product itself.
- Sample-based inspection can miss defective units that are not included in the sample.
- Places responsibility for quality only on inspectors, which can reduce other workers' sense of ownership over quality.

### 3. Quality Assurance
**Quality assurance (QA)** moves quality checking earlier and throughout the production process, rather than leaving it to a final inspection stage. Under QA, each worker (or each stage of production) is made responsible for checking that their own work — and often the work received from the previous stage — meets the required standard before it is allowed to proceed to the next stage. Written procedures, standardised checklists, and documented processes are typically used to make quality expectations explicit at every step.

**Advantages of quality assurance:**
- **Preventive rather than reactive** — problems are identified and corrected close to where they occur, before further time, labour, and materials are wasted on a faulty item.
- Distributes responsibility for quality across the whole workforce rather than concentrating it in a separate inspection department, which can increase worker engagement and pride in their own output.
- Tends to reduce the overall cost of poor quality compared with quality control alone, since fewer fully finished defective units are produced.

**Disadvantages of quality assurance:**
- Requires investment in training every worker in quality procedures and standards.
- Can slow down production, since each stage must pause to check quality before passing work on.
- Requires a cultural shift — workers must accept genuine responsibility for quality rather than assuming "someone else will catch it later."

### 4. Total Quality Management (TQM)
**Total quality management (TQM)** goes further still: it is a company-wide philosophy, not merely a production-line technique, in which every single employee — from senior management to shop-floor workers, and across every department including marketing, finance, and human resources, not just production — is expected to take personal responsibility for quality and to continuously look for ways to improve it. The guiding aim of TQM is often summarised as **"getting it right first time"** and pursuing **zero defects**, rather than accepting a tolerable error rate.

**Key features of TQM:**
- **Continuous improvement (kaizen):** quality is never treated as "finished" — small, incremental improvements are constantly sought (see Chapter 42, Lean Production, for the fuller treatment of kaizen).
- **Employee involvement at all levels:** **quality circles** — small groups of employees meeting regularly to discuss and solve quality problems — are a common TQM tool, since those closest to a process often best understand its weaknesses.
- **Customer focus:** quality is defined in terms of meeting or exceeding customer expectations, not just meeting an internal technical specification.
- **Whole-organisation scope:** TQM treats quality as everyone's job — an accounting error, a late delivery caused by poor logistics planning, or a rude customer-service interaction are all treated as "quality failures" just as much as a defective physical product.
- **Leadership commitment:** TQM requires visible, sustained commitment from senior management, since a genuine quality culture cannot be imposed solely from the shop floor.

**Advantages of TQM:** can achieve the lowest overall long-run cost of poor quality; builds a strong, quality-focused organisational culture; tends to improve staff motivation, since employees are trusted with responsibility (links to Herzberg's motivators, Unit 4); supports strong brand reputation for reliability.

**Disadvantages of TQM:** requires significant time, training, and cultural change to implement fully, often taking years; can be expensive and disruptive to introduce; requires consistent long-term commitment from management, and often fails when adopted only partially or as a short-term initiative rather than a genuine culture change.

**Exam pitfall:** A very common error is treating "quality control," "quality assurance," and "TQM" as synonyms, or as simply three names for the same thing done with different levels of enthusiasm. Examiners expect students to identify the specific structural difference: **QC checks quality at the end**, **QA builds checking into every stage of production**, and **TQM extends responsibility for quality to every employee across the whole organisation, as an ongoing culture, not just a production technique.**

### 5. The Costs of Poor Quality
Failing to achieve good quality is expensive, and the costs fall into two broad categories:

**Internal failure costs** — costs incurred *before* a defective product reaches the customer:
- Wasted raw materials and labour time on units that must be scrapped or reworked.
- Machine downtime while faults are corrected.
- Cost of re-inspecting reworked items.

**External failure costs** — costs incurred *after* a defective product has already reached the customer:
- Cost of refunds, replacements, or repairs under warranty.
- Cost of product recalls (which can be enormous — see the Samsung case study below).
- Lost future sales and damaged brand reputation as dissatisfied customers switch to competitors or warn others.
- Potential legal costs or compensation claims, particularly where poor quality causes injury or harm.

Businesses also incur costs in *achieving* good quality in the first place — **prevention costs** (staff training, quality-system design, supplier vetting) and **appraisal costs** (inspection, testing, and monitoring). A key principle in operations management is that investing more in prevention and appraisal usually reduces the far larger costs of internal and external failure — in other words, spending a little to get it right first time is normally cheaper than the cost of fixing it after the fact, especially once reputational damage is included.

**Worked example (illustrative cost comparison):** A factory produces 10,000 units. Under a quality-control-only system, 3% of units (300 units) are found defective at final inspection, each costing $40 in wasted materials/labour to scrap = $12,000 in internal failure costs, plus the risk that some undetected defects reach customers. If investing $5,000 in quality-assurance training and process redesign reduced the defect rate to 0.5% (50 units, $2,000 in internal failure costs), the business would spend $5,000 to save $10,000 in scrapping costs alone — before even counting the avoided risk of costly external failures such as recalls or lost customers.

### 6. Quality Standards and Certification
Many businesses seek external, independently verified **quality standards** to formally demonstrate that their products or processes meet a defined level of quality. The most widely recognised international example is **ISO 9000** (and its most commonly certified standard, **ISO 9001**), a family of standards published by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) that certifies a business has documented, consistent, and effectively managed quality management systems in place — covering everything from how processes are documented to how customer complaints are handled and how continuous improvement is pursued.

**Why businesses seek quality certification:**
- It provides independent, credible proof of quality processes to customers, particularly important in business-to-business (B2B) markets, where many large firms will only accept suppliers who hold recognised certification.
- It can be a condition of entering certain markets, tendering for government contracts, or being accepted into an international supply chain.
- The process of preparing for certification often forces a business to formalise and improve its own quality processes, delivering internal benefits beyond the certificate itself.
- It can be used as a marketing/reputational tool, signalling quality and reliability to consumers as well as business customers.

**Limitation:** Certification confirms that a business has a consistent, documented quality *process* in place — it does not guarantee that every individual product is defect-free, and certification can be costly and time-consuming for a small business to obtain and maintain.

### 7. Methods of Monitoring and Improving Quality (Summary)
Beyond the three overarching approaches above, businesses use specific tools and methods to monitor and improve quality on an ongoing basis: statistical sampling and inspection (a QC tool); quality circles and employee suggestion schemes (a TQM tool); **benchmarking** against competitors or industry best practice to identify performance gaps; supplier vetting and quality audits, to ensure poor-quality inputs do not enter the production process in the first place; and customer feedback/complaints analysis, used to identify recurring quality issues directly from the market.

**Cross-reference:** Links to Chapter 42 (Lean Production, since kaizen and quality circles are shared tools between lean production and TQM), Chapter 41 (Production and Productivity, since reworking defective output reduces effective productivity), and Chapter 43 (Technology in Production, since automated inspection technology, such as machine-vision systems, is increasingly used for quality control).

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Toyota, automotive manufacturing, Japan (global operations).
**Situation:** Toyota's production system is widely studied as an example of quality assurance and continuous improvement embedded throughout the production line — workers on Toyota assembly lines have long had the authority to stop the entire production line (the "andon cord" system) the moment they identify a quality problem, rather than allowing a defect to continue further down the line, reflecting a quality-assurance and TQM philosophy of catching and fixing problems at the source.
**Link to theory:** Quality assurance (checks built into every stage) and TQM (worker-level responsibility and authority for quality, continuous improvement).
**Discussion question:** "Discuss whether giving individual line workers the authority to stop an entire production line is a strength or a risk of Toyota's approach to quality."

**Company/context:** Samsung Electronics, consumer electronics manufacturing, South Korea (global operations).
**Situation:** In 2016, Samsung recalled its Galaxy Note 7 smartphone worldwide after reports of batteries overheating and, in some cases, catching fire, following a battery manufacturing defect; the recall and subsequent permanent discontinuation of the product were widely reported to have cost Samsung several billion dollars, alongside significant reputational damage.
**Link to theory:** External failure costs of poor quality (recall costs, reputational damage, lost sales) and the consequences of a quality-control/quality-assurance failure at the manufacturing stage reaching the customer.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse how a business such as Samsung can rebuild customer trust in the quality of its products following a major product recall."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business (Paper 1), such as an independent bakery, is more likely to rely on simple, informal quality control — the owner or a small number of staff visually checking finished products before sale — since it lacks the scale, budget, or dedicated staff to run a formal quality-assurance system or pursue costly external certification such as ISO 9000. A large or multinational business (Paper 2), such as Toyota or Samsung, has the scale and resources to implement full TQM systems, invest in extensive staff training, pursue internationally recognised quality certification as a condition of supplying other large businesses, and absorb the significant cost and cultural change required to embed quality responsibility across every department and every stage of a complex, often global, production process.
