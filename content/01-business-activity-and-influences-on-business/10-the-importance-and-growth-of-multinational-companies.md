## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Multinational company (MNC) | A business that owns or controls production or service operations in more than one country. | Toyota manufacturing vehicles in Japan, the United States, and Thailand. |
| Organic (internal) growth | Growth achieved by expanding a business's own existing operations, e.g. opening new outlets or increasing output, rather than joining with another firm. | A retailer opening new stores in additional countries one by one. |
| External (inorganic) growth | Growth achieved by merging with or taking over another business. | One company acquiring a competitor already operating in a foreign market. |
| Merger | The joining together of two businesses, by mutual agreement, to form a single new business. | Two airlines agreeing to combine into one larger company. |
| Takeover (acquisition) | The purchase of one business by another, where the acquiring business gains control. | A large company buying a majority shareholding in a smaller foreign competitor. |
| Foreign direct investment (FDI) | Investment by a business from one country into productive assets (e.g. factories, offices) located in another country. | A car manufacturer building a new assembly plant abroad. |
| Horizontal integration | A merger or takeover between two businesses at the same stage of production in the same industry. | Two rival smartphone manufacturers merging. |
| Vertical integration | A merger or takeover between two businesses at different stages of the same supply chain. | A coffee retailer acquiring a coffee bean plantation (backward) or a chain of cafés (forward). |
| Conglomerate integration | A merger or takeover between two businesses in unrelated industries. | A tobacco company acquiring a food and beverage business. |
| Repatriation of profit | The transfer of profits earned by a multinational's foreign operations back to its home country. | A multinational sending profits earned in its overseas subsidiaries back to shareholders in its home country. |
| Economies of scale | The cost advantages a business gains as it increases its scale of production, lowering average cost per unit. | A multinational car manufacturer buying components in bulk at a lower price per unit. |
| Host economy | The country in which a multinational company operates a subsidiary or facility, other than its home country. | Vietnam, as the host economy for a foreign-owned garment factory. |
| Home economy | The country in which a multinational company's headquarters is based. | The United States, as the home economy of an American multinational. |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)

A handful of multinational companies — including Walmart, Amazon, Saudi Aramco, and Apple — generate annual revenues larger than the entire economic output (GDP) of many individual countries. Understanding how and why businesses grow into multinationals explains some of the most powerful economic forces shaping the modern world: where jobs are created and lost, which governments multinationals can influence, and why a single company's decision to open or close a factory can transform the fortunes of an entire town or region. This chapter builds on the definition of a multinational introduced in Chapter 04 by examining exactly how and why that growth happens, and why it matters so much to the global economy.

## Core Content

### Recap: What Makes a Company Multinational?

As introduced in Chapter 04, a **multinational company (MNC)** is a business that owns or controls production or service facilities in more than one country, usually while maintaining a headquarters in a single "home" country. Multinationals range from manufacturing giants with factories on several continents (e.g. Toyota) to service and technology businesses with offices and data centres spread across dozens of countries (e.g. Google/Alphabet). What all multinationals share is direct ownership or control of operations abroad — this distinguishes a true multinational from a business that simply exports its products to foreign customers without owning any foreign operations.

### How Businesses Grow to Become Multinational

Businesses can grow internationally through two broad routes:

**1. Organic (internal) growth.** A business expands step by step using its own resources and profits — for example, opening new company-owned stores, offices, or factories in additional countries one at a time, or setting up a new overseas subsidiary from scratch (a form of **foreign direct investment**, or FDI). Organic growth is typically slower but lower-risk, since the business builds its own operations under its own established systems and culture.

**2. External (inorganic) growth** through merger or takeover. A business grows internationally more quickly by joining with, or acquiring, a business that already operates in a foreign market:
- **Horizontal integration** — merging with or acquiring a business at the same stage of production in the same industry, often to quickly gain market share, customers, and production capacity in a new country.
- **Vertical integration** — merging with or acquiring a business at a different stage of the same supply chain, either **backward** (acquiring a supplier, e.g. a raw-material source) or **forward** (acquiring a distributor or retailer), often to secure supply, cut costs, or control distribution in a foreign market.
- **Conglomerate integration** — merging with or acquiring a business in a completely unrelated industry, often to diversify risk by spreading operations across different, unrelated markets and economic conditions.

External growth is typically faster than organic growth, since the acquiring business immediately gains the target's existing customers, staff, brand, local market knowledge, and infrastructure. However, it carries substantial risks: cultural and management clashes between the merging organisations, the high cost of the acquisition itself, and the difficulty of successfully integrating two previously separate businesses (a challenge often referred to as "merger failure" when it goes wrong).

**Worked example (integration type):** A UK-based coffee shop chain buys a coffee bean plantation in Colombia. This is an example of **backward vertical integration** — the business has acquired an earlier stage of its own supply chain (the raw material source) in a foreign country, which can secure a reliable supply of coffee beans and reduce dependence on external suppliers, while also making the company multinational by giving it a directly owned operation abroad.

### Reasons for International Expansion

Businesses choose to expand into other countries, and thereby become multinational, for several interconnected reasons:

- **Access to new and larger markets.** Expanding abroad allows a business to reach far more customers than a single domestic market can offer, particularly valuable once a business has reached market saturation at home.
- **Lower costs of production.** Establishing factories or operations in countries with cheaper labour, land, energy, or raw materials can significantly reduce a multinational's overall production costs, increasing competitiveness and profit margins.
- **Access to raw materials.** Operating directly in a country rich in a required raw material (e.g. oil, minerals, or agricultural produce) can secure supply and reduce transport/import costs compared with sourcing it from abroad.
- **Avoiding trade barriers.** By producing inside a foreign market rather than exporting into it, a multinational can avoid tariffs, quotas, or other trade restrictions that would otherwise raise the cost of selling in that market.
- **Economies of scale.** Operating on a larger, international scale allows a multinational to spread fixed costs (e.g. research and development, marketing, head-office administration) over a much larger volume of output, reducing average cost per unit.
- **Spreading and reducing risk.** Operating across multiple countries reduces a business's dependence on the economic conditions, exchange rate, or political stability of any single country — if one market performs poorly, strong performance elsewhere can offset the loss.
- **Following key customers or competitors.** A business supplying a large multinational client may open operations in a new country simply to remain close to that client's international operations; equally, a business may expand abroad defensively to match or pre-empt a competitor's international growth.

### The Economic Importance of Multinational Growth

The continued growth of multinational companies has significant effects on both **home** and **host** economies:

**Importance/benefits to host economies:**
- Creation of large numbers of jobs, often including skilled positions, reducing unemployment.
- Transfer of technology, management expertise, and workforce training/skills to the local economy.
- Increased tax revenue for the host government from the multinational's local profits and employees' income.
- Improved local infrastructure, sometimes developed specifically to support the multinational's operations (e.g. roads, ports, or utilities).
- Increased export earnings if the multinational's local output is sold internationally.

**Risks/costs to host economies:**
- **Repatriation of profit** back to the home country, meaning much of the wealth generated may not stay in, or be reinvested in, the host economy.
- Risk that the multinational exploits weaker regulatory standards (e.g. labour or environmental law) in the host country compared with its home country.
- Local small businesses may be unable to compete with a large multinational's scale, potentially reducing local entrepreneurship and competition over time.
- Economic dependence on the multinational, with severe local job losses if the company later relocates operations to a lower-cost country.

**Importance to the home economy:**
- Multinationals headquartered domestically can generate significant export earnings, corporate tax revenue, and skilled head-office employment in their home country, even while much of their production happens abroad.
- Successful multinationals enhance a home country's international economic influence and reputation (sometimes described as a form of "soft power").

**Common misconception:** students often assume multinational expansion is purely beneficial for a host country, or purely exploitative. Strong AO3/AO4 answers weigh both the substantial benefits (jobs, technology transfer, tax revenue) and the genuine risks (profit repatriation, regulatory concerns, dependency) in the specific context given, rather than treating multinational growth as simply good or bad.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Toyota Motor Corporation, automotive manufacturing, Japan.
**Situation:** Toyota grew from a single Japanese manufacturer into one of the world's largest multinational car makers, establishing wholly owned manufacturing plants (a form of organic growth/foreign direct investment) in countries including the United States, the United Kingdom, Thailand, and India, rather than relying solely on exporting cars made in Japan. This allowed Toyota to avoid import tariffs in key markets, access lower production costs in some locations, and be closer to major customer bases.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates organic growth (foreign direct investment) as a method of becoming multinational, and demonstrates several classic reasons for international expansion: avoiding trade barriers, accessing new markets, and reducing production costs.
**Discussion question:** What are the risks to Toyota of relying on manufacturing plants spread across many different countries, compared with concentrating production in Japan?

**Company/context:** Anheuser-Busch InBev (AB InBev), brewing and beverages, headquartered in Belgium (formed through international mergers).
**Situation:** AB InBev became the world's largest brewer largely through a long series of international mergers and takeovers, most notably the 2008 merger between Belgian-Brazilian brewer InBev and American brewer Anheuser-Busch (maker of Budweiser), and the 2016 acquisition of London-listed SABMiller, a business with major operations across Africa, Latin America, and Asia. Each major deal was an example of horizontal integration, combining brewers at the same stage of the industry across different countries.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates external (inorganic) growth through international horizontal mergers and takeovers as a rapid route to becoming a dominant multinational company, gaining immediate access to new countries' markets, brands, and production facilities rather than building them from scratch.
**Discussion question:** Analyse the risks a company like AB InBev takes on by growing rapidly through international mergers and takeovers rather than organic growth.

## Large vs Small Business Application

A small business typically has neither the capital nor the risk appetite to expand its own operations into another country, and if it engages with international markets at all, it usually does so by exporting its products abroad rather than owning facilities overseas — this falls short of true multinational status. A large business, by contrast, has the financial resources (often as a public limited company able to raise capital through share issues, as covered in Chapter 04) to pursue organic growth abroad or to fund large-scale international mergers and takeovers. The scale of a large multinational also allows it to spread the very substantial risks and costs of international expansion — including political, currency, and cultural risk — across a much larger overall business, something a small business could rarely absorb if an international venture failed.
