## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Every time a customer buys a foreign-made product, or a business ships goods abroad, exchange rates quietly decide who wins and who loses. When the Turkish lira or the Nigerian naira sharply depreciates, importers of raw materials suddenly face much higher costs — sometimes forcing price rises or job losses — while exporters in the same country can become more competitive overnight. Understanding exchange rates is not abstract economics; it is the difference between a profitable export order and a cancelled one.

## Core Content

### Why Businesses Trade Internationally
Businesses look beyond domestic borders for several interconnected reasons:
- **Market growth and diversification.** Domestic markets may be saturated or small; overseas markets offer new customers and reduce reliance on a single economy's performance.
- **Access to resources.** Raw materials, components, or skilled labour may be cheaper or only available abroad (e.g. cocoa sourced from Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana by chocolate manufacturers).
- **Economies of scale.** Selling internationally increases output volume, spreading fixed costs and lowering unit costs.
- **Following globalisation.** Improved transport, communication technology, and trade agreements have made international trade cheaper and faster, encouraging even small firms to trade abroad (e.g. via e-commerce platforms).
- **Government incentives.** Some governments offer export subsidies, tax breaks, or trade credit guarantees to encourage firms to sell overseas.
- **Risk spreading.** Operating across multiple countries reduces exposure to a downturn or political instability in any single market.

### Methods of Entering International Markets
Businesses choose an entry method based on cost, control, and risk:
- **Exporting** — producing at home and selling abroad; lowest risk and investment, but exposed to transport costs, tariffs, and exchange rate risk.
- **Licensing** — allowing a foreign firm to produce/sell your product under license for a fee; low investment but less control over quality.
- **Franchising** — similar to licensing but includes the full business model and brand; common in fast food and retail.
- **Joint ventures** — partnering with a local firm, sharing costs, risks, profits, and local market knowledge; useful where local regulations or culture create barriers to entry alone.
- **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) / setting up overseas production** — building or acquiring facilities abroad; highest cost and risk, but greatest control and often lower long-run costs (avoiding tariffs, closer to customers).

### Trade Barriers and Free Trade
Governments can restrict trade to protect domestic industries, jobs, and infant industries, or to retaliate against unfair trade practices. Common trade barriers include:
- **Tariffs** — taxes on imports that raise their price relative to domestic goods.
- **Quotas** — physical limits on how much of a good can be imported.
- **Embargoes** — a total ban on trade with a specific country or in a specific good.
- **Subsidies to domestic producers** — lowering domestic firms' costs so they can undercut importers.
- **Administrative/regulatory barriers** — complex customs procedures, product standards, or licensing requirements that make importing difficult.

Conversely, **trade blocs** (such as the EU, ASEAN, or Mercosur) and **free trade agreements** remove barriers between member countries, boosting trade volumes but exposing domestic firms in weaker industries to greater competition. The overall effect of protectionism is debated: it can protect jobs and infant industries in the short run, but tends to raise prices for consumers, invite retaliation, and reduce the competitive pressure that drives efficiency.

### Exchange Rates: Determination and Movement
An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another (e.g. £1 = $1.27 means one pound buys 1.27 US dollars). Most major currencies today operate under a **floating exchange rate system**, where the rate is determined by the forces of **demand and supply** for that currency in the foreign exchange (forex) market.

**Demand for a currency** increases when:
- Foreigners want to buy that country's exports (they must buy the currency to pay for them)
- Foreign investors want to invest in that country (buying assets, opening factories, or depositing savings to earn higher interest rates)
- Speculators believe the currency will rise in value and buy now to profit later

**Supply of a currency** increases when:
- Domestic residents want to buy imports (they must sell their own currency to buy foreign currency)
- Domestic investors want to invest abroad
- The central bank increases the money supply

When demand for a currency rises relative to supply, the currency **appreciates** (rises in value). When supply rises relative to demand, or demand falls, the currency **depreciates** (falls in value). Some countries also operate managed or fixed exchange rate systems, where the government or central bank intervenes to hold the currency at or near a target rate — but the IGCSE specification's core focus is understanding the effects of a currency's rise or fall, not the technical mechanics of fixed systems.

### The Effects of Exchange Rate Changes on Businesses

**When a currency APPRECIATES (strengthens):**
- Exports become **more expensive** for foreign buyers (in their currency) → export sales may fall, hurting exporters' revenue and competitiveness.
- Imports become **cheaper** for domestic buyers → businesses that import raw materials or components see lower costs; consumers benefit from cheaper foreign goods.
- Overall effect: exporters and firms competing with imports are disadvantaged; importers and consumers benefit.

**When a currency DEPRECIATES (weakens):**
- Exports become **cheaper** for foreign buyers → export sales may rise, boosting revenue for exporters and improving international competitiveness.
- Imports become **more expensive** for domestic buyers → businesses relying on imported materials or components face higher costs, which can squeeze profit margins or force price increases.
- Overall effect: exporters benefit; importers and consumers of imported goods are disadvantaged.

**Common misconception:** students often assume a "strong" currency is always "good" for a country's businesses. In reality, whether an appreciation is good or bad news depends entirely on whether the specific business is an exporter, an importer, or purely domestic. Exam answers must always specify *which type* of business is affected and *how*.

### Worked Calculation: The Effect of an Exchange Rate Change on Price

**Formula:** To convert a price into another currency, multiply (or divide, depending on direction) by the exchange rate.

**Scenario:** A UK exporter sells a product priced at £200. The exchange rate is £1 = $1.20.

**Step 1 — Calculate the US dollar price at the original exchange rate:**
$$ \text{Dollar price} = £200 \times 1.20 = \$240 $$

**Step 2 — The pound then appreciates to £1 = $1.50. Recalculate the dollar price of the same £200 product:**
$$ \text{Dollar price} = £200 \times 1.50 = \$300 $$

**Step 3 — Interpret the result:**
The product now costs US customers $300 instead of $240 — a $60 (25%) increase — even though the UK seller has not changed the price at all. The product is now less competitive in the US market purely because of the exchange rate movement.

| Scenario | Exchange rate (£1 = ) | UK price | Foreign price | % change in foreign price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before appreciation | $1.20 | £200 | $240 | — |
| After appreciation | $1.50 | £200 | $300 | +25% |
| After depreciation (from original) | $1.00 | £200 | $200 | −16.7% |

This table should be reproduced and calculated live in class with different starting values so students practise the mechanic, then asked to state which stakeholder (exporter, importer, consumer) is helped or harmed in each row.

**Exam pitfall:** students frequently multiply when they should divide, or forget to state whether the change helps or harms exporters vs importers. Always show the calculation, then translate it into a one-sentence business implication.

**Purpose:** To show how an exchange rate can fluctuate over time and to visually anchor the concepts of appreciation and depreciation.
**Content spec:** X-axis labelled "Time (months)" with 6–12 time points; Y-axis labelled "Exchange rate (£1 = $)" ranging approximately from $1.00 to $1.50. Plot a fluctuating line. Mark one clear upward-sloping section and label it "Appreciation ↑"; mark one clear downward-sloping section and label it "Depreciation ↓". Add an annotation box next to each labelled section stating the effect on exporters and importers (e.g. next to "Appreciation ↑": "Exports more expensive abroad; imports cheaper at home").

**Diagram 2 — Type:** Demand and supply diagram (forex market)
**Purpose:** To explain how exchange rates are determined and why they move.
**Content spec:** Standard demand/supply axes — X-axis "Quantity of currency (£)", Y-axis "Exchange rate ($ per £)". Downward-sloping demand curve (D) and upward-sloping supply curve (S) crossing at equilibrium point E1 with equilibrium exchange rate marked. Show a second diagram/overlay where the demand curve shifts right (D1) — e.g. due to rising demand for UK exports — leading to a new, higher equilibrium E2, illustrating appreciation. **Diagram 3 — Type:** Two-column comparison table (flowchart-style)
**Purpose:** To summarise, at a glance, who benefits and who loses from appreciation vs depreciation — a key revision tool.
**Content spec:** Columns headed "Currency Appreciates" and "Currency Depreciates". Rows: "Exporters", "Importers", "Consumers buying imports", "Tourists travelling abroad", "Foreign tourists visiting". Each cell states a one-line effect (e.g. Exporters/Appreciates = "Worse off — goods more expensive abroad").

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Jaguar Land Rover (JLR), automotive manufacturer, United Kingdom.
**Situation:** JLR exports a substantial share of its vehicles, particularly to markets such as the USA, China, and the EU. As a UK-based exporter with significant import content (parts sourced internationally) and export revenue earned in foreign currencies, the company's profitability is materially affected by movements in the pound's exchange rate against the dollar, euro, and yuan. Periods of a weaker pound have historically been reported by UK automotive exporters as improving competitiveness abroad, while a stronger pound raises the effective price of UK-built cars overseas.
**Link to theory:** Direct illustration of how exchange rate appreciation/depreciation affects export competitiveness and revenue for a major manufacturer.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss whether Jaguar Land Rover should try to reduce its exposure to exchange rate fluctuations, and how it might do so."

**Company/context:** Samsung Electronics, technology and electronics manufacturer, South Korea.
**Situation:** Samsung is one of the world's largest exporters of smartphones, semiconductors, and consumer electronics, with major production facilities across multiple countries including Vietnam and South Korea. As a multinational exporter, Samsung's overseas revenue (earned in dollars, euros, and other currencies) is affected when the Korean won appreciates or depreciates against these currencies, directly affecting the reported value of its foreign earnings when converted back to won.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a large multinational manages currency risk across multiple markets and how exchange rate movements affect the competitiveness of exported goods (e.g. semiconductors) in price-sensitive global markets.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the extent to which becoming a multinational company (producing in several countries) can help a business like Samsung reduce the risks created by exchange rate fluctuations."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business exporting for the first time (e.g. a local craft producer selling via an online marketplace to overseas customers) is highly vulnerable to exchange rate swings because it typically lacks the financial expertise, hedging tools, or cash reserves to manage currency risk, and even a small movement can wipe out thin profit margins on an order. A large multinational, by contrast, can use financial instruments (forward contracts, hedging), spread production and sales across many countries to naturally offset currency exposure, and has treasury departments dedicated to managing exchange rate risk — though the scale of its foreign earnings also means currency swings affect much larger sums of money in absolute terms.
