## Learning Objectives
- Define business objectives and identify common financial and non-financial measures of business success.
- State examples of quantitative and qualitative measures of success.
- Explain why different stakeholders may judge business success differently.
- Apply appropriate measures of success to a given business scenario.
- Analyse the relationship between a business's objectives and the measures it uses to judge its performance.
- Evaluate whether profit is always the best measure of business success.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Business objective | A specific goal that a business aims to achieve within a given time frame | A target to increase market share by 5% within a year |
| Profit | The financial surplus remaining after total costs are deducted from total revenue | A business earning $50,000 profit in a year |
| Profitability | A measure of profit relative to a business's size, typically expressed as a ratio or percentage | A 15% profit margin |
| Market share | The percentage of total sales in a market accounted for by one business | A company holding 20% of the smartphone market |
| Survival | Remaining in operation, often the primary short-term objective of a new or struggling business | A start-up focused on survival in its first year |
| Growth | An increase in the size or scale of a business's operations, sales, or output over time | A business growing revenue from $1m to $2m over three years |
| Sales revenue | The total income received from selling goods or services, before costs are deducted | $500,000 in sales revenue |
| Return on investment (ROI) | A measure comparing the profit earned from an investment to the amount invested, usually expressed as a percentage | A 10% return on a new machine investment |
| Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) | A business's voluntary commitment to operating in an economically, socially, and environmentally sustainable manner | A company funding local community projects |
| Customer satisfaction | The extent to which a business meets or exceeds customer expectations | High satisfaction scores from customer surveys |
| Employee satisfaction | The extent to which employees feel positively about their job and workplace | Low staff turnover indicating high employee satisfaction |
| Ethical objective | A goal relating to acting in a morally responsible way toward stakeholders and society | Committing to fair-trade sourcing of raw materials |
| Stakeholder | Any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, a business's activities | Shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, and the local community |
| Quantitative measure | A measure of success expressed in numerical/financial terms | Profit of $100,000; market share of 12% |
| Qualitative measure | A measure of success based on judgement or opinion rather than numbers | Positive brand reputation; strong customer loyalty |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Ask ten different stakeholders in the same company whether it is "successful," and you may get ten different answers: shareholders might point to rising profit, employees to job security and pay, customers to product quality, and the local community to environmental impact. Understanding that "success" is not a single number, but a set of objectives that can conflict with one another, is essential to understanding why real businesses — from a family-run shop to a global technology firm — make the strategic choices they do.

## Core Content

### Business Objectives and Why They Matter
A **business objective** is a specific, often measurable goal that a business sets out to achieve, typically within a defined time frame. Objectives give a business direction, provide a benchmark against which performance can be judged, help motivate and coordinate staff toward a shared purpose, and provide a basis for making decisions (e.g. whether to accept a new order, launch a product, or expand into a new market). Objectives commonly change as a business grows and matures — a new start-up typically prioritises **survival**, while an established, growing business may prioritise **market share** or **profit maximisation**, and a mature business might increasingly emphasise **corporate social responsibility** or **shareholder value**.

Common business objectives include:
- **Survival** — particularly important for new businesses or those in a difficult trading environment; the most basic objective, since a business that fails to survive cannot pursue any other objective.
- **Profit maximisation** — aiming to earn the largest possible profit, often associated with the traditional/classical view of why businesses exist.
- **Growth** — increasing the scale of the business (sales, output, number of employees, number of markets served), which can bring economies of scale and increased market power but also increased risk and management complexity.
- **Market share** — increasing the proportion of total market sales the business controls, often pursued because a larger market share can bring pricing power and brand recognition, even where short-term profit is sacrificed.
- **Social/ethical objectives** — pursuing goals related to environmental sustainability, fair treatment of workers, or community benefit, sometimes even where this reduces profit in the short term.
- **Shareholder value** — for a public limited company (plc), maximising the returns to shareholders, through dividends and/or a rising share price.

### Financial (Quantitative) Measures of Success
Financial measures are numerical and typically drawn from a business's accounts:
- **Profit** — the surplus of revenue over costs; often seen as the primary indicator of success, but a single profit figure does not indicate whether that profit is high or low relative to the size of the business.
- **Profitability (profit margins)** — profit expressed as a percentage of sales revenue (e.g. gross profit margin, net profit margin), allowing comparison between businesses of different sizes or between the same business over time.
- **Sales revenue/turnover** — total income from sales; a business can have rising sales revenue while profit falls if costs rise faster than revenue, which is a key exam point.
- **Market share** — indicates competitive strength relative to rivals, even if not directly a financial figure, it is usually calculated from sales data.
- **Return on investment (ROI)** — evaluates whether a specific investment (e.g. a new machine or marketing campaign) has generated a worthwhile financial return.
- **Cash flow position** — the ability to meet short-term financial obligations; a profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash (links to Unit 3 finance chapters).

### Non-Financial (Qualitative) Measures of Success
Not all measures of success can be reduced to numbers, and even where they can be quantified (e.g. a customer satisfaction score), the underlying judgement is subjective:
- **Customer satisfaction and loyalty** — repeat purchases, positive reviews, and brand reputation reflect whether a business is meeting customer needs, which underpins long-term financial performance.
- **Employee satisfaction and staff retention** — a motivated, low-turnover workforce is often associated with higher productivity and better customer service, even though it is not a direct financial figure.
- **Brand reputation and image** — how a business is perceived by the public, media, and other stakeholders, which can affect sales, ability to attract talent, and resilience to crises.
- **Achievement of ethical/social objectives** — meeting self-set targets around sustainability, community engagement, or fair labour practices, valued by an increasing number of stakeholders including investors and consumers.
- **Innovation** — the extent to which a business is successfully developing new products, services, or processes, often seen as a leading indicator of future competitiveness even before it shows up in financial results.

### Why Different Stakeholders Judge Success Differently
Because different stakeholders have different interests in a business, they naturally emphasise different measures of success:
- **Shareholders/owners** typically prioritise profit, return on investment, and share price/dividends.
- **Employees** typically prioritise job security, pay, working conditions, and opportunities for career progression.
- **Customers** typically prioritise product quality, price, and customer service.
- **Suppliers** typically prioritise reliable, prompt payment and a stable, ongoing trading relationship.
- **The local community and society** typically prioritise employment opportunities, environmental impact, and ethical conduct.
- **Government** typically is interested in tax revenue generated, compliance with regulation, and contribution to overall economic objectives (e.g. employment, growth).

This divergence explains why a business can be judged a "success" by one stakeholder group and a "failure" by another — for example, a business that cuts jobs to boost short-term profit may be viewed positively by shareholders but negatively by employees and the local community.

**Common misconception:** students frequently assume "profit" is the single, universal measure of success. In reality, a business can achieve high sales revenue but low or negative profit (if costs are excessive), and non-financial measures such as customer satisfaction or ethical reputation often directly determine long-run financial performance — the two are linked, not separate.

**Exam pitfall:** when asked to evaluate whether profit is the best measure of success, students should not simply list alternative measures — they must directly compare the strengths and limitations of profit against at least one alternative measure, applied to the context given in the question, before reaching a justified conclusion.

## Diagrams Required

**Diagram 1 — Type:** Stakeholder wheel/matrix diagram
**Purpose:** To visually show how different stakeholder groups prioritise different measures of success around a single central business.
**Content spec:** A circular diagram with "The Business" at the centre. Radiating segments, one per stakeholder group: "Shareholders/Owners", "Employees", "Customers", "Suppliers", "Local Community/Society", "Government". In each segment, list that stakeholder's key measure(s) of success (e.g. Shareholders: "Profit, ROI, share price"; Employees: "Pay, job security, working conditions"). Use distinct colour-coding per segment.

**Diagram 2 — Type:** Two-column comparison table
**Purpose:** To organise financial versus non-financial measures of success clearly for revision and to support the AO4 evaluation question on whether profit is the best measure.
**Content spec:** Columns headed "Financial (Quantitative) Measures" and "Non-Financial (Qualitative) Measures". Rows list paired measures with a one-line description each: "Profit / profitability" vs "Customer satisfaction"; "Sales revenue" vs "Employee satisfaction/staff turnover"; "Market share" vs "Brand reputation"; "Return on investment" vs "Achievement of ethical/CSR objectives". Add a final row "Strength" and "Limitation" underneath the table summarising that financial measures are precise/comparable but backward-looking, while non-financial measures capture long-term drivers of success but are harder to measure objectively.

**Diagram 3 — Type:** Timeline/lifecycle diagram
**Purpose:** To show how a business's priority objective (and therefore its primary measure of success) typically shifts as it grows and matures.
**Content spec:** A horizontal timeline with four labelled stages: "Start-up" → "Growth" → "Maturity" → "Established/Large business". Under each stage, state the typically dominant objective/measure: Start-up = "Survival (cash flow, staying in business)"; Growth = "Market share and sales growth"; Maturity = "Profit and profitability"; Established/Large = "Shareholder value, CSR/ethical performance, and sustained profitability". Include a note that this progression is a general tendency, not a fixed rule — some businesses prioritise ethical objectives from the very start.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Patagonia, outdoor clothing and equipment company, United States.
**Situation:** Patagonia has publicly built its brand and corporate strategy around environmental and ethical objectives, including significant commitments to sustainable sourcing, repair-and-reuse programmes for its products, and directing profits toward environmental causes (the company's founder transferred ownership of the company in 2022 so that profits not reinvested in the business would go toward fighting climate change). Patagonia is frequently cited as an example of a company that measures and publicises success using non-financial, ethical/environmental criteria alongside financial performance.
**Link to theory:** Direct illustration of a business explicitly prioritising ethical/social objectives and non-financial measures of success, even where this may affect short-term profit maximisation.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate whether prioritising ethical and environmental objectives, as Patagonia has done, is likely to help or harm the company's long-term financial success."

**Company/context:** Safaricom, telecommunications company, Kenya.
**Situation:** Safaricom, East Africa's leading telecommunications provider and operator of the widely used M-Pesa mobile money service, is judged as successful by different stakeholders in different ways: shareholders and analysts focus on its profitability and market share in Kenya's telecoms sector, while customers and development-focused observers often highlight M-Pesa's role in extending financial services access to millions of previously unbanked Kenyans as a measure of the company's social impact and success.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a single large business can be simultaneously evaluated using financial measures (profit, market share) and non-financial/social measures (financial inclusion impact), and how these can diverge or align.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse whether market share or social impact (such as financial inclusion through M-Pesa) is a more meaningful measure of Safaricom's success."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business, especially a new start-up, typically measures success in the short term primarily through **survival** and **cash flow** — simply staying in business and being able to pay bills and wages is a major achievement — and non-financial measures such as building a loyal local customer base can matter as much as profit in the early years. A large, established multinational typically has the resources and market position to pursue a wider range of measures simultaneously, including sophisticated financial ratios, market share statistics, shareholder value, and formal CSR/ethical performance reporting, often published in detail in annual reports to satisfy the expectations of a broad range of stakeholders including institutional investors.
