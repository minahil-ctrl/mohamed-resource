## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)

Nearly every household name — Apple, Toyota, Nestlé, Samsung — is a limited company, and most of the largest are public limited companies whose shares anyone can buy on a stock exchange. Understanding how incorporation works, and why a company might choose to "go public," explains how businesses raise the vast sums of capital needed to become global multinationals, and why decisions at companies like these are so heavily influenced by shareholders around the world.

## Core Content

### Incorporation and Limited Liability

A **limited company** is legally distinct from its owners: it has its own **separate legal identity**, meaning it can own property, enter contracts, sue, and be sued in its own name — completely separate from the personal identities of its shareholders or directors. This is fundamentally different from a sole trader or ordinary partnership (Chapter 03), where the business and the owner(s) are legally the same entity.

Because a company is a separate legal person, its owners (**shareholders**) benefit from **limited liability**: if the company fails and cannot pay its debts, shareholders can only lose the amount of money they originally invested in shares — their personal assets (homes, savings, cars) are protected. This is the single most important reason entrepreneurs choose to incorporate as their business grows and risk increases, since unlimited liability (as seen with sole traders and partnerships) becomes an unacceptable personal risk once a business handles large contracts, debts, or potential legal claims.

To form a limited company, founders must complete legal registration processes (varying by country) which typically include filing documents such as a **Memorandum of Association** (declaring intent to form the company) and **Articles of Association** (the internal rulebook governing how the company is run, including how directors are appointed and how decisions are made).

### Private Limited Companies (Ltd)

A **private limited company** (denoted "Ltd" in many countries) sells shares only to a restricted, invited group — typically family, friends, employees, or private investors — and shares cannot be bought or sold on a public stock exchange. Existing shareholders often must approve any transfer of shares to a new owner.

**Advantages of a private limited company:**
- Limited liability protects shareholders' personal assets.
- Can raise more capital than a sole trader or partnership by selling shares to a wider (though still restricted) group of investors.
- Retains more control over who owns the company, since shares cannot be bought by the public or hostile outside investors without existing shareholders' agreement.
- Continuity — the company continues to exist as a legal entity even if an individual shareholder dies or sells their shares.

**Disadvantages of a private limited company:**
- More expensive and complex to set up than a sole trader or partnership, involving legal registration and ongoing administrative/reporting requirements.
- Cannot raise capital as extensively as a public limited company, since shares cannot be sold to the general public.
- Must usually publish annual accounts, reducing privacy compared to a sole trader.
- Original owners may lose some control as more shares are issued to new private investors.

### Public Limited Companies (PLC)

A **public limited company** can sell shares to any member of the public through a **stock exchange**. Becoming a PLC typically happens through a **flotation** (also called an **Initial Public Offering**, or IPO) — the first sale of a private company's shares to the public, converting it from a private to a public limited company. Flotation is usually pursued to raise very large amounts of capital for expansion, but it comes at a significant cost of control and scrutiny.

**Advantages of becoming a PLC:**
- Access to substantially larger amounts of capital by selling shares to potentially millions of investors, enabling large-scale growth, acquisitions, or international expansion.
- Increased public profile and prestige, which can improve brand reputation and make it easier to attract high-calibre staff or business partners.
- Shares can be used as a form of payment in takeovers/mergers.
- Existing shareholders can more easily sell their shares (greater "liquidity") since there is an active public market for them.

**Disadvantages of becoming a PLC:**
- Original owners/founders risk losing control, since enough shares could be bought by outside investors to gain a controlling stake, potentially even leading to a hostile takeover.
- Much greater legal, regulatory, and reporting requirements (e.g. publishing detailed financial statements, complying with stock exchange rules), increasing cost and reducing privacy.
- Pressure from shareholders and financial markets for strong short-term profit performance (e.g. quarterly results) can push management towards short-termist decisions rather than long-term strategy.
- The company's share price is exposed to market sentiment and speculation, which can be volatile and outside management's control.

A common exam pitfall is assuming that "PLC" automatically means a company is huge, or that all large companies are PLCs — some very large, well-known companies remain privately owned (for example, many family-owned multinationals), while some small companies register as public limited companies without ever actually floating shares on a stock exchange. Precision in definitions matters for AO1 marks.

### Multinational Companies (MNCs)

A **multinational company** is a business that owns or controls production or service facilities in more than one country, though its headquarters is usually based in a single "home" country. Multinationals are typically (though not exclusively) large public limited companies, since substantial capital is required to establish and manage international operations.

**Reasons businesses become multinational:**
- **Access to new markets** — reaching customers and revenue beyond the home country.
- **Lower production costs** — locating factories in countries with cheaper labour, land, or raw materials.
- **Avoiding trade barriers** — producing inside a foreign market to avoid import tariffs or quotas.
- **Access to raw materials** — operating directly in countries where key resources are located.
- **Spreading risk** — reducing dependence on the economic conditions of any single country.

**Advantages of multinationals for the host country:**
- Job creation and reduced unemployment.
- Transfer of technology, skills, and management expertise to the local workforce.
- Increased tax revenue for the host government.
- Improved infrastructure, sometimes built or funded to support the MNC's operations.

**Disadvantages of multinationals for the host country:**
- Profits are often repatriated (sent back) to the home country rather than being reinvested locally.
- Risk of exploiting lower regulatory standards (e.g. weaker labour or environmental laws) compared to the home country.
- Local small businesses may struggle to compete with the scale and market power of the MNC.
- Host country can become economically dependent on the MNC, with job losses widespread if the company relocates elsewhere for lower costs.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** IKEA, home furnishings retailer, originally founded in Sweden, now a global multinational headquartered (via its holding structure) with operations across Europe, Asia, and the Americas.
**Situation:** IKEA has grown from a single Swedish mail-order furniture business into one of the world's largest furniture retailers, with hundreds of stores across dozens of countries, sourcing components and materials globally and adapting store formats to local markets while maintaining a globally standardised flat-pack, self-assembly business model.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the growth of a company into a full multinational operation, including the advantages of accessing new international markets and the operational complexity of coordinating manufacturing and retail across many host countries.
**Discussion question:** What are the main benefits and risks for a host country when a large multinational retailer like IKEA opens stores and sourcing operations there?

**Company/context:** Alibaba Group, e-commerce and technology conglomerate, China.
**Situation:** Alibaba was founded as a private company in 1999 and grew rapidly in the Chinese domestic market before conducting one of the largest Initial Public Offerings (IPOs) in history when it listed on the New York Stock Exchange in 2014, raising billions of dollars in capital to fund further global expansion.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the process and purpose of flotation (IPO) — converting from a private to a public limited company specifically to raise very large amounts of capital for growth — and the resulting exposure to public shareholders and stock market scrutiny.
**Discussion question:** What might Alibaba's founders and original private investors have had to give up in terms of control by choosing to float the company on a public stock exchange, and was it worth the trade-off?

## Large vs Small Business Application

The distinction between private and public limited companies is itself a distinction largely relevant to medium and large businesses, since the cost and complexity of incorporation (and especially of a stock market flotation) is rarely justified for a very small enterprise. A small business is far more likely to remain a sole trader, partnership, or at most a small private limited company with a handful of shareholders, prioritising simplicity and retained control. A large business, particularly one pursuing multinational expansion, typically needs the vastly greater capital-raising capacity of PLC status to fund overseas factories, acquisitions, and marketing on a global scale — but must accept, in exchange, far greater regulatory scrutiny, loss of founder control, and pressure from shareholders for short-term financial performance.
