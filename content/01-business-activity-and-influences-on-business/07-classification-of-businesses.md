## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Primary sector | Economic activity involving the extraction of raw materials directly from the earth or sea | Farming, fishing, mining, forestry |
| Secondary sector | Economic activity involving the manufacture or processing of raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods | Car manufacturing, food processing, construction |
| Tertiary sector | Economic activity involving the provision of services to consumers and other businesses | Retailing, banking, education, healthcare, tourism |
| Quaternary sector | A sub-division of the tertiary sector covering knowledge-based and information services | Software development, research and development, consultancy |
| Chain of production | The sequence of stages a raw material passes through, from extraction to the final consumer | Cotton (primary) → fabric (secondary) → clothing retail (tertiary) |
| Deindustrialisation | The decline in the relative importance of the secondary (manufacturing) sector within an economy | Manufacturing's declining share of GDP in many developed economies over recent decades |
| Sector shift/structural change | Long-term movement of an economy's resources and employment from one sector to another (typically primary → secondary → tertiary) | An economy moving from mainly agricultural to mainly service-based |
| Business size | Classification of firms by scale, commonly measured using number of employees, turnover (sales revenue), capital employed, or market share | A business with over 250 employees is classified as large in many frameworks |
| Micro business | A very small business, typically with fewer than 10 employees | A one-person graphic design freelance business |
| SME (Small and Medium-sized Enterprise) | A business generally classified as having fewer than 250 employees (definitions vary by country) | A local independent bakery chain with 40 employees |
| Multinational company (MNC) | A business that owns or controls production or service operations in more than one country | A car manufacturer with factories on multiple continents |
| Private sector | Businesses owned and operated by private individuals or shareholders, aiming primarily for profit | A privately owned retail chain |
| Public sector | Organisations owned and controlled by the government, providing services often funded through taxation | A state-run hospital or public transport authority |
| Privatisation | The transfer of ownership of a business or industry from the public (government) sector to the private sector | A state-owned telecommunications company sold to private shareholders |
| Nationalisation | The transfer of ownership of a business or industry from the private sector to government/public ownership | A government taking over a failing bank during a financial crisis |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Every product a consumer buys — a phone, a loaf of bread, a haircut — has travelled through a chain of businesses operating in different sectors of the economy, from the miner who extracted the raw metal ore to the retailer who sold the finished device. Understanding how businesses are classified helps explain why some economies are dominated by farming and mining while others are driven by finance, technology, and tourism — and why a country's economic structure shapes the jobs, incomes, and opportunities available to its population.

## Core Content

### Classification by Sector of Economic Activity
Economists and business analysts classify all economic activity into sectors based on where a business sits in the **chain of production** — the journey a product takes from raw material to finished good in the hands of a consumer.

The **primary sector** covers businesses that extract or harvest raw materials directly from natural resources: agriculture, fishing, forestry, oil and gas extraction, and mining. These businesses provide the essential inputs that later sectors transform into usable products. Primary sector activity tends to dominate the economies of many lower-income and developing countries, where a large share of the workforce is engaged in farming or resource extraction.

The **secondary sector** covers businesses that take raw materials from the primary sector and manufacture, process, construct, or assemble them into semi-finished or finished goods. Examples include car manufacturing, steel production, textile manufacturing, food processing, and construction. Historically, secondary sector growth (industrialisation) has been a major driver of rising living standards as countries move from agriculture-based to manufacturing-based economies.

The **tertiary sector** covers businesses that provide services rather than physical goods, to both consumers and other businesses. This includes retailing, banking and financial services, education, healthcare, transport, tourism, and hospitality. In most developed economies, the tertiary sector is now by far the largest in terms of both output (GDP) and employment. Some specifications and analysts further distinguish a **quaternary sector** within tertiary activity, covering knowledge-intensive and information-based services such as software development, scientific research, and consultancy — though for IGCSE purposes the three-sector model (primary/secondary/tertiary) is the core requirement.

**Worked example — tracing a chain of production:** Consider a wooden dining table. A forestry company fells trees (primary sector). A timber processing and furniture manufacturing company cuts, shapes, and assembles the wood into a finished table (secondary sector). A furniture retailer then sells the table to the final consumer, and a delivery/logistics company transports it to their home (tertiary sector — both retail and logistics are service activities). This example shows how a single product typically passes through all three sectors before reaching the end user, and how sector classification depends on the specific activity a business performs, not the product itself.

**Common misconception:** Students sometimes assume a whole industry belongs entirely to one sector — for example, assuming "food" is only secondary sector. In reality, the food industry spans all three sectors: farming (primary), food processing/manufacturing (secondary), and restaurants/supermarkets (tertiary). Always classify based on the *specific activity described*, not the general product category.

### Sector Shift and Structural Change
Economies do not remain static in their sector balance — they undergo **structural change** (sometimes called sector shift) over time, typically progressing from primary-sector dominance, through secondary-sector dominance during industrialisation, toward tertiary-sector dominance as economies mature and incomes rise. This pattern has been observed historically across many now-developed economies. As incomes rise, consumer demand shifts toward services (education, healthcare, leisure, financial services), automation and overseas competition reduce manufacturing employment domestically, and technology creates entirely new service-based industries.

**Deindustrialisation** refers specifically to the declining relative importance of the secondary (manufacturing) sector within an economy — often measured by manufacturing's shrinking share of total output or employment. Causes include the relocation of manufacturing to lower-cost countries (linking to Chapter 09 on globalisation), automation reducing the number of workers needed in factories, and a genuine shift in consumer and business spending toward services. Deindustrialisation can have significant regional consequences, including structural unemployment in areas historically dependent on manufacturing (relevant when studying government influence on business, covered elsewhere in the specification).

**Exam tip:** When asked to analyse causes of a sector shift, always identify at least two distinct causes (e.g. rising incomes changing consumer demand, *and* technological change/automation reducing labour needs) rather than repeating the same point in different words.

### Classification by Business Size
Beyond sector, businesses are also classified by **size**, which affects the resources available to them, their market power, and the strategies open to them. Common measures of size include:
- **Number of employees** — the most commonly used and easiest to compare measure. A **micro business** typically has fewer than 10 employees; small businesses often up to 50; medium-sized businesses up to around 250; and large businesses more than 250 (exact thresholds vary between countries and awarding bodies, so descriptive comparison matters more than memorising a single cut-off).
- **Turnover (sales revenue)** — total value of sales made over a period, useful for comparing businesses within similar industries, though it does not account for profitability.
- **Capital employed** — the total value of the finance/assets invested in the business, indicating the scale of investment.
- **Market share** — the proportion of total industry sales captured by one firm, indicating competitive dominance regardless of employee count (e.g. a highly automated factory could have few employees but a huge market share).

No single measure is perfect, which is why examiners often expect students to recognise the **limitations of size classification**: a capital-intensive business (e.g. an oil refinery) may employ relatively few staff but generate enormous turnover and hold significant market power, making "number of employees" alone a misleading measure of its true economic significance. Comparing size across very different industries (e.g. a technology start-up vs a steel manufacturer) using the same measure can also produce misleading conclusions, since capital intensity and typical firm size vary hugely by industry.

An **SME** (Small and Medium-sized Enterprise) is a widely used umbrella classification for smaller firms, important because many governments design specific tax incentives, grants, and support schemes targeted at SMEs to encourage entrepreneurship and employment. A business that grows large enough to own or control operations in more than one country becomes classified as a **multinational company (MNC)** — this size/scope classification is explored fully in Chapter 10.

### Classification by Sector of Ownership: Private vs Public Sector
A separate classification distinguishes businesses by who owns and controls them. The **private sector** consists of businesses owned by private individuals, families, or shareholders, generally operating with the primary objective of making a profit (this includes all the ownership forms covered in Chapter 06: sole traders, partnerships, private and public limited companies). The **public sector** consists of organisations owned and controlled by government on behalf of citizens, often providing essential services — healthcare, education, defence, infrastructure — that may not otherwise be provided efficiently or fairly by the free market, and which are typically funded through taxation rather than solely through sales revenue.

Businesses can move between these two ownership sectors. **Privatisation** is the sale of a state-owned business or industry into private ownership, often intended to improve efficiency through competition and profit incentives, raise revenue for government, and reduce the burden on public finances. **Nationalisation** is the reverse process — government taking a private business into public ownership — often used to protect strategically important industries, jobs, or services, particularly during economic crises or where a private firm is failing but provides an essential service. Both processes generate ongoing debate about the trade-off between profit-driven efficiency and universal, affordable access to essential services.

**Cross-reference:** The private/public ownership distinction connects closely to Chapter 06 (Forms of Ownership) and to later study of government influence on business decision-making.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Saudi Aramco, oil and gas extraction and processing, Saudi Arabia.
**Situation:** Saudi Aramco is one of the world's largest companies by revenue, engaged primarily in the extraction (primary sector) and refining/processing (secondary sector) of crude oil and natural gas, and is majority owned by the Saudi Arabian government, with a minority stake publicly traded following its landmark stock market listing.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates both sector classification (a business spanning primary extraction and secondary processing activity) and ownership classification (a public sector/state-owned enterprise with a private-sector minority shareholding), plus illustrates the size and economic significance of a hugely capital-intensive but relatively lower-employee-count business.
**Discussion question:** Why might "number of employees" be a misleading way to measure the size and economic importance of a business like Saudi Aramco?

**Company/context:** M-Pesa (operated by Safaricom), mobile money and financial services, Kenya.
**Situation:** M-Pesa is a mobile-phone-based money transfer and financial services provider launched in Kenya, allowing millions of users — including those without access to traditional bank branches — to send money, pay bills, and access basic financial services via their mobile phones, representing rapid growth of the tertiary (financial services) sector in an emerging economy.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates tertiary sector growth and innovation in a developing economy, showing that structural change toward services is not limited to already-developed economies, and demonstrating how a service-sector business can transform economic activity even where secondary sector industrialisation remains limited.
**Discussion question:** To what extent does the growth of mobile money services like M-Pesa challenge the traditional idea that economies must industrialise (grow their secondary sector) before their tertiary sector can flourish?

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business is often confined to a single sector and a single, local stage of the chain of production — for example, a small bakery purely operating in the tertiary sector, buying in already-processed flour rather than growing wheat or milling it. A large or multinational business, by contrast, may operate across multiple sectors simultaneously through vertical integration — for instance, an oil multinational that extracts crude oil (primary), refines it into fuel (secondary), and operates its own branded petrol stations (tertiary) — giving it greater control over the entire chain of production and reducing dependence on external suppliers at each stage.
