# Chapter 13: External Factors

**Unit:** Unit 1 — Business Activity and Influences on Business
**Specification reference:** 4BS1 Topic 1.5 — External Influences on Business Activity
**Applies to:** Both (Paper 1 and Paper 2)
**Tracker code:** 1.13

## Learning Objectives
- Define external factors (external environment) and distinguish them from internal factors.
- Identify the main categories of external factor affecting business using a PEST/PESTLE framework.
- Explain how political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental (ethical) factors can each affect a business.
- Apply PEST/PESTLE analysis to a given business scenario to identify relevant external factors.
- Analyse how a specific external change affects a named business's costs, revenue, or strategy.
- Evaluate the extent to which a business can control or respond to external factors compared with internal factors.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| External factors | Influences on a business that originate outside the business and are largely beyond its control | A change in interest rates set by the central bank |
| Internal factors | Influences on a business that originate from within, and are largely within the business's control | A decision to change the marketing budget |
| PEST(LE) analysis | A framework for analysing external factors under the headings Political, Economic, Social, Technological (and Legal, Environmental) | Using PESTLE to assess the risks of entering a new country |
| Political factors | Government actions, policies, and political stability that affect business, including regulation and trade policy | A change in government trade policy affecting exporters |
| Economic factors | Macroeconomic conditions such as interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, and economic growth | A recession reducing consumer spending |
| Social factors | Changes in population, culture, lifestyle, and consumer tastes that affect demand | An ageing population increasing demand for healthcare services |
| Technological factors | Developments in technology that affect how businesses produce, market, or sell products | The growth of e-commerce changing retail business models |
| Legal factors | Laws and regulations that businesses must comply with | Minimum wage legislation raising labour costs |
| Environmental/ethical factors | Concerns about sustainability, environmental impact, and ethical business conduct affecting stakeholder expectations | Consumer pressure for reduced plastic packaging |
| Stakeholder | Any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, a business's activities | Employees, shareholders, suppliers, and the local community |
| Competitive environment | The nature and intensity of competition a business faces in its market | A highly competitive market with many rival firms |
| Consumer trends | Shifts in the tastes, preferences, and behaviours of customers over time | Rising demand for plant-based food products |
| Demography/demographic change | Changes in the size, structure, or composition of a population | A falling birth rate reducing demand for baby products |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
No business operates in a vacuum. A café owner cannot control a rise in the minimum wage, a fashion retailer cannot control a shift in social attitudes towards fast fashion, and a car manufacturer cannot control new emissions regulations — yet all of these external forces can make or break a business. The most successful companies are not those that avoid external change, but those that anticipate it and adapt fastest, as seen when businesses that had already invested in online sales infrastructure were far better placed to survive sudden shifts in consumer shopping behaviour.

## Core Content

### What Are External Factors?
**External factors** are influences on a business that arise from outside the organisation and that the business itself has little or no direct control over, in contrast to **internal factors** (such as staffing decisions, production methods, or marketing strategy) which management can directly change. Because external factors are largely uncontrollable, successful businesses focus not on preventing them but on **monitoring, anticipating, and adapting** to them. The standard tool for systematically identifying and analysing external factors is **PEST analysis** (sometimes extended to **PESTLE** to separate out Legal and Environmental factors explicitly).

### Political Factors
Political factors include the actions, stability, and policy decisions of governments, both domestic and international. These include:
- **Government trade policy** — tariffs, quotas, and trade agreements (see Chapter 11), which affect firms that import or export.
- **Political stability** — businesses are generally reluctant to invest in countries experiencing political instability, conflict, or unpredictable policy changes, as this increases risk.
- **Government regulation of industries** — some industries (e.g. pharmaceuticals, banking, energy) face close government oversight and licensing requirements.
- **Changes in government** — a new government may reverse the policies of its predecessor (e.g. changing tax rates, subsidies, or regulatory approaches), creating uncertainty for long-term business planning.

### Economic Factors
Economic factors cover the broader macroeconomic conditions in which a business operates, closely linked to Chapter 12's discussion of government policy:
- **Interest rates** — affect the cost of borrowing and consumer spending power.
- **Inflation** — affects costs (e.g. wages, raw materials) and the real value of revenue.
- **Exchange rates** — affect the price competitiveness of exports and the cost of imports (see Chapter 11).
- **Economic growth or recession** — determines the overall level of consumer and business spending in the economy; a recession typically reduces demand for most goods and services (particularly non-essential/luxury items), while growth increases it.
- **Unemployment levels** — high unemployment can reduce overall consumer spending power but may also increase the availability and lower the cost of labour for businesses hiring.

### Social Factors
Social factors relate to changes in the population and in society's attitudes, values, and lifestyles:
- **Demographic change** — shifts in population size, age structure, or geographic distribution alter demand patterns. An ageing population in many developed economies has increased demand for healthcare, retirement housing, and related services, while a young, growing population in many developing economies increases demand for education, housing, and consumer goods.
- **Changing tastes and lifestyles** — for example, growing health-consciousness has increased demand for organic and low-sugar products; increased awareness of sustainability has shifted demand toward eco-friendly products and services.
- **Cultural and social attitudes** — societal attitudes toward issues such as gender roles, work-life balance, or ethical consumption can shift consumer expectations and workplace practices.

### Technological Factors
Technological change can create opportunities and threats simultaneously:
- **New production technology** can reduce costs, improve quality, and enable mass customisation (e.g. automation and robotics in manufacturing).
- **E-commerce and digital marketing** have transformed how many businesses reach and sell to customers, reducing the need for physical retail space for some firms while threatening those slow to adapt.
- **Communication technology** enables remote working, global supply chain coordination, and instant customer feedback.
- **Risk of obsolescence** — businesses that fail to adopt new technology risk losing competitiveness to rivals who do.

### Legal Factors
Legal factors are the laws and regulations businesses must comply with, which can significantly affect costs and operating practices:
- **Employment law** — e.g. minimum wage legislation, working time regulations, and health and safety law, which raise labour costs but protect worker welfare.
- **Consumer protection law** — regulations ensuring products are safe and fairly described, protecting consumers but potentially raising compliance costs for businesses.
- **Competition law** — designed to prevent monopolistic or anti-competitive behaviour, protecting smaller firms and consumers from exploitation by dominant firms.
- **Environmental law** — regulations limiting pollution or waste, which can raise production costs but reduce negative externalities.

### Environmental and Ethical Factors
Increasingly treated as a distinct category (the "E" in PESTLE), environmental and ethical factors include:
- **Sustainability expectations** — consumers, investors, and regulators increasingly expect businesses to reduce their environmental footprint (e.g. carbon emissions, plastic waste, water use).
- **Ethical sourcing and labour practices** — growing stakeholder scrutiny of supply chains, particularly regarding labour conditions in supplier factories.
- **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** — businesses face growing pressure to demonstrate positive social and environmental impact beyond profit-making, which can affect brand reputation and customer loyalty.

**Common misconception:** students often list PEST categories without applying them to the specific business or scenario given in the question. A high-scoring answer must explain the mechanism by which a named external factor affects a named business's costs, revenue, or decisions — not simply state the factor exists.

**Exam pitfall:** confusing an internal factor with an external one (e.g. describing a business's own decision to raise prices as an "external factor" is incorrect — only forces beyond the business's control count as external).

### Using PEST/PESTLE Analysis
PEST/PESTLE analysis is used by businesses to systematically scan their external environment, typically as part of strategic planning before making a major decision (e.g. entering a new market, launching a new product, or making a significant investment). Its value lies in ensuring that decision-makers do not focus solely on internal factors (such as cost and capability) while overlooking external risks and opportunities. However, PEST analysis has limitations: it is a snapshot that can quickly become outdated as external conditions change, it does not indicate the relative importance of different factors, and it does not by itself generate a strategy — it is a starting point for analysis, not a decision-making tool in itself.

## Diagrams Required

**Diagram 1 — Type:** PEST(LE) wheel/matrix diagram
**Purpose:** To give students a single, memorable visual framework for organising all types of external factor.
**Content spec:** A circular diagram (wheel) with the business placed at the centre labelled "The Business". Six segments radiating outward, each labelled with one PESTLE category: "Political", "Economic", "Social", "Technological", "Legal", "Environmental". In each segment, list 2–3 example factors (e.g. under "Political": "Trade policy", "Political stability", "Regulation"). Use distinct colours per segment for visual memorability.

**Diagram 2 — Type:** Two-column table (internal vs external factors)
**Purpose:** To reinforce the crucial distinction between internal and external factors, since this is a common source of exam errors.
**Content spec:** Columns headed "Internal Factors (within the business's control)" and "External Factors (outside the business's control)". Rows populated with paired contrasting examples, e.g. "Marketing budget decision" vs "Change in consumer tastes"; "Choice of production method" vs "New technology available in the market"; "Pricing strategy" vs "Government tax on the product"; "Staff training programme" vs "Minimum wage legislation".

**Diagram 3 — Type:** Flowchart / cause-and-effect chain
**Purpose:** To model, step by step, how a single external factor change flows through to affect a business, reinforcing the analytical skill needed for AO3 exam questions.
**Content spec:** Sequential boxes: Box 1 "External factor changes (e.g. new environmental regulation banning single-use plastic packaging)" → Box 2 "Direct effect on the business (e.g. must find alternative packaging supplier)" → Box 3 "Effect on costs and/or operations (e.g. higher packaging costs)" → Box 4 "Effect on overall business performance (e.g. lower profit margin, or opportunity to market as more sustainable and attract new customers)". Include a branching arrow at Box 4 to show that the same external factor can create both a threat and an opportunity, depending on the business's response.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Unilever, consumer goods multinational, United Kingdom/Netherlands (global operations).
**Situation:** Unilever, which owns brands spanning food, personal care, and home care products across many countries, has publicly committed to sustainability targets (such as reducing plastic packaging and greenhouse gas emissions) partly in response to growing consumer and investor expectations around environmental responsibility. The company has faced both opportunities (marketing sustainable product lines to environmentally conscious consumers) and cost pressures (redesigning packaging and supply chains) as a result of these environmental/social external factors.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how Social and Environmental factors under PESTLE directly shape a large multinational's product strategy, costs, and brand positioning.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate whether responding to environmental and social pressures is primarily a cost or an opportunity for a large multinational company like Unilever."

**Company/context:** Jumia Technologies, e-commerce platform, operating across multiple African countries (headquartered with operations centred on Nigeria and other African markets).
**Situation:** Jumia's business model, often described as one of Africa's leading e-commerce platforms, has been shaped by a combination of external factors including rapid growth in smartphone and internet access (Technological), a young and growing urban population in many of its markets (Social/Demographic), variable regulatory environments across the different countries it operates in (Political/Legal), and currency volatility affecting costs and reported revenues (Economic).
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how multiple PESTLE categories interact simultaneously to shape strategy for a business operating across several emerging markets at once.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse which external factor (Political, Economic, Social, or Technological) is likely to have the greatest influence on the future growth of an e-commerce business operating across multiple African countries."

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small, local business (such as an independent café) is often more vulnerable to certain external factors, such as a local change in business rates or a national minimum wage rise, because it has limited financial reserves and cannot easily absorb sudden cost increases or spread the impact across a diversified range of products or markets. A large multinational, by contrast, can often better withstand a single external shock in one market (e.g. a political crisis or regulatory change in one country) because it operates across many countries and can shift resources or focus accordingly — although its sheer scale also means it faces a wider and more complex range of external factors simultaneously (different laws, cultures, and economic conditions in every country it operates in), requiring dedicated teams to monitor and respond to them.
