## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
A pair of trainers might be designed in the United States, have its rubber sourced from Southeast Asia, its fabric woven in South Asia, be assembled in Vietnam, and finally be sold in a shop in Nairobi or London. This everyday product embodies globalisation — the deepening web of trade, investment, and communication that connects businesses and consumers across the planet. Understanding globalisation explains why the same brands appear on nearly every continent, why job markets can be affected by decisions made thousands of miles away, and why a factory closure or trade dispute in one country can ripple through economies worldwide.

## Core Content

### Defining and Explaining Globalisation
**Globalisation** describes the growing economic, technological, political, and cultural interconnectedness of countries around the world. In a business context, it refers to the process by which firms increasingly operate, trade, invest, and compete across national borders rather than being confined to a single domestic market. Globalisation is not a single event but a long-term, ongoing trend, accelerating markedly over recent decades.

### Causes of Globalisation
Several interconnected forces have driven the acceleration of globalisation:

- **Improvements in transport:** Containerisation of shipping, larger and more efficient cargo ships, and cheaper, faster air freight have dramatically reduced the cost and time of moving goods internationally, making it economically viable to manufacture in one country and sell in another far away.
- **Improvements in communication technology:** The internet, email, video conferencing, and global logistics/tracking software allow businesses to coordinate complex international operations, supply chains, and customer relationships instantly and at low cost, something impossible before modern telecommunications.
- **Trade liberalisation and reduced trade barriers:** Many governments have progressively reduced **tariffs**, **quotas**, and other **trade barriers** through international agreements, encouraging cross-border trade and investment. International organisations promoting free trade have supported this trend over recent decades.
- **Growth of trading blocs:** Groups of countries forming **trading blocs** — agreeing to eliminate or reduce trade barriers between member states — have made it significantly easier and cheaper for businesses within the bloc to trade with each other, while sometimes maintaining tariffs against non-member countries. The European Union is a well-known example, allowing free movement of goods, services, and often labour and capital between member states.
- **Growth of multinational companies:** As firms grow large enough to establish operations directly in multiple countries (explored fully in Chapter 10), they further deepen global economic integration through cross-border investment, employment, and supply chains.
- **Increased consumer demand for international products:** Rising incomes and awareness of international brands (partly driven by global media and social media) have increased demand for imported and international products in many markets.

### Impacts of Globalisation on Businesses
Globalisation creates both significant opportunities and significant risks for businesses:

**Advantages for businesses:**
- **Access to new/larger markets:** Businesses can sell to a vastly larger global customer base rather than being limited to their domestic market, supporting sales growth beyond what a saturated home market could offer.
- **Access to cheaper resources and labour:** Firms can source raw materials, components, or labour from wherever they are cheapest globally, reducing costs — this underpins the widespread practice of **outsourcing** and **offshoring** production or services to lower-cost countries.
- **Economies of scale:** Selling into global markets allows businesses to produce at a much larger scale, spreading fixed costs over more units and reducing average cost per unit (cross-reference: economies of scale, covered in operations/finance topics).
- **Access to global supply chains:** Businesses can build **global supply chains**, sourcing different components or stages of production from whichever country offers the best combination of cost, quality, and expertise.

**Disadvantages/risks for businesses:**
- **Increased competition:** Globalisation exposes domestic businesses to competition from foreign firms entering their home market, which can be especially damaging for smaller businesses unable to match the prices or marketing budgets of large international competitors.
- **Exchange rate risk:** Businesses trading or investing internationally are exposed to fluctuations in currency values, which can unpredictably affect costs and revenues when converted back to the home currency.
- **Complexity of managing international operations:** Coordinating supply chains, staff, and regulations across multiple countries with different laws, languages, and business cultures adds significant management complexity and risk (e.g. supply chain disruption if one link in a distant country fails).
- **Reputational and ethical risk:** Businesses sourcing from overseas suppliers face scrutiny over labour standards, environmental practices, and working conditions in their global supply chains, and any scandal can cause significant reputational damage.
- **Vulnerability to protectionism:** Governments can respond to globalisation with **protectionist** measures (tariffs, quotas) that disrupt established international supply chains and raise costs unexpectedly.

### Impacts of Globalisation on Consumers and Workers
Consumers generally benefit from globalisation through greater choice of products, often lower prices (due to competition and access to lower-cost production), and access to innovations developed anywhere in the world. However, critics point to **cultural convergence** — the concern that increased global trade and the dominance of large international brands can erode local businesses, traditional industries, and cultural diversity, as similar global chains and products displace local alternatives.

For workers, globalisation can create new employment opportunities, particularly in emerging markets, where multinational investment and outsourced production have created significant jobs and contributed to economic development. However, it can also lead to **structural unemployment** in higher-cost countries when firms relocate or offshore production to reduce costs (linking to deindustrialisation, covered in Chapter 07), and has raised ongoing concerns about labour conditions and pay in some outsourced or offshored production facilities in lower-regulation countries.

**Common misconception:** Students sometimes treat globalisation as an unambiguously positive or negative force. Strong exam answers recognise that its effects are genuinely mixed and vary depending on the stakeholder (business, consumer, worker) and the country (developed vs developing/emerging economy) being considered — this nuance is essential for AO3/AO4 marks.

### Trading Blocs and Trade Barriers in Detail
A **trading bloc** is formed when a group of countries agrees to reduce or eliminate trade barriers between themselves, sometimes while maintaining a common external tariff against non-members. Benefits of trading bloc membership for businesses include tariff-free (or reduced-tariff) access to a much larger combined market, simplified customs procedures between member states, and in some blocs, freer movement of capital and labour, supporting easier cross-border investment and recruitment. The trade-off is that businesses located outside a trading bloc may face reduced competitiveness when selling into it, due to tariffs that do not apply to businesses based within the bloc — this is one reason multinational companies sometimes choose to locate production facilities inside a major trading bloc specifically to gain tariff-free access to its market (cross-reference: Chapter 08, Decisions on Location, and Chapter 10, Multinational Companies).

Conversely, governments sometimes pursue **protectionist** policies — imposing tariffs or quotas — to shield domestic industries and jobs from foreign competition, or in response to political disputes. While this can protect domestic producers in the short term, it typically raises costs for domestic consumers (who pay more for protected goods or have less choice) and can provoke retaliatory trade barriers from other countries, disrupting global supply chains.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Zara (Inditex group), fast-fashion retail, Spain, with a global supply chain and international store network.
**Situation:** Zara operates a highly globalised supply chain and store network, sourcing fabric and manufacturing garments across multiple countries and regions to balance cost and speed, while selling through thousands of stores and online channels in countries across the world, allowing it to respond quickly to changing fashion trends globally.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a business leverages globalisation — international sourcing, global supply chains, and access to markets across many countries — to achieve both cost efficiency and rapid international market reach.
**Discussion question:** Discuss the risks Zara faces by relying on a global, multi-country supply chain rather than sourcing and manufacturing entirely within one country.

**Company/context:** Made.com/Fairphone-style ethical sourcing debates; alternatively, Nike, sportswear manufacturing and retail, United States, with production largely outsourced to factories in countries such as Vietnam and Indonesia.
**Situation:** Nike outsources the vast majority of its footwear and apparel manufacturing to independently owned factories located in lower-cost countries in Asia, while retaining design, marketing, and brand management in the United States; the company has faced public scrutiny in the past over labour and working conditions within its outsourced supply chain factories.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates outsourcing and offshoring as key mechanisms of globalisation, showing both the cost advantages this brings to a multinational business and the reputational/ethical risks associated with managing labour standards across a globalised, multi-country supply chain.
**Discussion question:** Evaluate whether the benefits of outsourcing production to lower-cost countries outweigh the reputational risks a global brand takes on by doing so.

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business is typically far less exposed to globalisation directly — it may serve only a local market and source supplies domestically — but is nonetheless affected indirectly, for example by facing new competition from international online retailers entering its local market, or by rising input costs if its domestic suppliers are themselves affected by global price changes. A large or multinational business, by contrast, actively drives and exploits globalisation as a deliberate strategy: sourcing raw materials and labour from wherever is most cost-effective worldwide, selling into dozens of international markets, and building complex global supply chains — gaining major cost and scale advantages, but also taking on significantly greater exposure to exchange rate risk, international regulation, and global supply chain disruption.
