## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Public corporation | A business owned and controlled by the state (government) on behalf of the public, usually operating in an industry considered essential to the whole economy. | A state-owned national railway operator. |
| Nationalisation | The process of transferring a business or industry from private ownership into state (government) ownership. | A government taking over a struggling privately owned bank during a financial crisis. |
| Privatisation | The process of transferring a business or industry from state (government) ownership into private ownership. | The sale of a state-owned telecommunications company's shares to private investors. |
| Public sector | The part of the economy owned, funded, and controlled by the government. | State schools, public hospitals, and nationalised utilities. |
| Private sector | The part of the economy owned and controlled by private individuals and businesses, operating to make a profit. | Privately owned shops, factories, and PLCs. |
| Natural monopoly | An industry where it is most efficient for a single large supplier to provide the entire market, typically because of very high fixed infrastructure costs. | A national electricity grid or water distribution network. |
| Public interest | The wellbeing and general welfare of the population as a whole, rather than the profit of a private owner. | Keeping a loss-making rural bus route running because communities depend on it. |
| Subsidy | Financial support given by the government to a business or industry, often to keep prices low or to sustain an unprofitable but socially necessary service. | Government funding topping up the losses of a state-owned rail network. |
| Deregulation | The reduction or removal of government rules and regulations controlling an industry, often carried out alongside privatisation. | Opening up a previously state-controlled energy market to competing private suppliers. |
| Strategic industry | An industry considered too important to national security, infrastructure, or the economy as a whole to be left entirely to private control. | Defence manufacturing or the national power grid. |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)

When a government decides who should run the postal service, the electricity grid, or the railways, it is making one of the most consequential choices in economics: should this industry be left to private, profit-seeking owners, or should the state own and run it in the name of the public? Countries answer this question differently, and many have switched answers over time — Britain nationalised its railways and coal mines after 1945 and then privatised most of them from the 1980s onward, while France still owns large stakes in energy and rail. Understanding public corporations explains one of the most debated and politically charged areas of business ownership, and sets up the case for and against privatisation that recurs throughout the course.

## Core Content

### What Is a Public Corporation?

A **public corporation** (also called a nationalised industry or state-owned enterprise) is a business that is owned and controlled by the government on behalf of the public, rather than by private shareholders. Unlike a private limited company or public limited company (Chapter 04), where shares are owned by private individuals or institutions seeking a financial return, a public corporation's "shareholder" is effectively the state, and its stated purpose is usually to serve the **public interest** rather than to maximise profit.

A common exam pitfall is confusing "public corporation" with "public limited company" simply because both contain the word "public." A public limited company (PLC) is a *private-sector* business whose shares are sold to members of the public on a stock exchange; a public corporation is a *public-sector* business owned outright by the government, and its shares (if any exist in a technical legal sense) are not traded at all. Precise use of this vocabulary is essential for AO1 marks.

Public corporations are typically established, or created through **nationalisation**, in industries that governments consider too important, too large-scale, or too essential to leave entirely to private, profit-driven control.

### Why Governments Own and Run Certain Industries

Governments choose public ownership for a number of overlapping economic and political reasons:

- **Natural monopolies.** Some industries — such as national electricity grids, water supply networks, or rail infrastructure — involve extremely high fixed costs (laying cables, pipes, or track) that make it inefficient or impractical to have several competing suppliers each building duplicate infrastructure. Economists call this a **natural monopoly**: it is most efficient for a single supplier to serve the whole market. Governments often argue that such a monopoly should be publicly owned and accountable to the public, rather than a privately owned monopoly free to exploit its market power by charging excessive prices.
- **Provision of essential/strategic services.** Some services — healthcare, postal delivery, national defence production, or public transport — are considered essential to everyone in society, regardless of ability to pay or commercial profitability. Governments may judge that leaving these to a purely profit-driven private market would mean some citizens, or some unprofitable regions, are left without access.
- **Protecting employment and strategic industries.** Governments sometimes nationalise a large employer, or a **strategic industry** central to national infrastructure or security (e.g. energy generation, steel, or defence), to prevent large-scale job losses or to keep control of assets deemed too important to be owned by foreign or purely private interests.
- **Rescuing failing but essential businesses.** During financial crises, governments have nationalised banks or other critical businesses that were at risk of collapse, judging that their failure would cause unacceptable damage to the wider economy (an example of this is explored in the case studies below).
- **Redistributing the benefits of profit.** Because a public corporation is owned by the state, any profit it makes theoretically benefits taxpayers/citizens as a whole (e.g. by reducing the need for tax rises or funding public services), rather than flowing to private shareholders.

### Advantages of Public Corporations

- **Services can be provided in the public interest rather than for profit,** meaning unprofitable but socially valuable services (e.g. a rural mail delivery route or a little-used railway line) can be maintained even if a private, profit-maximising firm would close them down.
- **Prevents exploitation of monopoly power.** Where an industry is a natural monopoly, public ownership can prevent a single private firm from charging excessively high prices, since there is no competition to discipline it.
- **Coordinated, large-scale, long-term investment** in national infrastructure (e.g. energy grids, rail networks) can be planned more easily by a single state-owned body than by several competing private firms each pursuing their own short-term shareholder interests.
- **Profits (where made) return to the government/taxpayer**, rather than being paid out to private shareholders, and losses can be covered through **subsidy** where the government judges the service essential.
- **Greater public accountability and democratic control**, at least in principle, since the industry answers to elected government rather than to private shareholders whose main interest is financial return.

### Disadvantages of Public Corporations

- **Lack of competition can reduce efficiency.** Without competing rivals threatening to take customers away, a public corporation may have less incentive to control costs, innovate, or improve customer service — a criticism often summarised as a lack of a **profit motive**.
- **Political interference.** Decisions may be influenced by short-term political considerations (e.g. avoiding unpopular price rises before an election) rather than sound long-term business or financial management.
- **Funded by taxpayers.** Persistent losses in a public corporation must ultimately be covered through government subsidy, funded by taxation, which represents an opportunity cost — the same money could have been spent on other public services (e.g. health or education) or returned to taxpayers.
- **Bureaucracy and slower decision-making.** Public corporations are often larger and more heavily regulated organisations, which can lead to slower, more bureaucratic decision-making compared with a leaner, competitively pressured private firm.
- **Limited access to private capital markets.** Unlike a PLC, a public corporation cannot easily raise additional capital for expansion by selling shares to private investors; it typically depends on government funding, which is itself constrained by the state's budget and other spending priorities.

### Privatisation: Moving from Public to Private Ownership

**Privatisation** is the process of transferring ownership of a business or industry from the state to private owners, typically by selling shares in the former public corporation to private investors (often through a stock market flotation, as covered in Chapter 04). Privatisation is frequently accompanied by **deregulation** — the removal or reduction of government rules restricting competition in the industry — to allow new private competitors to enter the market.

**Arguments in favour of privatisation:**
- Introducing a **profit motive** and, where possible, competition is argued to increase efficiency, cut costs, and improve customer service, since a privately owned business risks losing customers and profit if it performs poorly.
- Privatisation can raise significant one-off revenue for the government from the sale of shares, which can be used to reduce government debt or fund other spending.
- Removes the industry's day-to-day losses from the government's budget, ending the need for ongoing taxpayer-funded subsidy.
- Private ownership can bring in private investment and specialist management expertise that may accelerate innovation and modernisation.

**Arguments against privatisation:**
- A privatised natural monopoly may still exploit its market power in the absence of effective competition or regulation, potentially raising prices for consumers who have no realistic alternative supplier.
- Newly private owners may prioritise shareholder profit over unprofitable-but-essential services, which can lead to service cuts, price rises, or reduced investment in less profitable areas (e.g. closing an unprofitable rural route).
- The state loses direct democratic control and accountability over a strategically important industry.
- Government regulation of a privatised former monopoly (e.g. price caps or quality standards) is often still required to protect consumers, which can partly offset the promised efficiency gains and adds ongoing regulatory cost.

**Common misconception:** students often assume privatisation automatically leads to lower prices and improved service. In reality, outcomes vary by industry and by the strength of the regulatory framework put in place after privatisation — strong exam answers should evaluate this rather than assert it as a certainty, especially where an industry remains a natural monopoly after privatisation (e.g. a single regional water company with no local competitor).

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Royal Bank of Scotland (RBS, later renamed NatWest Group), banking sector, United Kingdom.
**Situation:** During the 2008 global financial crisis, RBS faced imminent collapse due to enormous losses. To prevent a failure that could have caused severe damage to the wider UK financial system and economy, the UK government injected tens of billions of pounds of taxpayer money and took a majority ownership stake in the bank, effectively nationalising a large part of it. Over the following years the government gradually sold down its shareholding back to private investors.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates nationalisation used as an emergency measure to rescue a failing but systemically important business, and the later reversal of that ownership back toward the private sector — showing that public ownership is not always permanent or ideological, but sometimes a pragmatic crisis response.
**Discussion question:** Was it right for the UK government to use taxpayers' money to nationalise part of RBS rather than allow the bank to fail? Justify your view, considering the interests of different stakeholders.

**Company/context:** Saudi Arabian Oil Company (Saudi Aramco), oil and gas, Saudi Arabia.
**Situation:** Saudi Aramco is majority-owned by the Saudi Arabian state and is one of the world's largest and most profitable companies, controlling the country's vast oil reserves as a strategic national asset. In 2019, the government sold a small percentage of shares to private and institutional investors through a stock market listing, while retaining overwhelming majority ownership and control.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates a public corporation operating a nationally strategic natural-resource industry largely for state revenue and national economic control, and shows a partial (rather than full) privatisation, where a minority of shares are sold to private investors while the government retains majority ownership and decision-making power.
**Discussion question:** Why might a government choose to sell only a small minority stake in a highly profitable state-owned company rather than fully privatising it?

## Large vs Small Business Application

Public corporations are, almost by definition, large-scale organisations — a small, local business would rarely be nationalised, since nationalisation is typically reserved for industries of national economic significance (energy, rail, telecommunications, banking) where the scale of infrastructure and the impact on the whole population justify state involvement. A small business is far more likely to be directly affected by public corporations and privatisation as a customer or supplier — for example, a small manufacturer depends on a reliable, fairly priced electricity supply, whether that electricity is generated by a public corporation or a privatised, regulated energy company. Large multinational businesses, by contrast, may be directly involved as bidders when a government privatises an industry, or as competitors newly allowed to enter a market following deregulation.
