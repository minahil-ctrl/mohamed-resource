## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | A business owned and run by one person, who keeps all profit but bears all risk | A single owner-operated hair salon |
| Partnership | A business owned by two to twenty people who share capital, decision-making, profit, and liability | A firm of solicitors owned by several partners |
| Deed of Partnership | A legal document setting out the rights and responsibilities of partners (e.g. profit share, roles, dispute procedures) | Specifies that Partner A takes 60% of profit for extra capital invested |
| Unlimited liability | Owners are personally responsible for all business debts; personal assets can be seized to repay them | A sole trader whose shop fails may have to sell their house to pay creditors |
| Limited liability | Owners (shareholders) can only lose the amount they invested in the business; personal assets are protected | A shareholder in a Ltd who invested £1,000 loses at most £1,000 if it fails |
| Private limited company (Ltd) | A company with limited liability whose shares are owned privately (often by family/friends) and cannot be sold on a public stock exchange | A family-owned manufacturing firm registered as "XYZ Ltd" |
| Public limited company (PLC) | A company with limited liability whose shares are traded freely on a stock exchange and can be bought by the public | Shares of a PLC bought and sold on the London Stock Exchange |
| Shareholder | A person or institution owning shares (a portion of ownership) in a limited company | An individual investor holding 500 shares in a PLC |
| Memorandum of Association | A legal document stating a company's name, registered office address, and objectives | Filed with the company registrar when forming a company |
| Articles of Association | A legal document setting out the internal rules for running a company, including directors' powers and shareholder rights | Specifies how often shareholder meetings must be held |
| Franchise | A business arrangement where a franchisee pays to use the name, branding, and business model of an established franchisor | A local entrepreneur operating a McDonald's outlet under franchise |

| Franchisee | The individual or company that buys the right to trade using the franchisor's name and systems | A local investor running a Subway branch |
| Joint venture | Two or more businesses agree to work together on a specific project, sharing costs, risks, and profits | Sony and Ericsson forming Sony Ericsson to produce mobile phones |
| Public corporation | A business owned and controlled by the government/state on behalf of the public | A state-owned national railway or postal service |
| Incorporation | The legal process of forming a limited company, giving it a separate legal identity from its owners | A business becomes "incorporated" once registered at Companies House (or equivalent) |
| Separate legal identity | A company is legally distinct from its owners, meaning it can own assets, sue, and be sued in its own name | A Ltd company can be sued without the shareholders being personally liable |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Every entrepreneur, from a street-food vendor in Lagos to the founders of Google, had to make a foundational decision before selling a single product: what legal form should the business take? This single choice shapes how much risk the owner personally carries, how easily the business can raise money to grow, and who ultimately controls decision-making. Getting it wrong can mean losing a family home to pay business debts; getting it right can unlock the capital needed to become the next global brand.

## Core Content

### Why Ownership Form Matters
The choice of legal structure affects four things directly examinable at IGCSE: **liability** (how much personal risk the owner(s) carry), **capital** (how easy it is to raise finance for growth), **control** (how much say the owner(s) retain over decisions), and **continuity** (what happens to the business if an owner dies, retires, or leaves). Business owners must weigh these factors against their own priorities — a founder who wants to remain fully in control might tolerate more risk and less access to capital, while one focused on rapid growth may accept diluted control in exchange for outside investment.

### Unincorporated Businesses: Sole Traders and Partnerships
A **sole trader** is the simplest and most common form of business ownership worldwide, particularly among small businesses. Setting up is quick, inexpensive, and involves minimal legal formality — often just registering with the relevant tax authority. The owner keeps 100% of the profit and has complete control over all decisions, from opening hours to pricing. However, the sole trader bears **unlimited liability**: because the business has no separate legal identity from the owner, if the business cannot pay its debts, creditors can pursue the owner's personal possessions, including their home, car, and savings. Sole traders often also struggle to raise large amounts of capital, as banks may be reluctant to lend heavily to a business with only one person's income and assets as security, and the business usually cannot sell shares. Continuity is also fragile — if the owner becomes ill, retires, or dies, the business may cease to exist in its current form.

A **partnership** extends this basic model to two or more (commonly up to twenty) co-owners who pool capital, skills, and effort. Partners typically draw up a **Deed of Partnership**, a legal document specifying how profits and losses will be shared, each partner's role and voting rights, and procedures for resolving disputes or admitting new partners. Without a deed, many jurisdictions apply default rules (e.g. profits split equally), which can lead to disputes if partners contributed unequally. Partnerships allow more capital to be raised than a sole trader (each partner injects funds) and spread the workload and risk-taking across more people, plus they bring together complementary skills (e.g. one partner strong in finance, another in sales). The key drawback is that, like sole traders, ordinary partners usually have **unlimited liability** — and in many partnership structures, liability is *joint*, meaning each partner can be held responsible for debts run up by another partner, even without their knowledge. Disagreements between partners over strategy or effort can also slow decision-making compared to a sole trader who can act unilaterally.

**Common misconception:** Students often confuse "partnership" with any business that has more than one owner, including companies with multiple shareholders. A partnership is specifically an *unincorporated* structure without limited liability protection (unless it is a Limited Liability Partnership, which is beyond the core IGCSE specification but worth flagging if it arises in a source).

### Incorporated Businesses: Private and Public Limited Companies
Incorporation transforms a business into a **separate legal identity**, distinct from its owners. This is the single most important legal concept in this chapter: because the company itself — not the shareholders — owns the assets and owes the debts, shareholders benefit from **limited liability**. If the company fails, a shareholder can lose only the money they originally invested in shares; their personal assets are protected. This dramatically changes the risk calculus for investors and is why companies can attract far more outside capital than sole traders or ordinary partnerships.

To incorporate, founders must file a **Memorandum of Association** (stating the company's name, registered office, and purpose) and **Articles of Association** (the internal rulebook covering directors' powers, shareholder meeting procedures, and voting rights).

A **private limited company (Ltd)** sells shares privately — typically to family, friends, or invited investors — and these shares cannot be traded on a public stock exchange. Existing shareholders usually must approve any transfer of shares, which lets the founders retain control while still gaining limited liability and slightly easier access to capital than a partnership. This structure suits family businesses and firms that want to grow moderately without losing control to outside investors or facing public scrutiny of their finances.

A **public limited company (PLC)** can offer its shares for sale to the general public via a stock exchange, allowing it to raise very large amounts of capital quickly (for example, through an Initial Public Offering). This capital-raising power supports large-scale expansion, research and development, and acquisitions. However, becoming a PLC brings significant costs and obligations: extensive financial reporting and disclosure requirements, vulnerability to **takeover** (anyone can buy enough shares to gain control), and diluted control for the founders as ownership spreads across potentially thousands of shareholders, each entitled to vote at the Annual General Meeting (AGM). Shareholders elect a Board of Directors to run the company on their behalf, which introduces a separation between ownership (shareholders) and control (directors/managers) — a distinction examiners frequently test.

### Franchises
A **franchise** is not strictly a separate legal form of ownership but a business *model* that can be layered onto a sole trader, partnership, or company structure. The **franchisor** owns a proven business concept, brand, and operating system, and sells the right to replicate it to a **franchisee** in exchange for an initial fee and ongoing royalty payments (usually a percentage of turnover). For the franchisee, the key advantage is buying into a tested, recognised business format with a lower failure rate than starting completely from scratch, along with training, marketing support, and bulk-purchasing advantages from the franchisor's network. The trade-off is reduced independence: franchisees must follow strict operating rules set by the franchisor (menu, décor, suppliers, pricing in some cases) and pay ongoing fees that reduce profit margins. For the franchisor, franchising allows rapid geographic expansion using franchisees' capital and local market knowledge rather than the franchisor's own funds, though it carries the risk of reputational damage if a franchisee delivers poor quality.

### Joint Ventures and Public Corporations
A **joint venture** occurs when two or more separate businesses agree to combine resources, expertise, and risk for a specific project or period, while otherwise remaining independent companies. This allows firms to enter new markets (particularly useful when local knowledge, government relations, or distribution networks are required, such as when a multinational partners with a domestic firm to enter a new country), share the financial risk and cost of a large project, and combine complementary technology or expertise. The risk is that partners may disagree over strategy, profit-sharing, or cultural/management style, and one partner may gain valuable know-how that could create a future competitor.

A **public corporation** (state-owned enterprise) is owned and controlled by the government on behalf of the public, typically providing essential services (utilities, transport, postal services) where profit is not the primary objective, or where a natural monopoly makes private competition inefficient. These are less central to core IGCSE ownership comparisons but may appear in questions about government influence on business (cross-reference: Chapter 12 covers government economic policy).

### Choosing the Appropriate Form of Ownership
Examiners frequently ask students to recommend and justify the "most appropriate" ownership form for a described business. There is rarely one universally correct answer — the skill being tested is applying the trade-offs above to the specific scenario. Key factors to weigh:
- **Risk tolerance:** A business in a risky sector (e.g. one requiring large stock investment or significant debt) benefits more from limited liability.
- **Capital needs:** A business planning rapid, capital-intensive growth (e.g. building factories) is better suited to becoming a PLC; a small local service business may not need this.
- **Desire for control:** An owner who wants to keep full control should avoid PLC status, where control is diluted among shareholders.
- **Confidentiality:** Sole traders, partnerships, and private limited companies face fewer public disclosure requirements than PLCs, which must publish detailed accounts.
- **Stage of business lifecycle:** Start-ups commonly begin as sole traders/partnerships due to low cost and simplicity, and may convert to a Ltd or PLC as they grow and need more capital or liability protection.

**Exam tip:** Always link your answer explicitly back to details given in the case study (e.g. "Because Aisha wants to retain full control of decision-making and has limited savings to risk, a partnership would suit her better than forming a PLC, since...").

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Innocent Drinks, smoothie/beverage producer, United Kingdom.
**Situation:** Innocent Drinks began in 1998 as a small partnership-style start-up between three friends (Richard Reed, Adam Balon, and Jon Wright) testing their smoothie recipes at a music festival before deciding to launch. It later incorporated as a private limited company as it grew, before global drinks giant Coca-Cola progressively acquired a majority stake in the company over several years.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the typical lifecycle progression from an informal small-business start (partnership-like arrangement) to incorporation as a private limited company, and finally acquisition by a much larger multinational — showing how ownership needs change with capital requirements and growth ambitions.
**Discussion question:** To what extent do you think Innocent's founders benefited from selling a majority stake to a large multinational, compared with staying fully independent?

**Company/context:** Subway, fast-food restaurant chain, originally founded in the United States, operating globally including across emerging markets.
**Situation:** Subway is one of the world's largest franchise operations, with the founding company (the franchisor) licensing its brand, menu, and operating systems to thousands of independent franchisees worldwide who invest their own capital to open and run individual outlets.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates the franchise model in action — rapid international expansion achieved using franchisees' capital and local knowledge rather than the franchisor building every outlet itself, while franchisees gain a tested business format with reduced start-up risk compared to launching an independent restaurant.
**Discussion question:** Discuss whether becoming a franchisee is a more or less risky way to start a business than setting up as an independent sole trader restaurant.

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business is far more likely to operate as a sole trader or partnership, prioritising low set-up cost, simplicity, and full owner control, even though this means accepting unlimited liability and facing more difficulty raising large sums of capital. A large or multinational business is far more likely to be structured as a private or (especially) public limited company, since the scale of operations, capital investment, and risk involved makes limited liability essential, and PLC status provides access to the substantial capital markets needed to fund international expansion, acquisitions, and large-scale production — even though this means diluting founder control among potentially millions of shareholders.
