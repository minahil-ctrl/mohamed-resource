## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Need | A good or service that is essential for survival. | Food, clean water, shelter, and basic clothing. |
| Want | A good or service that people would like to have but is not essential for survival. | A smartphone, a branded pair of trainers, a holiday abroad. |
| Scarcity | The basic economic problem that resources are limited while human wants are unlimited. | Fertile agricultural land is scarce relative to global demand for food. |
| Opportunity cost | The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. | A firm investing capital in a new factory forgoes the interest it could have earned by leaving the money in a savings account. |
| Factors of production | The four resources needed to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital, and enterprise. | A bakery needs premises (land), bakers (labour), ovens (capital), and an owner willing to take the risk (enterprise). |
| Land | Natural resources used in production, including the ground itself, minerals, and raw materials. | Wheat fields used to grow grain for a flour mill. |
| Labour | The human effort, both physical and mental, used in producing goods and services. | Factory workers assembling cars on a production line. |
| Capital | Man-made resources used to produce other goods and services, including machinery, tools, and money invested in the business. | Industrial ovens in a bakery or robotic arms in a car plant. |
| Enterprise | The willingness and ability of an individual (the entrepreneur) to take the risk of organising the other factors of production to produce goods or services. | An entrepreneur who invests personal savings to open a café, risking financial loss if it fails. |
| Business activity | The production and exchange of goods and services to satisfy customer needs and wants, usually with the aim of making a profit. | A clothing manufacturer producing and selling garments to retailers. |
| Added value | The difference between the cost of purchasing raw materials/inputs and the price the finished product is sold for. | A furniture maker buys timber for $50 and sells the finished table for $200, adding $150 of value. |
| Goods | Physical, tangible products that can be touched and stored. | A television, a car, a loaf of bread. |
| Services | Intangible products that cannot be touched or stored, often involving an action performed for a customer. | Haircuts, banking, education, insurance. |
| Consumers | Individuals who buy and use goods and services to satisfy their needs and wants. | A shopper buying groceries for their household. |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)

Every product a student owns — from a phone to a school uniform — exists because a business identified a need or want and organised resources to satisfy it profitably. Understanding why business activity exists is the foundation for the entire course: it explains why firms exist, why they must add value to survive, and why every decision a business makes involves an opportunity cost. Without this concept, ideas like profit, competitiveness, and business objectives (covered in the next chapter) make little sense.

## Core Content

### The Economic Problem: Scarcity and Choice

Economics as a discipline exists because of one fundamental problem: **scarcity**. Human wants are effectively unlimited — people always want more or better goods and services — but the resources available to produce those goods and services (land, labour, capital, and enterprise) are limited. Because resources are scarce, choices must be made about what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. Every choice made by a consumer, a business, or a government necessarily means giving up an alternative. This lost alternative is the **opportunity cost** of the decision.

Opportunity cost is one of the most important underlying ideas in IGCSE Business Studies and appears repeatedly in analysis and evaluation answers. For example, if a government spends money building a new hospital, the opportunity cost might be a new school that could have been built with the same funds. If a small business owner invests $10,000 in new equipment, the opportunity cost could be the interest that money would have earned in a bank account, or an alternative marketing campaign that was not funded.

**Worked example (opportunity cost reasoning):**
A furniture business has $20,000 available. It could use the money to (a) buy a new delivery van, or (b) refurbish its showroom. It chooses (a). The opportunity cost of buying the van is the improved showroom the business has given up — along with any additional sales that a more attractive showroom might have generated.

### Needs and Wants

A **need** is something essential for survival — food, water, shelter, warmth, and basic clothing. A **want** is something people desire but could survive without — a new games console, branded clothing, or a foreign holiday. Businesses exist to satisfy both, but most business activity in modern economies is focused on wants, because as incomes rise, consumers demand a wider range of non-essential goods and services. Marketing (covered in Unit 4) is largely concerned with persuading consumers that a want is worth paying for.

A common misconception is that "need" and "want" are interchangeable in everyday speech ("I need a new phone"), but in an exam context students must distinguish precisely: a phone for basic communication may be a want, while food is a need. Examiners expect this distinction to be applied accurately, not loosely.

### Factors of Production

To produce any good or service, a business must combine four types of resource, known as the **factors of production**:

1. **Land** — not just the ground, but all natural resources: agricultural land, forests, water, oil, minerals, and other raw materials taken from nature. The reward for supplying land is **rent**.
2. **Labour** — the physical and mental effort of workers. This includes both unskilled and skilled labour. The reward for labour is **wages/salaries**.
3. **Capital** — man-made resources used in production, such as machinery, tools, factories, and vehicles. In economics this is distinct from "capital" meaning money used to start a business, though the two are related (money capital buys physical capital). The reward for capital is **interest**.
4. **Enterprise** — the entrepreneur's willingness to take the risk of bringing the other three factors together to produce a good or service, in the hope of making a profit. The reward for enterprise is **profit**.

Every business, regardless of size or sector, combines these four factors. A one-person hairdressing business uses land (the rented salon), labour (the hairdresser's own effort, or employees), capital (scissors, hairdryers, chairs), and enterprise (the owner's risk-taking and organisational decisions).

### The Purpose of Business Activity: Adding Value

The central purpose of business activity is to combine factors of production to create goods and services that customers value more than the raw inputs used to make them. This difference is called **added value**, and it is the mechanism by which businesses generate revenue and profit.

**Added value = Selling price − Cost of bought-in materials and components**

Added value can be increased in several ways:
- **Branding** — building a reputation or image that allows a premium price (e.g. a branded pair of trainers sells for far more than an unbranded equivalent, even if production costs are similar).
- **Quality** — superior materials, craftsmanship, or reliability.
- **Design and innovation** — unique features that competitors cannot easily copy.
- **Convenience** — making a product easier or faster to access, such as home delivery or 24-hour service.
- **Customer service** — a positive buying experience that increases perceived value.

**Worked example (added value):**
A jewellery maker buys $30 of silver and gemstones. After design and craftsmanship, the finished necklace sells for $180.
Added value = $180 − $30 = **$150**.
This $150 must cover labour, capital costs (tools, workshop rent), and profit for the entrepreneur.

A common exam pitfall is confusing added value with profit. Added value is the difference between selling price and the cost of bought-in materials only; profit is what remains after *all* costs (including labour, rent, and other overheads) have been deducted from revenue. Added value is a broader concept that funds those other costs as well as profit.

### Goods and Services

Business output is classified into **goods** (tangible, physical products, e.g. a car) and **services** (intangible, e.g. car insurance). Many businesses provide a combination of both — a car dealership sells the physical car (a good) and may also provide financing and servicing (services). This distinction matters because goods can typically be stored and transported, while services are usually consumed at the point of delivery and cannot be stockpiled, which has implications for operations management studied later in the course (Unit 3).

*Cross-reference: the concept of added value links directly to Chapter 02 (Business Objectives), since profit maximisation depends on maximising added value relative to costs, and to later chapters on marketing and competitiveness, where branding and quality are explored as ways of raising added value.*

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Nike Inc. — sportswear and footwear manufacturer, United States (globally operating multinational).
**Situation:** Nike outsources the physical manufacturing of its shoes to contract factories, mainly in Vietnam, China, and Indonesia, where the raw materials (rubber, textiles, leather) cost only a small fraction of the final retail price. Nike's own activity is concentrated on design, branding, marketing, and distribution.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates added value through branding and design — the enormous difference between the bought-in material/manufacturing cost and the final retail price is added value created almost entirely by Nike's brand identity and marketing rather than the physical product itself.
**Discussion question:** To what extent can a business's added value come entirely from branding rather than from the physical product itself? Justify your view.

**Company/context:** A smallholder coffee farmer cooperative in Ethiopia (e.g. cooperatives supplying under Fairtrade certification), agricultural sector, Ethiopia.
**Situation:** Smallholder farmers combine the factor of production "land" (coffee-growing plots) with their own labour to grow and harvest coffee cherries, which are then processed and sold onward, eventually reaching consumers as roasted coffee in developed-country markets at many times the price the farmer received for the raw beans.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates the factors of production (land and labour) at the start of a value chain, and shows how added value increases at each stage as the product moves from raw material towards a finished, branded consumer good.
**Discussion question:** Why might the added value created at the farming stage of a product's value chain be much smaller than the added value created at the branding and retail stage, and is this fair to producers?

## Large vs Small Business Application

A small business, such as a local bakery, typically controls and combines all four factors of production directly and personally — the owner (enterprise) may also supply labour, rent a single premises (land), and own a modest amount of equipment (capital). Its ability to add value often depends on personal service, freshness, and local reputation. A large multinational business, such as Nike or Unilever, separates these factors across many locations and specialist roles — enterprise is exercised by professional managers and shareholders rather than a single risk-taking owner, land and labour are sourced globally often through outsourcing, and capital investment is on a vastly larger scale (automated factories, global supply chains). Large businesses typically add value primarily through branding, economies of scale, and research and development, whereas small businesses more often add value through personalisation, convenience, and direct customer relationships.
