## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Business objective | A specific, measurable goal that a business aims to achieve within a given time frame, derived from its overall mission. | "Increase market share by 5% within two years." |
| Mission statement | A brief, formal statement of a business's overall purpose and values. | "To inspire and nurture the human spirit — one person, one cup, one neighbourhood at a time" (Starbucks). |
| Survival | The short-term objective of simply continuing to operate and avoid closure, typically pursued by new or struggling businesses. | A new café aiming just to cover its costs in its first year of trading. |
| Profit maximisation | The objective of achieving the largest possible profit, calculated as total revenue minus total costs. | A private shareholder-owned firm setting prices and costs to maximise annual profit. |
| Profit satisficing | Aiming for an acceptable, "good enough" level of profit rather than the maximum possible, often to balance other goals such as work-life balance or ethics. | A family-owned shop content with steady, comfortable profit rather than aggressive expansion. |
| Growth | An objective focused on increasing the size of the business, measured by revenue, output, market share, or number of employees/branches. | A restaurant chain opening ten new branches over five years. |
| Market share | The proportion of total sales in a market accounted for by one business, usually expressed as a percentage. | A soft drinks company holding 25% of the national cola market. |
| Social objectives | Goals relating to the wellbeing of employees, the local community, or wider society, beyond pure financial performance. | A company providing free meals or healthcare to employees in a developing country. |
| Ethical objectives | Goals relating to doing what is morally right, even where it is not legally required. | A clothing brand refusing to use suppliers linked to child labour. |
| Stakeholder | Any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities and performance of a business. | Employees, shareholders, customers, suppliers, government, and the local community. |
| Shareholder value | The objective of maximising the returns (dividends and share price growth) received by a company's shareholders. | A public limited company prioritising decisions that increase its share price. |
| Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) | A business approach where a firm voluntarily takes responsibility for its impact on society and the environment, beyond legal requirements. | A mining company funding local schools and environmental restoration near its sites. |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)

Every business decision — from pricing a product to closing a factory — is ultimately judged against the objectives the business has set itself. A start-up food truck and a global company like Unilever are both "in business," but they are chasing very different goals at very different stages of their life. Understanding objectives explains *why* businesses behave as they do, and sets up the recurring exam theme of balancing the sometimes-competing interests of different stakeholders.

## Core Content

### What Is a Business Objective?

A **business objective** is a specific goal a business sets itself, usually derived from its broader **mission** or purpose. Objectives give a business direction, allow managers to plan how resources should be used, and provide a benchmark against which performance can be measured. Good objectives are commonly described using the "SMART" framework: **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, and **T**ime-bound. For example, "to grow" is not a strong objective because it cannot be measured or checked; "to increase sales revenue by 10% within the next financial year" is SMART because progress can be tracked and success clearly judged.

A **mission statement** sits above specific objectives — it is a short, often aspirational statement of what the business exists to do and the values that guide it. Objectives are the practical, measurable steps taken to work towards that mission.

### Common Business Objectives

**1. Survival**
For new businesses, or established businesses in a crisis (e.g. during a recession or after a major shock such as a pandemic), simply staying in operation and avoiding insolvency becomes the primary objective, sometimes temporarily overriding profit. Around half of new small businesses fail within their first five years, which is why survival is realistically the dominant early-stage objective for entrepreneurs, rather than growth or profit maximisation.

**2. Profit**
Profit is the reward for enterprise and the primary objective of most private-sector businesses in the medium-to-long term, since without profit a business cannot reward its owners, reinvest, or attract investment. A distinction is drawn between:
- **Profit maximisation** — trying to earn the greatest possible profit, often associated with larger, shareholder-owned companies under pressure to deliver returns.
- **Profit satisficing** — accepting a "good enough" level of profit, common among small, owner-managed businesses that value independence, work-life balance, or ethical considerations over squeezing out every possible dollar of profit.

**3. Growth**
Growth can be measured by rising sales revenue, increased output, a larger workforce, more branches/outlets, or expansion into new markets (domestic or international). Growth often becomes a priority once survival is secure, because a larger business can achieve economies of scale (covered in Unit 5), greater market power, and improved status/security for the owners. However, growth carries risks, including loss of control, increased debt, and difficulty maintaining quality or culture as the organisation expands (diseconomies of scale).

**4. Market Share**
Market share is the percentage of total industry sales captured by one firm. Increasing market share is often pursued as a route to profit and influence — a higher market share can bring pricing power, brand recognition, and bargaining power with suppliers. Market share is sometimes pursued even at the expense of short-term profit (e.g. through aggressive, low-price strategies) because a dominant market position is seen as valuable in the long run.

**5. Social and Ethical Objectives**
Some businesses explicitly build social or ethical goals into their core objectives, not merely as public relations. This includes fair treatment and pay for workers, environmentally sustainable production, community investment, and refusing to deal with unethical suppliers. **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)** describes the voluntary actions a business takes to manage its impact on society and the environment beyond what the law requires. Social enterprises (covered in Chapter 03) are built with a primary social objective from the outset, but even conventional profit-driven firms increasingly adopt CSR objectives to protect their brand reputation and respond to consumer and investor pressure.

### Why Objectives Differ and Change

Objectives are not fixed. They typically evolve as a business moves through its life cycle:
- **Start-up stage:** survival is usually paramount, since cash flow is fragile and the business has no track record.
- **Growth stage:** once stable, businesses commonly shift focus towards growth and increasing market share.
- **Maturity stage:** established, larger firms often prioritise profit maximisation and shareholder value, alongside maintaining market position.
- **External shocks:** events such as recessions, new competition, changes in government regulation, or reputational crises can force any business, regardless of size, to revert temporarily to survival-focused objectives.

Objectives also differ by **ownership type**. A sole trader may prioritise personal independence and a satisfactory income (profit satisficing), while a public limited company answerable to thousands of shareholders is under continual pressure to maximise shareholder value, often via published quarterly profit targets.

### Objectives and Stakeholder Conflict

A **stakeholder** is any person or group affected by, or with an interest in, a business's activities — including owners/shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, the local community, government, and pressure groups. Different stakeholders often want different things from the same business, meaning that pursuing one objective can create conflict with another stakeholder group:

- **Shareholders** typically want profit maximisation and rising share value, which may push managers to cut costs — potentially through redundancies or lower wages — creating conflict with **employees**, who want job security and higher pay.
- **Customers** want low prices and high quality, which can conflict with the **profit objective** if it requires the business to accept lower margins.
- **Local communities** may want the business to limit pollution or noise, which can conflict with a **cost-minimisation** objective if environmental controls are expensive.
- **Government** wants businesses to comply with regulation and pay taxes, which can reduce profit compared to non-compliance.

A common exam pitfall is treating "stakeholders" as if they always want the same thing, or listing stakeholders without explaining *why* their interests might clash with a specific objective in the given scenario — strong AO3/AO4 answers always explain the specific conflict, not just name the stakeholders.

## Diagrams Required

1. **Type:** Pyramid/hierarchy diagram
   **Purpose:** To show the relationship between mission, objectives, and day-to-day decisions.
   **Content spec:** A three-tier pyramid. Top tier (smallest): "Mission Statement — overall purpose and values." Middle tier: "Objectives — specific, measurable goals (e.g. SMART targets for survival, profit, growth, market share, CSR)." Bottom tier (largest): "Strategies and Day-to-Day Decisions — pricing, staffing, marketing actions taken to meet objectives."

2. **Type:** Life-cycle line graph with objective labels
   **Purpose:** To show how a typical business's dominant objective changes as it grows over time.
   **Content spec:** X-axis labelled "Time," Y-axis labelled "Business Size / Sales Revenue." An S-shaped or rising curve divided into three labelled stages: "Start-up — Objective: Survival," "Growth — Objective: Growth / Market Share," "Maturity — Objective: Profit Maximisation / Shareholder Value." Add a downward dip near the end labelled "External shock (e.g. recession) — Objective reverts to Survival" to show that objectives are not always linear.

3. **Type:** Stakeholder conflict table/matrix
   **Purpose:** To clarify how the same business decision affects different stakeholders differently.
   **Content spec:** Table with columns "Stakeholder," "What they typically want," "Potential conflict with profit/growth objective." Rows for Shareholders, Employees, Customers, Suppliers, Local Community, Government — each row completed with a specific example conflict (e.g. Employees: want higher wages and job security vs. cost-cutting to raise profit).

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Patagonia, outdoor clothing and equipment retailer, United States.
**Situation:** Patagonia has built explicit environmental and ethical objectives into its core business model, including its "1% for the Planet" commitment (donating 1% of sales to environmental causes) and its "Don't Buy This Jacket" anti-consumerism advertising campaign, which discouraged unnecessary purchasing even though the company still needs sales revenue to survive.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a company can build social/ethical objectives alongside — and sometimes appearing to conflict with — the conventional profit objective, and how ownership structure (Patagonia is privately owned, not subject to public shareholder pressure) allows more freedom to prioritise ethics over pure profit maximisation.
**Discussion question:** Can a business genuinely prioritise environmental objectives over profit maximisation and still survive long-term, or is CSR ultimately just a marketing strategy that supports profit indirectly?

**Company/context:** Amazon.com Inc., e-commerce and technology, United States (global operations).
**Situation:** For many years after its 1994 founding, Amazon deliberately ran at low profit or loss, reinvesting almost all revenue into growth — warehouses, technology, and market expansion — prioritising growth and market share over short-term profit maximisation, a strategy that shareholders largely tolerated because of confidence in long-term returns.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates that growth and market share can be pursued as primary objectives instead of profit maximisation, particularly by a company confident that long-term market dominance will eventually generate greater profit than an early focus on short-term returns.
**Discussion question:** Under what circumstances might shareholders be willing to accept low or no profit for many years in exchange for a growth objective, and what risks does this strategy carry?

## Large vs Small Business Application

A small, newly-formed business — for example a sole trader launching a mobile car-valeting service — will almost always prioritise **survival** first, since cash flow is tight, there is no track record to reassure lenders or customers, and the owner's personal finances are often directly at risk. Objectives tend to be informal and flexible, set by the owner alone. A large multinational business, such as a public limited company, faces continuous formal pressure from thousands of external shareholders and financial markets to deliver **profit maximisation and shareholder value**, with objectives set out formally in annual reports and strategic plans, and performance judged every financial quarter. Large businesses also face far greater public scrutiny of their social and ethical objectives, since reputational damage can affect a much larger customer base and share price.
