- Explain the concept of unlimited liability and how it differs from limited liability.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | A business owned and run by one person, who keeps all the profit but bears unlimited liability for all debts. | A self-employed plumber trading under their own name. |
| Partnership | A business owned by two or more people (typically 2–20) who share capital, responsibilities, profits, and unlimited liability. | Two friends jointly running a law firm as "Smith & Osei Solicitors." |
| Deed of partnership | A legal document setting out the terms of a partnership, including how profits are shared and how disputes are resolved. | An agreement stating that profits are split 60:40 between two partners based on capital invested. |
| Unlimited liability | A legal condition where the owner(s) of a business are personally responsible for all business debts, risking personal assets if the business fails. | A sole trader's house being sold to repay business debts after bankruptcy. |
| Limited liability | A legal condition where an owner's liability for business debts is restricted to the amount they invested in the business. | A shareholder in a limited company losing only the value of their shares if the company fails, not their personal savings. |
| Social enterprise | A business that trades to fulfil a social or environmental purpose, reinvesting most of its profit back into that mission rather than distributing it to owners. | A café that trains and employs homeless individuals, reinvesting profits into further training programmes. |
| Franchise | A business model in which a franchisor grants a franchisee the right to trade using its established brand, products, and systems, usually in exchange for fees and royalties. | A local entrepreneur operating a McDonald's restaurant under a franchise agreement. |
| Franchisor | The individual or company that owns the original business idea/brand and sells the right to use it to others. | McDonald's Corporation as the franchisor of its restaurant brand. |
| Franchisee | The individual or company that buys the right to trade under an established brand and business format from the franchisor. | A local businessperson who buys and operates a Subway outlet. |
| Royalty | An ongoing fee, usually a percentage of sales revenue, paid by a franchisee to a franchisor for continued use of the brand and support. | A franchisee paying 5% of monthly sales revenue to the franchisor. |
| Start-up capital | The initial finance needed to set up a business before it starts generating its own revenue. | Savings and a bank loan used to buy equipment and stock for a new shop. |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)

Most businesses in the world — from a single market-stall trader in Lagos to a family-run bakery in rural Wales — are not giant corporations, but small, often unincorporated businesses owned by one or a handful of people. The legal form an entrepreneur chooses shapes how much risk they personally carry, how much control they keep, and how easily they can raise money. This chapter equips students to reason about which structure best fits a real entrepreneurial scenario — a skill directly tested through Paper 1 case studies.

## Core Content

### Sole Traders

A **sole trader** is a business owned by one individual. It is the simplest and most common form of business ownership worldwide, particularly in retail, trades (plumbing, hairdressing), and personal services. Setting up as a sole trader typically requires minimal legal formality — often just registering with the relevant tax authority — which makes it fast and cheap to start.

**Advantages of being a sole trader:**
- Keeps all the profit, since there are no other owners to share it with.
- Full control over all business decisions, without needing to consult partners or shareholders.
- Simple and inexpensive to set up, with few legal formalities.
- Can maintain privacy, since detailed accounts do not usually have to be made public.
- Flexibility to change direction, hours, or products quickly.

**Disadvantages of being a sole trader:**
- **Unlimited liability** — the single most important disadvantage. If the business fails or is sued, the owner's personal assets (house, savings, car) can be used to pay business debts, because in law the sole trader and the business are not separate legal entities.
- Difficulty raising large amounts of capital, since banks may be reluctant to lend heavily to a business backed only by one person, and the owner cannot sell shares.
- Heavy workload and reliance on one person — illness or absence of the owner can halt the business entirely.
- Limited specialist skills, since one person cannot be an expert in every business function (finance, marketing, operations).
- Business continuity risk — the business may cease to exist if the owner retires, dies, or wishes to leave.

### Partnerships

A **partnership** is formed when two or more people (commonly limited to a maximum, e.g. 20, in many jurisdictions, though this varies) agree to run a business together, sharing capital, workload, and unlimited liability. Partnerships are common among professionals such as doctors, lawyers, and accountants.

It is strongly advisable for partners to draw up a **deed of partnership**, a legal document specifying how profits/losses will be shared, how much capital each partner contributes, how decisions will be made, and how the partnership can be dissolved. Without a deed of partnership, disputes can be costly and profits are typically assumed to be split equally regardless of unequal contribution — a common source of conflict.

**Advantages of a partnership:**
- More capital can usually be raised than a sole trader, since more than one person is investing.
- Shared workload and responsibility, and the ability to specialise (e.g. one partner focuses on finance, another on sales).
- Shared risk-taking and decision-making can bring more balanced, better-informed judgement.
- Relatively simple and inexpensive to set up compared to forming a company.

**Disadvantages of a partnership:**
- **Unlimited liability** still applies to (most) partners — each is personally liable, and in many jurisdictions can even be held responsible for debts caused by another partner's decisions ("joint and several liability").
- Profits must be shared among all partners.
- Potential for disagreement and conflict between partners over decisions or unequal effort.
- Slower decision-making, since partners must typically be consulted.
- The partnership may need to be reformed or dissolved if a partner leaves, retires, or dies, creating instability.

*Note: some jurisdictions allow a "Limited Partnership" or "Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)," which gives some or all partners limited liability, but the standard/ordinary partnership studied at this level carries unlimited liability for all partners.*

### Social Enterprises

A **social enterprise** is a business that trades in the market like any other, but whose central purpose is to achieve a social or environmental goal rather than simply maximise profit for private owners. Crucially, social enterprises earn their income through trading (selling goods/services), which distinguishes them from charities that rely mainly on donations — but the majority of any profit made is reinvested into the social mission rather than paid out to owners as personal income.

**Advantages of a social enterprise:**
- Can attract customers, employees, and investors who want to support an ethical or socially beneficial mission.
- May qualify for grants, government support, or favourable tax treatment in some countries due to social benefit.
- Builds strong brand loyalty and positive public relations.
- Can address market failures or social needs that purely profit-driven businesses ignore.

**Disadvantages of a social enterprise:**
- Balancing commercial viability with social mission can be difficult — the enterprise still needs enough revenue to survive as a trading business.
- May find it harder to attract profit-seeking investors, since returns to investors are limited by the reinvestment requirement.
- Can be harder to scale rapidly compared to a purely profit-driven competitor.

A common misconception is that a social enterprise is the same as a charity or a non-profit organisation — students must be precise: a social enterprise *trades* to generate its own income and can make a profit, but reinvests that profit into its mission, whereas a charity typically depends primarily on donations and grants.

### Franchises

A **franchise** is a business format in which the **franchisor** (owner of an established, proven business concept, brand, and operating system) grants a **franchisee** the legal right to trade under that brand, usually in a defined territory, in exchange for an initial franchise fee and ongoing **royalty** payments (typically a percentage of sales revenue). The franchisee runs their own legally separate business but must follow the franchisor's established methods, branding, and quality standards.

**Advantages for the franchisee:**
- Reduced risk of failure compared to starting an entirely new business, since the franchisee is using an already-proven business model and recognised brand.
- Benefits from the franchisor's national/international advertising and marketing.
- Receives training, support, and established supplier relationships from the franchisor.
- Easier to obtain finance from banks, since lenders view established franchise brands as lower-risk.

**Disadvantages for the franchisee:**
- Must pay an initial franchise fee and ongoing royalties, reducing overall profit.
- Limited independence — the franchisee must follow the franchisor's rules on products, pricing, layout, and suppliers, restricting creative control.
- The franchisee's reputation is tied to the wider brand — poor performance or scandal at other franchise outlets can damage their own business through no fault of their own.
- The franchise agreement may include restrictive terms on renewal, resale, or territory.

**Advantages for the franchisor:**
- Rapid business expansion without needing to raise or risk all the capital themselves, since franchisees provide their own investment.
- Franchisees, as owners of their own outlet, are typically highly motivated to work hard for its success (more so than a hired branch manager).
- Growing brand presence increases overall brand recognition and bargaining power with suppliers.

**Disadvantages for the franchisor:**
- Less direct day-to-day control over each individual outlet than the franchisor would have over a wholly-owned branch.
- Poor performance or bad practice by one franchisee can damage the reputation of the entire brand.
- Franchisor shares profit (through royalties) rather than retaining 100% of each outlet's earnings.

*Cross-reference: this chapter connects to Chapter 04 (Limited Companies), since forming a limited company is often the next step for a sole trader or partnership seeking to raise capital and gain limited liability, and to Unit 2 (Finance), where sources of start-up capital for each business form are studied in greater depth.*

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Subway (Doctor's Associates Inc.), sandwich restaurant franchise, originally founded in the United States, now operating globally including in many emerging markets.
**Situation:** Subway is one of the world's largest franchise systems, with the vast majority of its restaurants owned and operated by independent franchisees who pay an initial franchise fee and an ongoing royalty and advertising fee to the franchisor in exchange for using the Subway brand, recipes, and operating systems.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the franchisor–franchisee relationship, including the balance of reduced risk and brand support for the franchisee against ongoing royalty payments and restricted independence.
**Discussion question:** Why might an entrepreneur choose to become a Subway franchisee rather than open an independent sandwich shop under their own brand, and what freedoms would they be giving up?

**Company/context:** Grameen Bank, microfinance and social business movement founded by Muhammad Yunus, Bangladesh.
**Situation:** Grameen Bank was established to provide small loans (microcredit) to poor individuals, particularly women, who lacked access to conventional banking, with the explicit social objective of reducing poverty; it operates as a trading financial institution (charging interest to remain sustainable) while reinvesting to expand its social mission, and its founder was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006 for this work.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the social enterprise model — a business that trades commercially (lending and collecting interest) but exists primarily to achieve a social objective (poverty reduction) rather than to maximise returns to private owners.
**Discussion question:** Should a social enterprise like Grameen Bank be allowed to make a profit, and does charging interest on loans to poor borrowers conflict with its social mission?

## Large vs Small Business Application

Sole traders, partnerships, and individual franchisees are, by definition, small businesses and represent the archetypal Paper 1 context — a single owner or small group bearing personal risk, with limited access to capital and typically operating in one location. However, the *franchisor* side of a franchise relationship is often a very large, sometimes multinational business (such as Subway's parent, Doctor's Associates Inc., or McDonald's Corporation), showing that a single business "system" can simultaneously involve small-business owners (franchisees) operating under the umbrella of a large corporate brand. This dual nature makes franchising a useful bridge between Paper 1 (small business) and Paper 2 (large business) thinking.
