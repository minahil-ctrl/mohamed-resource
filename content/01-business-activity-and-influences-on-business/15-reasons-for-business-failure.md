## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Business failure | The point at which a business is unable to continue operating, typically resulting in closure, insolvency, or bankruptcy. | A retailer permanently closing all its stores after running out of money. |
| Insolvency | The state of being unable to pay debts as they fall due. | A business unable to pay its suppliers' invoices on time. |
| Bankruptcy/liquidation | The formal legal process of closing a business and selling its assets to repay creditors when it can no longer continue trading. | A court-appointed administrator selling off a failed company's equipment and stock. |
| Cash flow | The movement of money into and out of a business over a period of time. | A business receiving customer payments (inflow) and paying staff wages (outflow). |
| Cash flow problem | A situation where a business does not have enough cash available to meet its immediate obligations, even if it is profitable on paper. | A profitable business unable to pay a supplier because customers have not yet paid their invoices. |
| Overtrading | Expanding a business too quickly without sufficient cash or capital to support the higher level of activity. | A small business accepting a huge new order it cannot finance the materials and labour for. |
| Poor management | Ineffective decision-making, planning, or leadership within a business that undermines its performance. | A manager failing to monitor stock levels, leading to costly shortages or waste. |
| Competition | Rival businesses offering similar products or services, competing for the same customers. | A new low-cost supermarket entering a town and taking customers from an established local shop. |
| External shock | A sudden, unpredictable event outside a business's control that significantly disrupts its operations or market. | A global pandemic forcing the closure of non-essential shops. |
| Market research | The process of gathering information about customers and competitors to inform business decisions. | A business surveying customers before launching a new product. |
| Break-even point | The level of output/sales at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither profit nor loss. | A café needing to sell 200 coffees a day to break even. |
| Working capital | The finance available to a business for its day-to-day operating expenses, calculated as current assets minus current liabilities. | Cash and stock a business uses to pay for immediate running costs. |
| Gearing | The proportion of a business's capital that comes from borrowed funds (debt) relative to funds from owners (equity). | A highly geared business relying heavily on bank loans rather than owner investment. |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)

The vast majority of new businesses do not survive their first five years, and even long-established, world-famous companies can collapse — the UK department store chain Debenhams and the American retailer Toys "R" Us both went into liquidation despite decades, in some cases over a century, of trading history. Understanding why businesses fail is not simply a matter of listing bad luck; it requires analysing specific, recurring internal and external causes that entrepreneurs, managers, and investors must recognise and manage. This topic draws together many ideas from earlier in the course — cash flow, competition, management, and external factors — and asks what happens when a business fails to manage them.

## Core Content

### Defining Business Failure

**Business failure** occurs when a business is no longer able to continue operating and is forced to close, often through formal **insolvency** or **liquidation/bankruptcy** proceedings, in which the business's assets are sold to repay what it owes to creditors. It is important to distinguish business failure from simple poor performance: a business can experience a difficult year, falling sales, or even a loss, and still survive if it has enough cash and financial resilience to recover. Failure specifically refers to the point where a business cannot continue — most commonly because it has run out of cash and cannot pay what it owes (it has become **insolvent**), though a business can also be deliberately closed by its owners for other reasons (e.g. retirement with no successor), which is not the same as failure.

A useful way to organise the causes of business failure is to separate **internal causes** (arising from decisions and management within the business itself) from **external causes** (arising from the wider business environment, outside the business's direct control).

### Internal Causes of Business Failure

**1. Poor cash flow management.** A business can be profitable on paper (revenue exceeds costs over a period) yet still fail if it runs out of *cash* to pay its immediate bills, wages, or suppliers — this is one of the single most common causes of business failure, particularly among small and new businesses. Cash flow problems often arise when customers are slow to pay (especially if a business offers generous trade credit), when too much cash is tied up in unsold stock, or when a business spends heavily on fixed assets without retaining enough **working capital** for day-to-day running costs.

**Worked example (cash flow vs profit):** A furniture business sells $50,000 of goods on credit in a month, recording a healthy profit. However, its customers do not pay their invoices for 60 days, while the business must pay its own suppliers and staff within 30 days. Despite being profitable, the business faces a cash flow shortage in the interim and may be unable to pay its own bills on time — illustrating that profit and cash flow are not the same thing (cross-reference: Unit 3 cash flow forecasting chapters).

**2. Poor management and planning.** Weak decision-making — such as inadequate market research before launching a product, poor stock control, ineffective financial planning, or a failure to adapt to a changing market — significantly increases the risk of failure. Many failures stem from a founder's strong technical or product skills not being matched by strong business/financial management skills, especially as a business grows beyond what one person can effectively oversee.

**3. Overtrading.** **Overtrading** occurs when a business expands its level of activity (e.g. accepting large new orders or opening new outlets) faster than its available cash and capital can support, leaving it unable to pay for the increased materials, wages, and overheads the expansion requires, even though the growth itself may appear to be a success. Overtrading is a particular risk for fast-growing small businesses that lack access to sufficient additional finance.

**4. Insufficient or inappropriate finance.** A business may fail because it did not raise enough start-up or working capital in the first place, relied too heavily on expensive short-term borrowing (poor **gearing**), or could not access additional finance when it needed it (e.g. a bank refusing a loan), leaving it unable to survive a difficult period or fund necessary investment.

**5. Failure to innovate or respond to changing customer needs.** Businesses that do not keep their products, technology, or business model up to date risk losing customers to competitors who better meet evolving needs — long-term decline of this kind can ultimately lead to failure even without any single dramatic event.

### External Causes of Business Failure

**1. Increased competition.** The entry of a new, larger, or lower-cost competitor into a market can rapidly erode a business's sales and market share, particularly threatening small, local businesses that struggle to match the prices, product range, or marketing budgets of larger rivals.

**2. Changes in the economic environment.** A downturn in the wider economy (recession), rising interest rates (increasing the cost of borrowing), or high inflation (raising costs) can all reduce consumer spending and business profitability, pushing already vulnerable businesses into failure (cross-reference: Chapter 13, External Factors).

**3. Changes in government policy/regulation.** New laws, taxes, or regulations can raise a business's costs or restrict how it operates, sometimes suddenly enough that a business cannot adapt in time.

**4. External shocks.** Sudden, unpredictable events — such as a natural disaster, a pandemic, or a major supply chain disruption — can severely damage or destroy a business's ability to trade, regardless of how well it was previously managed. The COVID-19 pandemic, for example, forced the prolonged closure of many physical retail and hospitality businesses worldwide, pushing many otherwise viable businesses into failure due to a sudden, extreme, and unforeseeable loss of revenue.

**5. Technological change.** A business whose product or business model is made obsolete by new technology, or whose competitors adopt more efficient technology first, can fail even if it was previously very successful, if it does not adapt quickly enough.

**Common misconception:** students often attribute business failure to a single dramatic cause ("the company went bankrupt because of the recession"), when in most real cases failure results from a *combination* of internal weaknesses (e.g. poor cash flow management or high debt) that leave a business unable to withstand an external shock (e.g. a downturn or new competitor) that a financially stronger business could have survived. Strong AO3/AO4 answers should identify this interaction between internal vulnerability and external trigger, rather than naming only one cause in isolation.

### Why New and Small Businesses Are Especially Vulnerable

New and small businesses fail at a significantly higher rate than established, large businesses, for several linked reasons:
- **Limited financial reserves/working capital**, meaning even a short period of poor trading or delayed customer payments can cause a cash flow crisis.
- **Reliance on a single founder or small management team**, often without specialist experience in finance, marketing, or operations, increasing the risk of poor management decisions.
- **Limited access to finance and credit**, since lenders often see new businesses (with no trading history) as higher risk, restricting their ability to borrow through a difficult period.
- **No established customer base or brand loyalty** to fall back on if a new competitor enters the market or if sales dip.
- **Greater exposure to a single product, market, or supplier**, meaning a business has little diversification to absorb a shock affecting that one area, unlike a large diversified business.

*Cross-reference: this chapter builds directly on Chapter 14 (Measuring Success in Business), since a sustained failure to achieve key measures of success — particularly profit and positive cash flow — is usually what precedes business failure, and connects to Unit 3 chapters on cash flow forecasting and sources of finance, and Chapter 13 (External Factors), which explores the economic, legal, and technological forces that can trigger failure.*

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Toys "R" Us, toy and games retailer, United States (also operated internationally, including in the UK).
**Situation:** Toys "R" Us, once the dominant toy retailer in the United States, filed for bankruptcy and ultimately closed its US stores in 2017–2018. The company had taken on very high levels of debt following a leveraged buyout in 2005, meaning a large share of its revenue had to go toward interest payments rather than investment; combined with intensifying competition from online retailers such as Amazon and discount retailers such as Walmart, the business could not generate enough cash to service its debt and fund the store improvements needed to compete.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the combination of an internal cause (very high gearing/debt from the buyout, leaving little financial flexibility) with an external cause (intensifying competition from online and discount retailers), showing how the interaction of internal weakness and external pressure led to failure.
**Discussion question:** To what extent was Toys "R" Us's failure caused by its own financial decisions (high debt) rather than external competition, or was it a combination of both?

**Company/context:** Thomas Cook Group, travel and tour operator, United Kingdom (one of the world's oldest travel companies, founded in 1841).
**Situation:** Thomas Cook, a very long-established international travel company, collapsed into liquidation in September 2019 after failing to secure additional emergency financing. The company had built up very high levels of debt over previous years (partly from a large merger), while facing declining demand for traditional package holidays as more customers booked flights and accommodation independently online, along with rising costs and a series of poor trading periods that eroded its cash reserves.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how even a very long-established, globally recognised business can fail through a combination of high debt/poor financial management (internal) and a changing competitive/technological environment reducing demand for its core product (external), reinforcing that business failure is not limited to small or new businesses.
**Discussion question:** Analyse why a business as large and long-established as Thomas Cook could still fail, and discuss what warning signs might have indicated the risk of failure in advance.

## Large vs Small Business Application

Small businesses are statistically far more likely to fail than large businesses, primarily because of limited cash reserves, heavy reliance on a single founder's management skill, and restricted access to finance during difficult periods — a single large unpaid invoice or a short downturn in trade can be enough to cause a cash flow crisis that forces closure. Large businesses, as the Toys "R" Us and Thomas Cook case studies show, are not immune to failure, but the specific pattern differs: large business failure is more often driven by excessive debt taken on to fund earlier growth or acquisitions, combined with a slow strategic response to major changes in the competitive or technological environment, rather than a single short-term cash flow shock. Large businesses do, however, typically have more financial resilience, more diversified operations, and greater access to emergency finance than small businesses, giving them more opportunities to recover from difficulties before failure becomes inevitable.
