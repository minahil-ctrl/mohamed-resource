## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
No matter how good a product is, how fairly it is priced, or how conveniently it is distributed, customers will not buy what they do not know exists. Promotion is how a business gets its message in front of the right people and persuades them to act — and the tools available for doing this have expanded enormously with the rise of social media and digital advertising. Coca-Cola's decades-long sponsorship of major sporting events has built global brand recognition through consistent above-the-line promotion, while small businesses today can run highly targeted social media campaigns that would once have required a television advertising budget far beyond their reach. Choosing the right mix of promotional methods, matched to the product, budget and target audience, is central to a marketing campaign's success.

## Core Content

### What Is "Promotion" in the Marketing Mix?
Promotion is the element of the marketing mix concerned with communicating with customers: informing them a product exists, explaining its benefits, and persuading them to buy it rather than a competitor's product. Promotional decisions must be consistent with the rest of the marketing mix — the product's features and positioning (Chapter 36), its price point (Chapter 37), and the distribution channels through which it is sold (Chapter 38) — since these all shape what message is appropriate and where customers should be reached.

### The Promotional Mix
Businesses rarely rely on a single promotional method. Instead, they typically combine several methods into a **promotional mix**, chosen to complement one another and reach the target audience effectively across different stages of the buying process (from first creating awareness through to prompting an immediate purchase). The main elements of the promotional mix are:

**Advertising**: paid-for communication through independent media (television, radio, print, billboards, online display advertising, and so on) designed to inform and/or persuade a target audience. Advertising is well suited to building broad brand awareness and reaching a large audience, but can be expensive, and its precise impact on sales can be difficult to measure directly.

**Sales promotion**: short-term incentives added on top of advertising to encourage customers to buy now rather than delay their purchase. Examples include money-off coupons, "buy one, get one free" (BOGOF) offers, competitions, free samples, and loyalty point schemes. Sales promotions are effective at generating a quick, short-term boost in sales, but if overused they can damage a brand's image (making it seem cheap) and reduce profit margins.

**Personal selling**: direct, often face-to-face, communication between a salesperson and a potential customer. This allows the message to be tailored precisely to the individual customer's questions and concerns, and is especially valuable for expensive, complex, or technical products where customers need detailed advice before buying (for example, cars, insurance, or business-to-business equipment sales). Personal selling is, however, typically far more expensive per customer reached than mass advertising.

**Direct marketing**: promotional material sent directly to targeted individual customers, based on data the business already holds about them (such as past purchase history), for example through email marketing, direct mail, or targeted app notifications. Direct marketing allows for a high degree of personalisation and can be relatively low-cost per contact, but depends on the business holding accurate, up-to-date, and appropriately consented customer data.

**Public relations (PR)**: activities designed to build and protect a positive public image of a business, often through free (unpaid, or "earned") media coverage rather than paid advertising space, such as press releases, media events, or responses to news stories. Effective PR can build significant trust and credibility because it is delivered through an independent source rather than the business itself, but a business has much less control over exactly what is said about it compared with paid advertising.

**Sponsorship**: paying to be associated with an event, team, individual, or organisation, in order to build brand awareness and associate the business's image with the qualities of what it sponsors (such as excitement, success, or health). Sponsorship can build long-term brand recognition, especially when linked to a major, widely watched event, but is often a very significant fixed cost commitment, frequently over multiple years.

### Above-the-Line vs Below-the-Line Promotion
Promotional methods are often classified into two broad categories:

- **Above-the-line (ATL) promotion**: promotion through independent, paid-for media that the business does not itself control — most commonly, advertising through television, radio, print, billboards, and online display advertising. The business pays for space or airtime but does not control the surrounding content.
- **Below-the-line (BTL) promotion**: promotion that a business organises and controls directly, rather than paying an independent media outlet. This includes sales promotion, direct marketing, personal selling, and much of PR and sponsorship activity, since the business itself designs and runs these activities.

This distinction is useful because it highlights a trade-off: above-the-line methods can reach very large, broad audiences but offer less control and often less precise targeting, while below-the-line methods usually allow more targeted, controllable communication with a specific customer group, sometimes at the cost of reaching a smaller overall audience.

### Digital and Social Media Promotion
The growth of the internet and smartphones has created an entire additional category of promotional methods that cut across, and increasingly dominate, the traditional promotional mix. Digital and social media promotion includes: paid advertising on social media platforms and search engines (which can be very precisely targeted by demographic, location, and even individual browsing behaviour), a business's own website and social media accounts (a low-cost, direct channel to communicate with customers), email marketing (a form of direct marketing), and **influencer marketing**, where a business pays or otherwise incentivises an individual with an established online following to promote its product to their audience.

Digital and social media promotion has grown rapidly because it typically allows far more precise targeting of a chosen audience than traditional mass media, can be significantly cheaper to enter than television or national print advertising (making it especially accessible to small businesses), and provides detailed, immediate data on how customers are responding (such as how many people viewed, clicked, or purchased as a result of an advert), which is more difficult to measure with traditional above-the-line advertising.

### Factors Affecting Choice of Promotional Method
Several factors influence which promotional method, or combination of methods, a business chooses:

- **Cost and finance available**: television advertising and major sponsorship deals require very large budgets, generally putting them out of reach for small businesses, which are more likely to rely on lower-cost methods such as social media promotion, local sales promotions, or personal selling.
- **Nature of the product**: complex, technical, or high-value products (such as industrial machinery or financial services) often benefit most from personal selling, where detailed explanation and reassurance can be given directly; simple, low-cost, frequently purchased products are more often promoted through advertising and sales promotion aimed at a mass market.
- **Target market and its media habits**: a business must choose promotional methods that will actually reach its target customer segment (Chapter 33) — for example, a product aimed at teenagers is likely to be promoted heavily through social media and influencer marketing, whereas a product aimed at an older demographic may still rely more on television or print advertising.
- **Stage of the product life cycle**: heavier advertising spending is common at the introduction stage of the product life cycle (Chapter 36) to build initial awareness, while sales promotions are frequently used as an extension strategy to boost sales again once a product reaches maturity.
- **Legal restrictions**: some products (such as tobacco, and in many countries alcohol or gambling) face legal restrictions or outright bans on how, where, or to whom they can be advertised, which restricts the promotional methods available.
- **Desired brand image**: a premium brand seeking to maintain an exclusive, high-quality image may avoid promotional methods such as frequent price-based sales promotions, which can undermine perceived quality, and instead rely more on carefully controlled advertising, sponsorship of prestigious events, and PR.

**Common exam pitfall**: students often assume "above-the-line" simply means "expensive" and "below-the-line" means "cheap," but the real distinction is about independent paid media (ATL) versus business-controlled promotion (BTL) — some below-the-line activities, such as a large-scale direct mail campaign, can still be very costly. Students should also avoid treating "promotion" and "marketing" as synonyms: promotion is only one of the four elements of the marketing mix (alongside product, price and place), not the whole of marketing.

Cross-reference: promotional choices must stay consistent with pricing strategy (Chapter 37) — a heavily discounted sales promotion, for example, should not undermine a price-skimming strategy aimed at premium early adopters — and with the chosen distribution channels (Chapter 38), since customers must be able to actually purchase the product through the channel being promoted to them.

## Case Studies
**Company/context:** Coca-Cola, soft drinks manufacturer, United States (global multinational).
**Situation:** Coca-Cola has used sponsorship as a core promotional tool for decades, including long-running sponsorship of the Olympic Games and major football tournaments such as the FIFA World Cup, alongside globally recognised advertising campaigns (such as its long-standing Christmas advertising) designed to build broad brand awareness and positive emotional association with the brand across many countries and cultures.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates above-the-line advertising and sponsorship used together as part of an integrated promotional mix to build and sustain global brand recognition over a very long period.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse why sponsorship of major global sporting events has been an effective promotional method for a business like Coca-Cola." (AO3)

**Company/context:** Gymshark, fitness apparel retailer, United Kingdom (originated as a small start-up, now an international business).
**Situation:** Gymshark grew rapidly from a small start-up founded in a garage into a global fitness apparel brand largely through digital and social media promotion, including sending free products to fitness influencers on platforms such as Instagram and YouTube in exchange for promotion to their followers, rather than relying on traditional above-the-line advertising such as television.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how digital and social media promotion, including influencer marketing, has allowed even a small business with a limited initial budget to build a large, engaged target audience without traditional mass-media advertising spend.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the extent to which influencer marketing can be as effective as traditional above-the-line advertising for building a new brand." (AO3/AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically has a limited promotional budget, so it is more likely to rely on lower-cost below-the-line and digital methods, such as local sales promotions, word-of-mouth, personal selling, and targeted social media posts, which can still reach a relevant local or niche audience effectively without the cost of national advertising. A large or multinational business is more likely to be able to afford, and to benefit from, a much wider promotional mix, including expensive above-the-line advertising campaigns across multiple countries, long-term sponsorship deals with major global events, and dedicated PR teams to manage its corporate image across international media — while still using digital and social media promotion alongside these traditional methods to reach specific customer segments precisely.
