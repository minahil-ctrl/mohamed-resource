## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Market research | The process of gathering, recording and analysing data about customers, competitors and the market for a good or service | A phone manufacturer surveys teenagers before designing a new handset |
| Primary research (field research) | The collection of new, first-hand data specifically for the business's own purpose | A café owner hands out questionnaires to passers-by |
| Secondary research (desk research) | The use of data that already exists, collected by someone else for a different original purpose | A start-up reads a government report on national coffee consumption |
| Qualitative data | Data based on opinions, attitudes and feelings; descriptive rather than numerical | Comments from a focus group about a new perfume's scent |
| Quantitative data | Data that is numerical and can be measured and statistically analysed | 62% of survey respondents said they would buy the product |
| Questionnaire/survey | A set of written questions used to gather primary data from a sample of people | An online survey asking customers to rate a product 1–5 |
| Focus group | A small group of people brought together to discuss their opinions on a product, service or idea, guided by a moderator | Ten teenagers discuss a new snack food's packaging |
| Consumer panel | A group of consumers who provide feedback repeatedly over time, e.g. by keeping a diary of purchases | Households recording weekly grocery purchases for a research firm |
| Observation | Watching and recording consumer behaviour without direct interaction | Counting footfall past a shop window at different times of day |
| Sample | A group of people selected from the target population to take part in research, intended to represent the whole population | 200 shoppers selected from a city of 500,000 |
| Sampling | The process of choosing who will be surveyed, from the total target population | Choosing every 10th customer who enters a store |
| Random sampling | A sampling method where every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected | Names drawn from a computerised list at random |
| Quota sampling | A sampling method where researchers select a fixed number of people from specific groups (e.g. age, gender) to match the population's known proportions | Interviewing 50 men and 50 women to match a 50/50 population split |
| Stratified sampling | A sampling method that divides the population into sub-groups (strata) by a shared characteristic, then randomly samples proportionally from each | Sampling students proportionally from each year group in a school |
| Market segment | A distinct group of customers within a market who share similar characteristics and needs (fully developed in Chapter 35) | Teenagers versus retirees within the smartphone market |
| Test marketing | Trialling a new product in a limited geographic area before a full national or global launch | Releasing a new drink flavour in one region before a country-wide rollout |
| Market mapping | A visual diagram that positions competitors' products against two key variables (e.g. price and quality) | Plotting supermarket brands from "budget" to "premium" and "narrow range" to "wide range" |
| Bias (in research) | A distortion in results caused by an unrepresentative sample, leading questions, or flawed methodology | Only surveying people outside a gym about fitness habits |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Every successful product you can name — from a new flavour of instant noodles in Lahore to the latest iPhone — began with a business trying to answer one question: what do customers actually want? Get market research wrong, and even a well-funded company can fail spectacularly, as Coca-Cola discovered in 1985 when "New Coke" was rejected by loyal customers despite passing taste tests. Get it right, and a small business with almost no budget can still make confident, evidence-based decisions instead of guessing. Market research is the bridge between an idea and a product people will actually buy.

## Core Content

### The Purpose of Market Research
Market research exists to reduce the risk and uncertainty involved in business decision-making. Specifically, it helps a business to:
1. **Identify customer needs and wants** — what features, price points, and quality levels customers are looking for.
2. **Identify the target market** — who is most likely to buy the product (age, income, location, lifestyle).
3. **Predict future trends** — spotting changes in tastes, technology, or competitor behaviour before they cause problems.
4. **Reduce the risk of business failure** — a new product launched without research is essentially a guess; research replaces guesswork with evidence.
5. **Assess the level of competition** — understanding what rivals offer and at what price.
6. **Inform decisions across the marketing mix** — research findings feed directly into decisions on Product, Price, Place and Promotion (Chapters 36–39).

It is essential to note that market research does not guarantee success — it reduces risk but cannot eliminate it, because consumer tastes can change quickly and samples are never perfectly representative of the whole population.

### Primary (Field) Research
Primary research involves collecting original, first-hand data for a specific purpose. It is sometimes called field research because it is gathered "in the field" directly from consumers.

**Methods of primary research:**
- **Questionnaires/surveys**: structured lists of questions, distributed on paper, by phone, online, or face-to-face. Can generate both quantitative data (closed questions, e.g. multiple choice or rating scales) and qualitative data (open questions, e.g. "What did you dislike about this product?").
- **Interviews**: one-to-one conversations, usually more detailed than a questionnaire, generating richer qualitative data but from fewer people.
- **Focus groups**: small groups (typically 6–10 people) discussing a product or idea under the guidance of a moderator. Useful for exploring reactions to new concepts, packaging, or advertising in depth.
- **Consumer panels**: a fixed group of consumers tracked over an extended period, useful for spotting changes in behaviour over time (e.g. brand-switching).
- **Observation**: watching consumer behaviour directly, e.g. counting how many people pick up a product in a supermarket aisle, without asking them questions. This avoids the problem of people giving inaccurate or dishonest answers, but cannot explain *why* people behave as they do.
- **Test marketing**: launching a product in a limited area or to a limited group before a wider rollout, to observe real purchasing behaviour rather than stated intentions.

**Advantages of primary research:** it is specific to the business's exact needs, up to date, and can be kept confidential from competitors (giving a competitive advantage).

**Disadvantages of primary research:** it is usually expensive and time-consuming to design and carry out, requires expertise to avoid bias, and a small sample may not be representative of the whole market.

### Secondary (Desk) Research
Secondary research uses data that has already been collected and published by someone else, for a different original purpose. It is called desk research because it can typically be gathered without leaving the office.

**Sources of secondary research:**
- **Internal sources**: the business's own past sales records, accounts, and customer databases.
- **External sources**: government publications and census data, trade/industry association reports, newspapers and market research reports (e.g. Mintel, Euromonitor), competitor websites and annual reports, and academic or NGO studies.

**Advantages of secondary research:** it is much cheaper and faster to obtain than primary research (often free or low-cost), and it can provide a broad, industry-wide or national picture that no single business could generate alone.

**Disadvantages of secondary research:** it may be out of date, was not collected for this business's specific purpose (so may not exactly fit its needs), and is often available to competitors too, so provides no unique advantage.

### Qualitative versus Quantitative Data
- **Quantitative data** is numerical: percentages, sales figures, ratings, frequencies. It is easy to analyse statistically, present in charts, and compare over time, but it does not explain the reasons behind consumer behaviour.
- **Qualitative data** is descriptive: opinions, feelings, and explanations. It gives businesses a much richer understanding of *why* consumers think or act as they do, but it is harder to analyse objectively, harder to compare across large numbers of people, and more open to researcher interpretation/bias.

Good market research typically combines both: quantitative data to establish patterns (e.g. "70% prefer flavour A") and qualitative data to explain them (e.g. focus group comments revealing flavour A "tastes more natural").

### Sampling
Because it is normally impossible or far too expensive to survey an entire target population, businesses select a **sample** — a smaller group intended to represent the whole. The quality of the sample directly affects how reliable and useful the research findings are.

- **Random sampling**: every individual in the population has an equal chance of selection (e.g. computer-generated random selection from a customer database). Reduces researcher bias but may still, by chance, produce an unrepresentative sample, especially if the sample size is small.
- **Quota sampling**: the researcher decides in advance how many people from each relevant group (e.g. age band, gender) should be interviewed, then fills each quota — often by approaching people in the street until the quota is met. Faster and cheaper than random sampling, but the actual individuals within each quota are chosen non-randomly, which can introduce bias.
- **Stratified sampling**: the population is divided into meaningful sub-groups (strata) — such as age groups or income bands — and a random sample is then taken from each stratum, in proportion to its size in the overall population. This produces a highly representative sample but requires good background data on the population's structure and is more complex to organise.

**Common exam pitfall**: students often confuse quota and stratified sampling. The key distinguishing feature is that stratified sampling uses **random selection within each group**, whereas quota sampling allows the researcher to select individuals non-randomly (e.g. simply stopping people in the street) as long as the quota numbers are met.

### Market Mapping
A market map is a diagram used to visually position a business's own products against competitors along two chosen variables — most commonly price and quality, but the axes can be any two relevant characteristics (e.g. "traditional to modern" or "narrow range to wide range"). Market mapping helps a business identify **gaps in the market** — positions with few or no competitors — as well as understand how crowded a particular market segment is. It is often used as an early step in deciding where a new product should be positioned, directly feeding into Chapters 35 (Segmentation) and 36 (Product).

### Choosing Appropriate Research Methods
The best choice of research method depends on:
- **Cost and available budget** — a small business will often rely heavily on cheap secondary research and simple, low-cost primary methods like an online survey, whereas a large multinational can afford commissioned market research agencies, extensive focus groups, and international test marketing.
- **Time available** — research must fit the decision-making timeline; test marketing takes far longer than a quick online survey.
- **The type of decision being made** — decisions about, for example, brand image and emotional appeal may need qualitative methods (focus groups, interviews), while decisions about pricing thresholds may need quantitative survey data.
- **The need for accuracy versus speed** — a bigger, well-designed sample gives more reliable results but takes longer and costs more.

Cross-reference: findings from market research directly shape decisions covered in Chapter 35 (Market Segmentation), Chapter 36 (Product), Chapter 37 (Price), Chapter 38 (Place) and Chapter 39 (Promotion) — market research is the foundation on which the entire marketing mix is built.

## Case Studies
**Company/context:** Coca-Cola, beverages, United States (global brand) — the "New Coke" launch, 1985.
**Situation:** Coca-Cola conducted extensive blind taste tests before launching a reformulated recipe ("New Coke"), and the data showed consumers preferred the new, sweeter taste over both original Coca-Cola and Pepsi. Coca-Cola discontinued the original formula and launched New Coke nationally. The public reaction was intensely negative — consumers felt an emotional attachment to the original product that the taste-test data had not captured — and within roughly three months the company reintroduced the original formula as "Coca-Cola Classic."
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates the limits of quantitative research (blind taste tests) when it fails to capture qualitative, emotional factors such as brand loyalty and nostalgia; shows that research method choice must match what is really being decided.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate whether Coca-Cola's market research method (blind taste testing) was appropriate for deciding whether to change a globally established brand's recipe." (AO3/AO4)

**Company/context:** Jollibee, fast food, Philippines (multinational, emerging-market origin).
**Situation:** Jollibee built its menu and expansion strategy around continuous local market research into Filipino taste preferences (notably sweeter, more locally-flavoured items such as its signature sweet-style spaghetti), which allowed it to outcompete larger multinational fast-food chains within its home market before expanding internationally to serve Filipino diaspora communities and, increasingly, wider local markets abroad.
**Link to theory:** Shows how ongoing primary and secondary research into local consumer tastes (rather than adopting a "one size fits all" global product) can be a source of sustained competitive advantage, and links directly to market segmentation.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the extent to which a growing business should rely on primary research into local tastes versus adopting a standardised product used in its other markets." (AO3/AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically relies on low-cost primary methods it can run itself — simple questionnaires, informal customer conversations, direct observation of footfall — combined with free or cheap secondary sources such as government statistics, because it lacks the budget for commissioned research. A large or multinational business can afford to commission specialist market research agencies, run large-scale surveys and focus groups across multiple countries, and use test marketing in selected regions before a full launch, giving it more reliable and more geographically representative data, but at significantly higher cost and over a longer timescale.
