## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Product | A good or service offered to satisfy a customer need or want, including its features, quality and design | A smartphone's specification, design and operating system |
| Product life cycle | A model showing the stages a product passes through from launch to withdrawal from the market, in terms of sales volume over time | A game console moving from introduction through to decline |
| Introduction stage | The first stage of the product life cycle, where a new product is launched, sales are low and growing slowly, and heavy promotional spending is common | A newly launched product with high advertising spend and low initial sales |
| Growth stage | The stage where sales rise rapidly as the product becomes more widely known and accepted | A product's sales climbing quickly after positive word of mouth |
| Maturity stage | The stage where sales growth slows and levels off as the market becomes saturated; competition is intense | Sales of an established product remaining broadly stable year to year |
| Saturation | A point in the maturity stage where the market is fully supplied and further sales growth is very difficult without extension strategies | Nearly every potential customer already owns the product or a competitor's version |
| Decline stage | The stage where sales fall, often due to changing tastes, new technology or new competitors | Sales of an outdated product falling as customers switch to newer alternatives |
| Extension strategy | Marketing techniques used to prolong a product's life, typically applied at or before the maturity/decline stages | Relaunching a product with new packaging and a new flavour |
| Product differentiation | Making a product distinct from competitors' products, through features, quality, branding or design | A brand emphasising a unique design feature no rival offers |
| Branding | Giving a product a distinct identity (name, logo, image) that differentiates it and builds customer recognition and loyalty | A recognisable logo appearing on all of a company's products |
| Unique Selling Point (USP) | A specific feature or benefit that makes a product different from, and preferable to, competitors' products | A vacuum cleaner advertised as "never loses suction" |
| Packaging | The physical material and design used to contain, protect, and present a product | Distinctive bottle shape used to identify a soft drink brand |
| Research and development (R&D) | The process of researching and developing new or improved products and processes | A pharmaceutical company researching a new drug formulation |
| Boston Matrix | A model that classifies a business's product portfolio into four categories (Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks/Problem Children, Dogs) based on market growth and market share | Classifying products across a company's whole range |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Products do not last forever — even the most iconic ones eventually fade unless a business actively manages their life cycle. Consider the humble Nokia 3310 mobile phone, once a market-dominating product, which declined almost to obsolescence as smartphones emerged, only for Nokia's brand to later attempt a nostalgic relaunch. Understanding where a product sits in its life cycle — and what to do about it — is one of the most powerful tools a business has for managing revenue and staying ahead of the competition.

## Core Content

### What Is "Product" in the Marketing Mix?
"Product" refers to everything about the good or service itself: its physical features, functionality, quality, design, branding, and packaging. It is the starting point of the marketing mix, because decisions about price, place and promotion are all built around what the product is and who it is designed for (linking back to Chapter 35, Market Segmentation).

### The Product Life Cycle
The product life cycle (PLC) is a model showing how a typical product's sales (and often profit) change over time, from launch to eventual withdrawal from the market. It has four main stages:

1. **Introduction**: the product is newly launched. Sales are low and grow slowly as customers become aware of it. Costs are high due to research and development, and heavy promotional spending is usually needed to build awareness. Profit is often negative at this stage, since costs exceed revenue.
2. **Growth**: sales increase rapidly as the product gains wider acceptance and awareness spreads. Competitors may begin to enter the market having seen its success. Profit typically turns positive and rises, as sales revenue grows faster than costs (which may fall due to economies of scale — see Unit 5).
3. **Maturity**: sales growth slows down and levels off, as most potential customers have already bought the product and the market becomes saturated. Competition is usually at its most intense in this stage, often leading to price competition. Profit is typically at its highest in absolute terms during maturity, even though sales growth has stopped, because production is efficient and marketing costs may be lower relative to revenue than in the introduction stage.
4. **Decline**: sales fall, often because of changing customer tastes, new technology, or newer competing products. Profit falls, and a business must decide whether to withdraw the product, sell it off, or attempt to extend its life.

**Worked interpretation point**: on a product life cycle graph, the sales curve typically resembles an elongated "S" shape when plotted correctly (slow initial rise, steep rise through growth, flattening at maturity, then decline), while a separate profit curve usually peaks during late growth/early maturity and can turn negative again during decline.

### Extension Strategies
Because no business wants its products to reach decline and be withdrawn if this can be avoided, businesses use **extension strategies** — marketing actions designed to prolong a product's maturity stage and delay or prevent decline. Extension strategies typically involve modifying one or more elements of the marketing mix, and include:
- **New/updated packaging** to refresh the product's image.
- **Price adjustments**, such as a temporary price reduction to stimulate demand or price changes to appeal to a new segment.
- **Finding new markets or market segments** for the existing product (e.g. exporting to a new country, or repositioning towards a new age group).
- **Finding new uses for the existing product**.
- **Product modification/improvement**, such as adding new features, flavours, or variants without creating an entirely new product.
- **Increased or refreshed promotion**, such as a new advertising campaign or sponsorship deal.

Extension strategies do not create a brand-new product life cycle; rather, they are shown on the PLC diagram as an upward "bump" branching off the sales curve at or just before the maturity stage, temporarily lifting sales again before the curve eventually declines once more.

### Product Differentiation and Branding
In competitive markets, products are rarely sold on their basic function alone — businesses must differentiate their product from rivals' offerings to attract and retain customers. Differentiation can be achieved through:
- **Unique features or technology** (a genuine Unique Selling Point, or USP).
- **Superior or perceived-superior quality**.
- **Design and aesthetics**.
- **Branding** — building a distinct name, logo and reputation that customers recognise and trust, which can support premium pricing and customer loyalty even where the underlying product is functionally similar to competitors'.
- **Packaging** — using distinctive, memorable, or premium-feeling packaging to stand out on shelves and reinforce brand identity.

Strong branding and differentiation reduce a product's exposure to direct price competition, because customers become willing to pay for the brand itself, not just the underlying good.

### Research and Development (R&D) and New Product Development
New products are typically developed through a structured process: generating ideas (often informed by market research, Chapter 33), screening ideas for feasibility, developing and testing prototypes, test marketing (trialling in a limited area), and finally full launch. Research and development spending is highest relative to sales in industries where technology or formulation change quickly, such as pharmaceuticals and consumer electronics, since these industries rely heavily on continual innovation to avoid rapid decline of existing products.

### The Boston Matrix (Product Portfolio Analysis)
Many businesses sell a range (portfolio) of products, each potentially at a different stage of its own life cycle. The **Boston Matrix** (Boston Consulting Group Matrix) is a tool used to analyse a product portfolio by classifying each product according to two dimensions: its **market growth rate** and its **relative market share**. This produces four categories:
- **Stars**: high market share in a high-growth market. Require continued investment but have strong future potential (often linked to products in the growth stage of the PLC).
- **Cash Cows**: high market share in a low-growth (mature) market. Generate strong, reliable cash flow with relatively low investment needed (linked to the maturity stage of the PLC).
- **Question Marks (Problem Children)**: low market share in a high-growth market. Uncertain future — could become Stars with further investment, or could fail (often linked to the introduction stage).
- **Dogs**: low market share in a low-growth market. Usually generate little profit and may be considered for withdrawal (linked to the decline stage).

A well-balanced product portfolio typically uses cash generated by Cash Cows to fund investment in Stars and promising Question Marks, while phasing out Dogs. The Boston Matrix is a useful complement to the product life cycle because it allows a business to view all of its products together rather than just one product's life cycle in isolation.

**Common exam pitfall**: students often forget that extension strategies are applied *at or before maturity*, not after decline has already set in — by the time a product is deep into decline, an extension strategy may be too late to be effective. Students should also avoid confusing the product life cycle (which tracks one product's sales over time) with the Boston Matrix (which compares multiple products in a portfolio at one point in time).

Cross-reference: pricing decisions during each PLC stage link directly to Chapter 37 (Price) — for example, penetration pricing is often used at introduction, while price reductions are a common extension strategy at maturity.

## Case Studies
**Company/context:** Lego, toy manufacturing, Denmark (global multinational).
**Situation:** Lego experienced a significant sales decline in the early 2000s as it diversified away from its core brick-based product into areas such as clothing and theme parks, moving away from what had made it distinctive. The company later recovered by refocusing on its core product and using extension strategies including new themed sets tied to licensed franchises (such as Star Wars and Harry Potter), new product lines aimed at adult builders, and expansion into digital content and video games.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates both the risk of a product moving into decline and the successful use of multiple extension strategies (new variants, new segments, new promotional tie-ins) to revive sales.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse how Lego used extension strategies to move its core product back from decline toward renewed growth." (AO3)

**Company/context:** Nokia, mobile phones, Finland (formerly a dominant global multinational in mobile handsets).
**Situation:** Nokia's classic handsets, including the widely recognised Nokia 3310, dominated the mobile phone market during the growth and maturity stages of that product category, but Nokia's mobile phone business entered a steep decline stage as smartphones from competitors reshaped the market and Nokia was slow to adapt its product accordingly, eventually leading to the sale of its mobile phone business.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the decline stage of the product life cycle at an industry level and the risks of failing to innovate the product (via R&D) in response to a changing market.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the extent to which failure to invest in research and development can explain a product's move into the decline stage." (AO3/AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often has a narrow product range, sometimes based around a single core product, meaning that managing that one product's life cycle carefully (through timely, low-cost extension strategies such as new flavours, seasonal variants, or local promotions) is critical to the survival of the whole business. A large or multinational business typically manages a wide portfolio of products at different life cycle stages simultaneously, and can use tools such as the Boston Matrix to balance investment across the portfolio — using cash generated by mature, high-share "Cash Cow" products to fund research, development and promotion of newer "Star" or "Question Mark" products.
