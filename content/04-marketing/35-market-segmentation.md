| Market segment | A distinct sub-group of a market whose members share similar characteristics and are likely to respond similarly to marketing | Teenagers within the footwear market |

| Psychographic segmentation | Segmenting a market based on lifestyle, interests, values, attitudes or personality | An outdoor brand targeting customers who value adventure and sustainability |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
No single product can please every customer on earth — a car designed to be cheap, luxurious, fuel-efficient and enormous all at once simply cannot exist. Successful businesses instead choose their customers deliberately, and design their entire marketing mix around that chosen group. This is why Nike sells shoes ranging from ultra-lightweight racing spikes to cushioned lifestyle sneakers, and why a bank might offer a basic no-frills account for students alongside a premium wealth-management service for high-income clients. Market segmentation is how businesses decide exactly who they are trying to sell to — and getting it right shapes every other marketing decision that follows.

## Core Content

### What Is Market Segmentation?
Market segmentation is the process of dividing a broad, diverse market into smaller sub-groups (segments) of consumers who share similar characteristics, needs, or buying behaviour. Rather than trying to appeal to everyone in a market with one undifferentiated product, a business identifies specific segments and then selects one or more of them as its **target market** — the group its marketing mix will specifically be designed to appeal to.

Segmentation matters because customers within a market are rarely uniform: a market for "shoes" contains customers who want running performance, others who want fashion, others who want maximum comfort for standing all day at work, and others who want the lowest possible price. A single generic shoe cannot satisfy all of these needs equally well, so businesses segment the market and target specific groups more precisely.

### Bases of Market Segmentation
Markets can be segmented using several different criteria, often in combination. The main bases relevant at IGCSE level are:

**1. Demographic segmentation** — dividing the market by measurable population characteristics:
- *Age*: e.g. children's, teen, adult and senior product ranges.
- *Gender*: e.g. separate ranges of clothing or grooming products.
- *Family size/life stage*: e.g. family-sized versus single-serving food packaging.
- *Ethnicity or religion*: e.g. halal-certified food ranges, or hair-care products formulated for different hair types associated with different ethnic groups.

**2. Geographic segmentation** — dividing the market by location:
- *Country or region*: products adapted for different national tastes, laws, or climates.
- *Climate*: e.g. lighter fabrics marketed in tropical countries, insulated clothing in cold climates.
- *Urban versus rural*: e.g. compact appliances marketed in dense cities, larger-capacity versions marketed in rural areas.

**3. Income/socio-economic segmentation** — dividing the market by income level, wealth, or occupation-based social grouping:
- Businesses often create a range of products at different price points to capture customers across income bands, e.g. budget, mid-range and premium/luxury versions of the same core product (a strategy sometimes called "good-better-best").

**4. Psychographic segmentation** — dividing the market by lifestyle, values, interests, or personality:
- E.g. targeting environmentally conscious consumers, health-and-fitness-focused consumers, or status-conscious consumers, regardless of their specific age or income.

**5. Behavioural segmentation** (an additional, more advanced basis worth introducing) — dividing the market by how customers behave towards the product, such as how often they buy it, how loyal they are to the brand, or the occasion on which they buy it (e.g. everyday use versus special-occasion purchases).

In practice, businesses frequently combine multiple bases — for example, targeting "higher-income, environmentally conscious adults aged 30–50 in urban areas" blends income, psychographic, demographic and geographic segmentation together into one precise target market.

### Benefits of Market Segmentation
- Allows a business to design a marketing mix that closely matches the specific needs of a chosen group, increasing the likelihood of purchase.
- Can reduce direct competition if a business successfully targets an under-served niche segment.
- Enables premium pricing where a segment has specific needs it is willing to pay more to have met.
- Improves the effectiveness and efficiency of promotional spending, since advertising can be targeted precisely at the segment most likely to respond, rather than wasted on the whole market.
- Supports business growth through developing new products for newly identified segments (linked to Ansoff's Matrix and product development, covered elsewhere in the specification).

### Drawbacks and Risks of Market Segmentation
- Researching and identifying segments accurately can be costly and time-consuming (links to Chapter 33, Market Research).
- Producing multiple versions of a product for different segments can reduce the economies of scale available from mass production (linked to Unit 5, Operations), raising unit costs.
- A segment may turn out to be too small to be profitable, or may shrink or disappear if tastes change.
- Over-segmentation can dilute a brand's identity or confuse customers if too many slightly different versions of a product are offered.
- Focusing tightly on one segment increases vulnerability if that segment's needs change or if a larger competitor decides to target the same niche.

### Segmentation and the Marketing Mix
Once a target market/segment has been chosen, it directly shapes decisions across the whole marketing mix:
- **Product**: features and quality level are designed to match the segment's specific needs.
- **Price**: set according to what the target segment is willing and able to pay.
- **Place**: distribution channels chosen to be where the target segment actually shops.
- **Promotion**: messages, media channels and tone chosen to appeal specifically to that segment (e.g. advertising on platforms the segment actually uses).

**Common exam pitfall**: students often confuse "market segment" (a group of customers with shared characteristics) with "target market" (the specific segment(s) a business has chosen to focus on) — a segment exists whether or not any business targets it; a target market is the segment(s) a specific business has actively chosen to serve. Students should also avoid vague answers like "they target young people" without specifying which segmentation base (age) and ideally combining it with another relevant base for full marks.

## Case Studies
**Company/context:** Toyota Motor Corporation, automobiles, Japan (global multinational).
**Situation:** Toyota segments the car market by income/socio-economic status and lifestyle by offering distinct brands and model ranges — from affordable, practical mass-market Toyota models, through mid-range SUVs and hybrids aimed at families and environmentally conscious buyers (e.g. the Prius, historically marketed toward eco-conscious consumers), up to the separate luxury Lexus brand aimed at high-income customers seeking premium features and status.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates how one company can use several segmentation bases (income, psychographic/lifestyle) simultaneously and design an entirely separate brand and marketing mix for each targeted segment.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse why Toyota chose to create an entirely separate brand (Lexus) rather than simply adding a premium model to the existing Toyota range." (AO3)

**Company/context:** Nike Inc., sportswear, United States (global multinational, with product ranges also relevant to smaller regional sports retailers).
**Situation:** Nike segments its footwear and apparel market by demographic factors (age and gender-specific ranges), psychographic factors (performance athletes versus casual "athleisure" lifestyle customers), and behavioural factors (loyalty programme members via the Nike App/Nike Membership, offering tailored promotions based on purchasing behaviour).
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the combined use of demographic, psychographic and behavioural segmentation within a single global brand, and shows how segmentation supports targeted promotion through digital channels.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the benefits and risks to Nike of segmenting its market so extensively across age, lifestyle and purchasing behaviour." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often has neither the budget nor the production flexibility to serve many segments at once, so it typically identifies and focuses on one clearly defined niche segment where it can compete effectively against larger rivals — for example, a local bakery specialising in gluten-free products for a health-conscious niche. A large or multinational business usually has the resources to segment the market extensively and serve multiple segments simultaneously, often through entirely separate product ranges or sub-brands (as Toyota does with Lexus), allowing it to capture a much larger overall share of the total market while still tailoring its offer to each group.
