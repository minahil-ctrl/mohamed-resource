## Key Terms
| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Price | The amount of money a customer must pay to acquire a product, and the element of the marketing mix that most directly generates revenue | A retailer setting a shelf price for a new snack product |
| Cost-plus pricing | Setting a price by calculating the cost per unit of producing the product and adding a fixed percentage or amount as profit (mark-up) | A furniture maker adding a 40% mark-up onto the cost of materials and labour per item |
| Penetration pricing | Setting a deliberately low price when a product is launched, in order to gain market share quickly, with the intention of raising price later | A new streaming service launching at a low introductory monthly fee |
| Price skimming | Setting a high price when a product is launched to maximise revenue from customers willing to pay a premium, before gradually lowering the price over time | A new games console launching at a high price before falling in later years |
| Competitive pricing | Setting a price mainly by reference to competitors' prices for similar products, rather than based on a business's own costs | A petrol station matching the price displayed at a rival station across the road |
| Psychological pricing | Setting a price to influence customer perception, such as pricing just below a round number to make the price seem lower than it is | Pricing an item at $9.99 rather than $10.00 |
| Price discrimination | Charging different prices to different customer groups or at different times for the same, or almost the same, product | A cinema charging a lower ticket price for a matinee showing than an evening showing |
| Loss leader pricing | Setting the price of a product deliberately low, sometimes below cost, to attract customers into a store in the hope they buy other, more profitable items | A supermarket selling bread very cheaply to draw in shoppers |
| Price elasticity of demand | A measure of how responsive the quantity demanded of a product is to a change in its price | A luxury good with few close substitutes, whose demand changes relatively little when price rises |
| Mark-up | The amount added to the cost per unit of a product to determine its selling price under cost-plus pricing | A cost per unit of $20 with a $10 mark-up giving a selling price of $30 |
| Market positioning | The place a product occupies in customers' minds relative to competing products, often in terms of price and quality | A brand positioned as a premium, high-price, high-quality option in its market |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Price is the only element of the marketing mix that directly brings money into a business — product, place and promotion all cost money to deliver, but price is where that spending is recovered. Get it wrong and the consequences are immediate: price too high and a new product may never gain customers at all, price too low and a business may sell in huge volumes while barely covering its costs. Apple's iPhone launches are a well-known example of skimming a high initial price from early adopters, while budget airlines such as Ryanair have built entire business models around low headline prices supported by extensive extra charges. Pricing decisions are rarely just about "what feels fair" — they are calculated, strategic choices tied directly to a business's objectives.

## Core Content

### What Is "Price" in the Marketing Mix?
Price is the amount a customer pays for a product, and it is the marketing mix element most closely tied to a business's revenue and profit (linking to Chapter 27, revenue, costs and profit). Because it also communicates value and quality to customers, a pricing decision is never purely mathematical — it must fit the product itself (Chapter 36), the target market segment (Chapter 33), the distribution channel used (Chapter 38) and the promotional image being built (Chapter 39). A business's chosen pricing strategy should be consistent with its overall marketing objectives, whether these are to maximise market share, maximise profit, or build a premium brand image.

### Pricing Strategies

**Cost-plus pricing**
Cost-plus pricing calculates the price by first working out the cost of producing one unit of the product (including both variable costs, such as materials, and an allocated share of fixed costs, such as rent), and then adding a mark-up — a fixed percentage or fixed amount of profit — on top.

*Formula:* Selling price = Cost per unit + Mark-up

*Worked example:* A bakery calculates that each cake costs $8 to make (ingredients, packaging and a share of oven/rent costs). It wants a 50% mark-up on cost.
- Mark-up = $8 × 0.50 = $4
- Selling price = $8 + $4 = **$12 per cake**

Cost-plus pricing is simple to calculate and guarantees a profit margin on every unit sold, provided the units are actually sold at that price. Its main weakness is that it ignores what competitors charge and what customers are actually willing to pay — a business could set a cost-plus price that is far above or far below the market rate.

**Penetration pricing**
Penetration pricing sets a low price when a product is first launched (the introduction stage of the product life cycle — Chapter 36) in order to encourage trial, build market share quickly, and discourage competitors from entering. Once a sufficient customer base and brand loyalty have been established, the price is often raised gradually. This strategy suits products entering a market with existing close substitutes, where price is a key factor in customers' initial choice.

**Price skimming**
Price skimming sets a high initial price at launch, aimed at customers who are willing to pay a premium to be among the first to own an innovative or exclusive product (often called early adopters). As the initial demand from these customers is satisfied and competitors begin to enter the market, the price is gradually lowered to attract more price-sensitive customers. Skimming suits genuinely innovative products with little direct competition at launch, and allows a business to recover high research and development costs quickly.

**Competitive pricing**
Competitive pricing sets a price mainly with reference to what rival businesses charge for similar products, rather than starting from the business's own costs. A price may be set level with, just below, or just above a key competitor's price, depending on how the business wants to position itself. This strategy is common in markets with many similar competing products (such as fuel retailing or supermarket staples), where customers can easily compare prices and switch between suppliers.

**Psychological pricing**
Psychological pricing sets a price to influence how customers perceive it, exploiting the fact that customers often respond to the way a price is presented rather than purely to its numerical value. The most common example is pricing just below a round number (for example, $9.99 instead of $10.00), which can make a product appear noticeably cheaper than it is. Other examples include pricing a "premium" version of a product noticeably higher to signal superior quality.

**Price discrimination**
Price discrimination involves charging different prices to different groups of customers, or at different times, for essentially the same product, where the differences in price are not based on differences in the cost of supplying the product. This is possible when a business can separate customer groups (for example, by age, time of purchase, or location) and when those groups cannot easily resell the product to each other. Common examples include off-peak versus peak train fares, and lower ticket prices for students, older people or children compared with standard adult prices.

**Loss leader pricing**
A related strategy, often used by retailers with a wide range of products, is loss leader pricing: pricing one specific product very low (sometimes below its cost) to attract customers into a store, in the expectation that those customers will also buy other, more profitably priced items during the same visit.

### Factors Influencing Pricing Decisions

**Costs**
A business must ensure that, in the long run, its prices are sufficient to cover both variable and fixed costs and to generate a profit margin (see Chapter 27 and break-even analysis in Chapter 28). Even where a strategy such as penetration or loss leader pricing sets prices near or below cost in the short term, this can typically only be sustained temporarily.

**Competition**
The number of competitors, and how similar their products are, strongly affects how much control (or "pricing power") a business has. In a market with many competitors selling near-identical products, a business has little scope to charge more than the going rate without losing customers to rivals; in a market with few competitors or a highly differentiated product, a business has much greater freedom to set its own price.

**Demand and price elasticity of demand**
Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how sensitive the quantity customers demand is to a change in price. Where demand is price inelastic (customers keep buying even if price rises, often because there are few substitutes, as with addictive or essential products), a business can raise price with only a small fall in the quantity sold, increasing total revenue. Where demand is price elastic (customers are highly sensitive to price changes, often because there are many substitutes), even a small price rise can cause a large fall in the quantity sold, reducing total revenue. Businesses use their understanding of the price elasticity of demand for their product to judge how far prices can be moved without unacceptable falls in sales volume.

**Market positioning and brand image**
A business's chosen position in the market — for example, as a budget, mid-range, or premium/luxury supplier — strongly constrains its pricing choices. A premium brand generally must maintain a comparatively high price to remain consistent with its quality image; setting price too low can actually damage a premium brand by suggesting lower quality (undermining the perceived value the brand has built through promotion in Chapter 39).

**Stage of the product life cycle**
Pricing strategy commonly shifts as a product moves through its life cycle (Chapter 36): penetration or skimming strategies are typically used at introduction, competitive pricing often dominates during the maturity stage as rivals compete for a saturated market, and price cuts are a common extension strategy used to prolong sales as a product approaches decline.

**Common exam pitfall**: students often use "penetration" and "skimming" interchangeably, or confuse them with general "high price"/"low price" statements. Remember that penetration pricing is specifically a *low launch price used to build market share quickly*, while skimming is specifically a *high launch price used to maximise revenue from early adopters before gradually lowering it*. Also, price discrimination should not be confused simply with "different shops charging different prices" — it specifically means charging different prices to different customer groups or occasions for the *same* product by the *same* seller.

## Case Studies
**Company/context:** Apple Inc., consumer electronics, United States (global multinational).
**Situation:** Apple has consistently used price skimming when launching new iPhone models, setting a high initial price for the newest model aimed at customers willing to pay a premium to own the latest technology first, while continuing to sell older iPhone models at progressively lower prices alongside the new launch.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates price skimming in practice, and shows how a single business can run several pricing strategies for different products in its range simultaneously (new flagship model skimmed, older models priced more competitively).
**Discussion question:** "Analyse why price skimming may be an appropriate pricing strategy for a newly launched flagship smartphone." (AO3)

**Company/context:** Ryanair, budget airline, Ireland (large multinational operating across Europe).
**Situation:** Ryanair is well known for advertising very low headline ticket prices, while generating significant additional revenue from optional extras such as checked baggage, seat selection and priority boarding, which are charged separately. Ryanair's pricing for the same route and date also varies considerably depending on how far in advance a ticket is booked and how full the flight already is, reflecting active management of demand for each seat.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates penetration-style low headline pricing combined with elements of price discrimination between customers (based on booking time and how much they are willing to pay for a specific seat/date), and highlights the link between price elasticity of demand and revenue management.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the extent to which a very low headline price is an effective pricing strategy for an airline like Ryanair." (AO3/AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often has limited market research resources and less pricing power, meaning it may rely heavily on straightforward cost-plus pricing to guarantee a margin, or on competitive pricing to match well-known local rivals, since it typically cannot risk a loss-making penetration strategy for long. A large or multinational business is more likely to have the financial resources to sustain a loss-making penetration price for an extended period to build market share, the brand strength to support price skimming or premium positioning, and the customer data and market power needed to implement sophisticated price discrimination across different customer segments and markets.
