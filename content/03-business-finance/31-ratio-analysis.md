## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Ratio analysis | The technique of comparing figures from financial statements (expressed as percentages or simple ratios) to assess business performance | Comparing gross profit margin between two years |
| Profitability ratio | A ratio measuring how effectively a business converts revenue or capital into profit | Gross profit margin, net profit margin, ROCE |
| Liquidity ratio | A ratio measuring a business's ability to meet its short-term (current) liabilities | Current ratio, acid test ratio |
| Gross profit margin | Gross profit expressed as a percentage of revenue | A margin of 40% means 40p of every £1 of sales is gross profit |
| Net profit margin | Net profit expressed as a percentage of revenue | A margin of 15% means 15p of every £1 of sales is net profit |
| Return on capital employed (ROCE) | Net profit (before interest and tax) expressed as a percentage of capital employed, measuring overall efficiency of using invested capital | A ROCE of 12% compared against a bank's interest rate to judge worth of investment |
| Capital employed | The total long-term finance invested in a business, calculated as equity plus non-current liabilities | £100,000 equity + £34,000 non-current liabilities = £134,000 capital employed |
| Current ratio | A liquidity ratio comparing current assets to current liabilities | A current ratio of 2:1 means £2 of current assets for every £1 of current liabilities |
| Acid test (quick) ratio | A stricter liquidity ratio excluding inventory from current assets, testing immediate ability to pay short-term debts | An acid test ratio of 0.9:1 |
| Interfirm comparison | Comparing a business's ratios with those of a competitor or industry average | Comparing ROCE against a rival firm in the same sector |
| Trend analysis | Comparing a business's own ratios across several years to identify improvement or decline | A rising net profit margin over three consecutive years |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Two businesses can report exactly the same net profit figure — say £50,000 — yet be in completely different financial health: one might have earned it from £200,000 of sales, the other from £2 million. Raw profit figures alone, taken from the statement of comprehensive income (Chapter 29) and statement of financial position (Chapter 30), can be dangerously misleading on their own. Ratio analysis converts these raw figures into percentages and proportions that can be meaningfully compared — between years, between competitors, or against industry benchmarks — which is exactly why banks, investors, and company boards rely on ratios, not raw numbers, when judging financial performance.

## Core Content

### The Purpose of Ratio Analysis
**Ratio analysis** takes figures from the statement of comprehensive income and the statement of financial position and expresses them as percentages or proportions, allowing meaningful comparison:
- Between different **time periods** for the same business (trend analysis) — is performance improving or declining?
- Between **different businesses**, even of different sizes, since ratios remove the distortion of scale (interfirm comparison).
- Against **industry averages or targets**, to judge whether performance is strong, weak, or typical for the sector.

Ratios fall into two broad categories examined in this chapter: **profitability ratios**, which measure how effectively a business turns sales or investment into profit, and **liquidity ratios**, which measure a business's ability to meet its short-term debts.

### Profitability Ratios

#### 1. Gross Profit Margin
**Formula:** Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

This ratio shows what percentage of revenue remains after deducting the direct cost of the goods sold (cost of sales), before any other running expenses are considered. It reflects pricing strategy and control over the direct cost of production/purchasing.

#### 2. Net Profit Margin
**Formula:** Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

This ratio shows what percentage of revenue remains as profit after **all** costs (cost of sales and expenses) have been deducted. It reflects the overall efficiency of running the entire business, including overhead/expense control.

#### 3. Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)
**Formula:** ROCE = (Net Profit ÷ Capital Employed) × 100, where Capital Employed = Equity + Non-Current Liabilities

ROCE measures how efficiently a business generates profit from the total long-term capital invested in it (both the owners'/shareholders' equity and long-term borrowing). It is often compared to the interest rate a business could earn by simply investing its capital elsewhere (e.g. in a bank savings account or in a low-risk asset) — if ROCE is lower than the return available elsewhere, it raises the question of whether the capital is being used productively.

### Worked Example: "Northgate Electricals Ltd" (illustrative figures for a fictional business) — Profitability Ratios

Financial data extracted from Northgate Electricals Ltd's year-end statements:
- Revenue: **£400,000**
- Cost of Sales: **£240,000**
- Gross Profit: **£160,000**
- Net Profit: **£48,000**
- Equity: **£250,000**
- Non-Current Liabilities: **£50,000**

**Step 1 — Gross Profit Margin:**
Gross Profit Margin = (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100 = (£160,000 ÷ £400,000) × 100 = **40%**
*Interpretation:* For every £1 of sales, Northgate Electricals retains 40p as gross profit before other expenses are deducted. This reflects healthy pricing and cost-of-sales control relative to revenue.

**Step 2 — Net Profit Margin:**
Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100 = (£48,000 ÷ £400,000) × 100 = **12%**
*Interpretation:* Only 12p of every £1 of sales remains as net profit once all expenses are deducted — the gap between the 40% gross margin and the 12% net margin (28 percentage points) indicates that expenses are consuming a substantial share of gross profit, worth investigating further (e.g. wages, rent, marketing spend).

**Step 3 — Capital Employed:**
Capital Employed = Equity + Non-Current Liabilities = £250,000 + £50,000 = **£300,000**

**Step 4 — ROCE:**
ROCE = (Net Profit ÷ Capital Employed) × 100 = (£48,000 ÷ £300,000) × 100 = **16%**
*Interpretation:* Northgate Electricals generates a 16% return on the total long-term capital invested in it. If a bank savings account or a comparable low-risk investment were currently paying, say, 5% interest, a ROCE of 16% suggests the capital is being used considerably more productively within the business than it would be if withdrawn and invested elsewhere — a positive signal for shareholders and potential investors.

### Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity ratios test whether a business holds enough short-term resources to pay its short-term debts as they fall due — a business can be profitable (a good statement of comprehensive income) yet still fail if it runs out of liquid funds, linking directly to the working capital concept introduced in Chapter 30.

#### 4. Current Ratio
**Formula:** Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities (expressed as "X : 1")

This compares total current assets to total current liabilities. A commonly cited healthy benchmark is roughly **1.5:1 to 2:1**, meaning the business has £1.50–£2.00 of current assets for every £1 of current liabilities — though the ideal ratio varies by industry (e.g. supermarkets, which sell stock and collect cash very quickly, can operate safely with a lower ratio than a manufacturer holding large stockpiles).

#### 5. Acid Test (Quick) Ratio
**Formula:** Acid Test Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities (expressed as "X : 1")

This is a stricter test of immediate liquidity because it excludes **inventory**, which may take time to sell and convert into cash. A commonly cited healthy benchmark is roughly **1:1**, meaning the business could cover its current liabilities without needing to sell any stock at all.

### Worked Example: "Northgate Electricals Ltd" — Liquidity Ratios

Additional data from the statement of financial position:
- Current Assets: **£90,000** (of which Inventory = **£35,000**)
- Current Liabilities: **£60,000**

**Step 1 — Current Ratio:**
Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities = £90,000 ÷ £60,000 = **1.5 : 1**
*Interpretation:* Northgate Electricals has £1.50 of current assets for every £1 of current liabilities, sitting within the commonly cited healthy range of 1.5:1 to 2:1 — suggesting it should be able to meet its short-term obligations comfortably.

**Step 2 — Acid Test Ratio:**
Acid Test Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities = (£90,000 − £35,000) ÷ £60,000 = £55,000 ÷ £60,000 = **0.92 : 1**
*Interpretation:* Excluding inventory, Northgate Electricals has £0.92 of readily available current assets for every £1 of current liabilities — just below the commonly cited benchmark of 1:1. This suggests that if the business needed to pay all current liabilities immediately without selling any stock, it would fall slightly short, which could be a concern if inventory proves slow to sell. A stakeholder should compare this to previous years or competitors before drawing firm conclusions (see below).

### Using Ratios: Comparison Is Essential
A single ratio figure, viewed alone, tells a stakeholder very little. Ratios only become genuinely useful when compared:
- **Over time (trend analysis):** Is Northgate Electricals' net profit margin of 12% an improvement on last year's 9%, or a decline from 15%?
- **Between firms (interfirm comparison):** Is a ROCE of 16% strong or weak compared to a direct competitor in the same industry?
- **Against a benchmark or target:** Does the acid test ratio of 0.92:1 meet the business's own internal target, or the industry norm?

### Common Misconceptions and Exam Pitfalls
- Students frequently forget to **multiply by 100** when calculating a percentage-based ratio (gross profit margin, net profit margin, ROCE) — the raw decimal is not the final answer.
- Students often confuse the **current ratio** and **acid test ratio** — remember the acid test *excludes inventory*, making it the stricter test.
- A common error is treating a single ratio result as automatically "good" or "bad" without comparison — always compare across time, competitors, or a benchmark before evaluating.
- Students sometimes use the wrong profit figure — gross profit margin must use **gross** profit, net profit margin and ROCE must use **net** profit; mixing these up is a frequent, easily avoided mistake.
- ROCE requires **capital employed**, not total assets — remember Capital Employed = Equity + Non-Current Liabilities, not the full statement of financial position total.

### Limitations of Ratio Analysis
- Ratios are based on **historical data** from past financial statements and may not reflect current or future performance.
- Ratios depend on the **accuracy and consistency of the underlying accounts** — different accounting policies (e.g. depreciation methods, stock valuation) between businesses can distort comparisons.
- Ratios **ignore qualitative factors** such as staff morale, brand reputation, customer satisfaction, or the quality of management, all of which affect real business performance.
- **External factors** (economic conditions, exchange rates, competitor actions) affecting the figures are not visible in a ratio itself.
- Seasonal businesses may show liquidity ratios that look very different depending on which point in the year the statement of financial position was prepared, since it is only a snapshot (as noted in Chapter 30).

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Unilever plc, UK/Netherlands, consumer goods multinational (large business).
**Situation:** Unilever, like most large listed multinationals, publishes detailed annual financial statements from which analysts and investors routinely calculate profitability ratios (such as net profit margin) and ROCE to compare its performance year-on-year and against rival consumer goods companies such as Procter & Gamble and Nestlé, directly informing share price and investment decisions.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how large, publicly listed companies are subject to continuous ratio-based scrutiny from financial analysts, and how ROCE in particular is used by investors to judge whether a company is using shareholder capital efficiently compared to rivals in the same sector.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss why investors might place more importance on a company's ROCE than on its net profit margin alone when comparing two large consumer goods companies." (AO3)

**Company/context:** A small independent hardware store, Jamaica (small/emerging-market example, illustrative of common local retail businesses).
**Situation:** Small retail businesses such as independent hardware stores in Kingston often hold significant amounts of stock (inventory) relative to their cash and receivables, meaning their current ratio can look reasonably healthy while their acid test ratio reveals a much tighter liquidity position — a common and important distinction for owners deciding whether they can safely take on a new short-term loan or extend credit to customers.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the practical difference between the current ratio and the acid test ratio for a stock-heavy small business, and why relying on the current ratio alone can overstate a business's true short-term liquidity.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the usefulness of the acid test ratio, compared to the current ratio, for a small retail business owner deciding whether the business can safely take on further short-term borrowing." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically calculates ratios informally, often with the help of an accountant preparing year-end accounts, mainly to satisfy itself, a bank, or a tax authority that it remains solvent and reasonably profitable, and it rarely benchmarks itself formally against named competitors. A large business, particularly a public limited company, is subject to continuous, formal ratio analysis by professional financial analysts, institutional investors, and credit rating agencies, who publish detailed interfirm comparisons across an entire industry; because large businesses' shares are publicly traded, a decline in key ratios such as net profit margin or ROCE can immediately and significantly affect the company's share price, giving ratio analysis far higher stakes and visibility at this scale.
