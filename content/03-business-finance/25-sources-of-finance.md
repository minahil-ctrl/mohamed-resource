## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Finance | Money required by a business to set up, run day-to-day, or expand its operations | A café needs finance to buy ovens and pay rent before it earns revenue |
| Internal finance | Finance raised from within the business itself, without borrowing from outside | Using retained profit to buy a new delivery van |
| External finance | Finance raised from sources outside the business | Taking out a bank loan |
| Short-term finance | Finance needed for less than one year, usually for working capital | An overdraft to cover a temporary cash shortage |
| Long-term finance | Finance needed for more than one year, usually for fixed assets or expansion | A mortgage to buy a new factory |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business after tax and owner withdrawals, reinvested rather than distributed | A sole trader ploughing back last year's profit into new stock |
| Sale of assets | Raising cash by selling unused or unwanted fixed assets | Selling an old machine no longer needed |
| Owner's savings/capital | Personal money invested by the owner(s) into the business | A sole trader using personal savings to start up |
| Share capital | Money raised by a limited company selling shares (ownership stakes) to investors | Facebook raising billions in its 2012 IPO |
| Loan capital | Money borrowed, usually from a bank, repaid with interest over an agreed period | A £50,000 bank loan repayable over 5 years |
| Overdraft | Permission from a bank to withdraw more money than is in the account, up to an agreed limit | A shop using its overdraft to pay a supplier before customer payments arrive |
| Trade credit | Buying goods/services now and paying the supplier later (e.g. 30/60/90 days) | A retailer receiving stock and paying the supplier 60 days later |
| Hire purchase | Paying for an asset in instalments while using it; ownership passes after the final payment | A haulage firm acquiring a lorry via hire purchase |
| Leasing | Renting an asset for regular payments without ever owning it | An airline leasing aircraft from a leasing company |
| Venture capital | Finance provided by specialist investors to high-risk, high-growth businesses in exchange for a share of ownership | A tech start-up receiving venture capital funding for expansion |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Every business, from a single street-food cart in Lagos to a multinational like Unilever, faces the same fundamental question: where does the money come from? Getting the choice of finance wrong — borrowing too much, too little, at the wrong cost, or for the wrong length of time — is one of the most common reasons small businesses fail in their first three years. Understanding sources of finance is therefore not abstract theory; it is the difference between a business surviving its cash-flow gap and closing its doors.

## Core Content

### Why Businesses Need Finance
Businesses need finance for three broad reasons:
1. **Starting up** — buying premises, equipment, initial stock, and covering costs before any revenue is earned.
2. **Day-to-day running (working capital)** — paying wages, rent, and suppliers while waiting for customers to pay.
3. **Expansion** — opening new outlets, buying larger premises, investing in new machinery, or entering new markets.

The amount, purpose, and time period of the need for finance all affect which source is appropriate. A business needing to smooth a temporary cash-flow dip should not take out a 10-year mortgage; a business buying a new factory should not rely on an overdraft.

### Internal Sources of Finance
Internal finance comes from within the business and does not create new external obligations.

- **Owner's savings/personal capital**: the owner invests their own money. Advantage: no interest to pay, no loss of control. Disadvantage: limited by the owner's personal wealth; puts personal savings at risk.
- **Retained profit**: profit from previous trading periods reinvested in the business. Advantage: free (no interest), does not dilute ownership, no repayment required. Disadvantage: only available to established, profitable businesses; may not be enough for large projects; reduces funds available for owner drawings/dividends.
- **Sale of assets**: selling unused fixed assets (e.g. surplus land, old machinery) for cash. Advantage: raises cash without borrowing. Disadvantage: one-off source only; the business may need the asset later; may signal financial distress to stakeholders.

### External Sources of Finance
External finance comes from outside the business.

**Short-term external finance** (typically under 1 year, used for working capital):
- **Overdraft**: flexible, only pay interest on the amount used; but interest rates are usually higher than loans, and the bank can demand repayment at short notice.
- **Trade credit**: interest-free if paid within the credit period; improves short-term cash flow. Disadvantage: may damage supplier relationships if payments are late, and suppliers may charge higher prices or refuse future credit.
- **Debt factoring**: immediate cash from unpaid invoices; the business receives roughly 80-90% of the invoice value upfront. Disadvantage: the factoring company takes a percentage/fee, reducing overall revenue, and it can signal cash-flow weakness to the market.

**Long-term external finance** (typically over 1 year, used for fixed assets and expansion):
- **Bank loans**: a fixed sum repaid with interest over an agreed period. Advantage: predictable repayments, retains full ownership. Disadvantage: interest increases the total cost; banks may require security/collateral; regular repayments are a fixed cost even if the business has a bad month.
- **Share capital (for limited companies)**: selling shares (ownership stakes) to investors, e.g. via an Initial Public Offering (IPO) or, for existing shareholders, a **rights issue**. Advantage: does not have to be repaid, no interest. Disadvantage: dilutes ownership and control (existing owners own a smaller percentage), and profits must now be shared via dividends among more shareholders.
- **Venture capital**: specialist investors provide finance (often to start-ups or high-growth firms) in exchange for equity and sometimes a role in management. Advantage: large sums available, plus investor expertise/contacts. Disadvantage: significant loss of ownership/control, investors expect high returns and may push for rapid growth or exit.
- **Hire purchase**: pay in instalments for an asset while using it immediately; legal ownership transfers after the final payment. Advantage: spreads the cost, asset is usable immediately. Disadvantage: more expensive overall than paying cash due to interest/charges.
- **Leasing**: renting rather than owning an asset. Advantage: lower upfront cost, maintenance often included, easy to upgrade equipment. Disadvantage: never own the asset; total cost over time can exceed purchase price.
- **Debentures**: long-term loan certificates issued by companies at fixed interest. Advantage: does not dilute ownership. Disadvantage: fixed interest must be paid regardless of profit levels; increases financial risk (gearing).
- **Government grants/subsidies**: financial support, often for specific purposes (e.g. job creation, green technology, deprived areas). Advantage: usually does not need to be repaid. Disadvantage: often has strict conditions attached, limited availability, application process can be slow and competitive.
- **Crowdfunding**: raising many small amounts from the public, typically online (e.g. Kickstarter, Indiegogo). Advantage: also tests market demand and builds a customer base/publicity. Disadvantage: not guaranteed to reach the funding target; ideas can be copied once publicised; may involve giving away equity or rewards.
- **Microfinance**: small loans (and sometimes savings/insurance services) aimed at entrepreneurs excluded from mainstream banking, common in developing economies. Advantage: gives access to capital for those without collateral or credit history. Disadvantage: loan amounts are small and interest rates can still be relatively high.

### Choosing the Appropriate Source of Finance
Exam questions frequently ask students to recommend a source of finance. Key factors to weigh:
- **Amount needed**: small amounts might use an overdraft or retained profit; large amounts may need a loan or share issue.
- **Purpose/time period**: short-term needs (working capital) suit short-term finance; long-term needs (buying a factory) suit long-term finance — this is called the **matching principle**.
- **Cost**: interest rates, fees, and dilution of ownership all represent a "cost".
- **Control**: owners who do not want to give up control should avoid share capital or venture capital.
- **Risk/gearing**: too much loan capital increases financial risk (high gearing) because interest must be paid regardless of performance.
- **Legal form of the business**: a sole trader cannot issue shares; only limited companies can.

**Common misconception**: students often confuse "internal" with "cheap" and "external" with "expensive" — this is not always true (retained profit has an opportunity cost; a government grant, though external, may be free). Also, students often forget that a business's legal structure restricts which sources are available (e.g. only companies can raise share capital).

Links to Chapter 26 (Cash Flow Forecasting) — short-term finance decisions are directly informed by the cash flow forecast; and to Chapter 31 (Ratio Analysis) — gearing ratio measures the balance between loan and share capital chosen here.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Facebook (Meta Platforms), USA, technology/social media sector.
**Situation:** Facebook raised approximately $16 billion in its May 2012 Initial Public Offering (IPO), selling shares to the public for the first time and converting from a private to a publicly listed company.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates share capital as a source of long-term finance for a large business, and the trade-off of diluting the founders' ownership and control in exchange for large-scale funding.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the advantages and disadvantages to Mark Zuckerberg of using an IPO rather than a bank loan to fund Facebook's growth." (AO3)

**Company/context:** M-KOPA, Kenya, solar energy and consumer finance (small/emerging-market example).
**Situation:** M-KOPA developed a pay-as-you-go finance model (similar in principle to hire purchase) that allows customers in East Africa without access to traditional credit to acquire solar power systems and smartphones through small daily instalment payments.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how instalment-based finance (a form of hire purchase) and microfinance-style models extend access to assets/finance in markets where conventional bank lending or collateral is unavailable.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate whether instalment-based finance models like M-KOPA's are more suitable than traditional bank loans for low-income entrepreneurs in developing economies." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business (e.g. a sole trader) typically relies on owner's savings, retained profit, bank overdrafts/loans, trade credit, and possibly crowdfunding or microfinance, because it usually cannot issue shares and has limited collateral or track record to attract venture capital. A large business, especially a public limited company, has access to a much wider range of sources — including share issues (IPOs and rights issues), debentures, and venture capital — and can typically borrow larger sums at lower interest rates due to its size, assets, and credit history, but must also manage the expectations of a larger and more diverse group of shareholders.
