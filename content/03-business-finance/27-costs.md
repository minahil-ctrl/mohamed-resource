## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Money a business spends to produce goods or provide services | Raw materials, wages, rent |
| Fixed cost | A cost that does not change with the level of output in the short run | Rent on a factory stays the same whether it produces 100 or 1,000 units |
| Variable cost | A cost that changes directly with the level of output | Raw material costs rise as more units are produced |
| Total cost | The sum of all fixed and variable costs at a given level of output | Total cost = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs |
| Total variable cost | Variable cost per unit multiplied by the number of units produced | 500 units × £4 variable cost per unit = £2,000 |
| Average cost (unit cost) | The total cost of production divided by the number of units produced | Total cost £10,000 ÷ 1,000 units = £10 average cost |
| Direct cost | A cost that can be clearly attributed to producing a specific product or service | The wood used to make a specific table |
| Indirect cost (overhead) | A cost that cannot be attributed to one specific product; it supports the business as a whole | Head office administration costs, insurance |
| Semi-variable cost | A cost with both a fixed and a variable element | A phone bill with a fixed line rental plus variable call charges |
| Economies of scale | Falling average costs as a business increases its scale of production | A large supermarket chain buying stock in bulk at a lower cost per unit |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Whether it's a street vendor pricing a plate of jollof rice or Toyota deciding how many cars to manufacture next quarter, no pricing or production decision can be made sensibly without knowing exactly what it costs to produce one more unit versus what it costs to simply keep the lights on. Cost classification is the invisible foundation beneath pricing, break-even analysis, and profit calculation — get it wrong, and every decision built on top of it is wrong too.

## Core Content

### Classifying Costs by Behaviour: Fixed vs Variable
**Fixed costs** do not change with output in the short run — a business pays them even if it produces zero units. Examples: rent, insurance, salaried staff wages, loan repayments, business rates.

**Variable costs** change directly and proportionally with the level of output — the more units produced, the higher the total variable cost. Examples: raw materials, packaging, piece-rate wages, delivery costs per unit.

**Semi-variable costs** contain both elements — e.g. a delivery van's insurance (fixed) plus its fuel cost (variable, depending on how many deliveries are made).

**Formula:**
Total Cost (TC) = Total Fixed Costs (TFC) + Total Variable Costs (TVC)
Total Variable Cost (TVC) = Variable Cost per Unit (VC) × Quantity Produced (Q)
Average Cost (AC) = Total Cost ÷ Quantity Produced (Q)

### Classifying Costs by Purpose: Direct vs Indirect
This is a separate classification system, used mainly for allocating costs to specific products/departments:
- **Direct costs** can be traced directly to one product — e.g. the fabric used in a specific dress, the wages of the worker who assembled a specific product (direct labour).
- **Indirect costs (overheads)** cannot be traced to a single product — e.g. factory rent, management salaries, marketing spend covering the whole business.

**Common misconception**: students often assume "direct cost" and "variable cost" mean the same thing, and "indirect cost" and "fixed cost" mean the same thing. This is not always true — for example, a factory supervisor's salary is an indirect cost (not tied to one product) but is also a fixed cost (behaviour), while direct labour on a piece-rate system is both a direct cost AND a variable cost. Always classify by the specific question asked: "by behaviour" (fixed/variable) or "by traceability" (direct/indirect).

### Worked Example: "Kofi's Furniture Workshop" (illustrative figures for a fictional small business)
Kofi's Furniture Workshop makes wooden chairs. In one month:
- Fixed costs (rent, insurance, supervisor's salary): **£3,000**
- Variable cost per chair (wood, varnish, direct labour): **£25**
- Output: **200 chairs**

Step-by-step:
1. Total Variable Cost = VC per unit × Q = £25 × 200 = **£5,000**
2. Total Cost = TFC + TVC = £3,000 + £5,000 = **£8,000**
3. Average Cost = Total Cost ÷ Q = £8,000 ÷ 200 = **£40 per chair**

**Interpretation:** Kofi must charge more than £40 per chair simply to cover costs at this output level (before any profit margin). If Kofi doubled output to 400 chairs (assuming fixed costs remain the same in the short run):
- Total Variable Cost = £25 × 400 = £10,000
- Total Cost = £3,000 + £10,000 = £13,000
- Average Cost = £13,000 ÷ 400 = **£32.50 per chair**

This demonstrates an important principle: as output rises, fixed costs are spread over more units, so **average cost falls** — this is the basis of economies of scale, and is central to why larger businesses can often compete on price more effectively than small ones.

### Why Cost Classification Matters for Decision-Making
- **Pricing**: a business must understand its average cost to set a price that at least covers costs (cost-plus pricing).
- **Break-even analysis** (Chapter 28): fixed and variable costs are the essential building blocks of break-even calculations and charts.
- **Make-or-buy and outsourcing decisions**: comparing variable costs of in-house production versus buying from a supplier.
- **Budgeting and cost control**: identifying which costs can realistically be reduced in the short run (variable costs) versus which are locked in (fixed costs).

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Toyota Motor Corporation, Japan, automotive manufacturing (large business).
**Situation:** Toyota is renowned for its "Toyota Production System," which emphasises tightly controlling both fixed costs (efficient factory layout, minimal idle machinery) and variable costs (just-in-time parts delivery to avoid holding costly inventory) to keep average unit costs low across huge production volumes.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a large manufacturer manages both fixed and variable costs simultaneously to achieve low average costs through economies of scale and efficient production methods.
**Discussion question:** "Analyse how Toyota's approach to managing fixed and variable costs might give it a competitive advantage over smaller car manufacturers." (AO3)

**Company/context:** A local tailoring business in Nairobi, Kenya (small business, illustrative of many emerging-market micro-enterprises).
**Situation:** Small tailoring businesses across Kenya typically have very low fixed costs (a small rented stall, one sewing machine) but rely heavily on variable costs (fabric, thread, buttons) that fluctuate with each order, meaning their average cost per garment barely falls even as output rises, unlike a large clothing manufacturer.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates that small businesses, lacking the scale to spread fixed costs over large output, cannot achieve the same falling average costs (economies of scale) as large manufacturers, keeping their unit costs relatively high.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate whether a small tailoring business could realistically reduce its average cost per garment, and how." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically has low fixed costs (e.g. a small rented unit, few salaried staff) but limited ability to reduce average costs, since it cannot buy materials in bulk or spread overheads across a large output — its average cost per unit tends to stay relatively high and stable. A large business often has much higher fixed costs (large factories, extensive management structures) but can spread these fixed costs over a far greater output, achieving significant economies of scale and much lower average costs per unit, which supports competitive pricing that small businesses often cannot match.
