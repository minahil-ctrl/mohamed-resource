## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Cash flow | The movement of cash into and out of a business over a period of time | A bakery's cash flow shows more cash going out in December (ingredients, wages) than coming in |
| Cash inflow | Money received by a business, e.g. from sales, loans, or owner investment | Cash received from customers paying for goods |
| Cash outflow | Money paid out by a business, e.g. for wages, rent, or stock | Rent paid to the landlord each month |
| Net cash flow | Cash inflows minus cash outflows for a given period | £5,000 inflow − £4,200 outflow = £800 net cash flow |
| Opening balance | The amount of cash a business has at the start of a period | £1,000 cash held on 1 January |
| Closing balance | The amount of cash a business has at the end of a period (opening balance + net cash flow) | £1,000 + £800 = £1,800 closing balance |
| Cash flow forecast | A prediction of future cash inflows and outflows over a future period, usually month by month | A 6-month cash flow forecast for a new café |
| Cash-flow problem | A situation where a business does not have enough cash to meet its short-term obligations | A business unable to pay a supplier on time due to late customer payments |
| Insolvency | The inability of a business to pay its debts as they fall due | A business becoming insolvent despite being profitable on paper |
| Working capital | The finance available for day-to-day operations (current assets minus current liabilities) | A retailer needing enough working capital to restock shelves |
| Overtrading | Expanding a business too quickly without enough cash/working capital to support the growth | A rapidly growing start-up running out of cash despite rising sales |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
"Cash is king" is one of the oldest sayings in business — and for good reason. A profitable business can still go bankrupt if it runs out of cash to pay wages or suppliers on time. High-profile UK retail collapses like Carillion (2018) were driven substantially by cash-flow crises, not a lack of profit on paper, showing why every manager — from a market-stall owner to a multinational CFO — must forecast and manage cash flow carefully.

## Core Content

### Why Cash Flow Forecasting Matters
Cash flow forecasting predicts when and how much cash will flow into and out of a business, allowing managers to:
- Identify months where cash shortfalls (negative net cash flow) are likely, so finance (e.g. an overdraft) can be arranged in advance.
- Plan the timing of major purchases so they don't create a cash crisis.
- Reassure lenders/investors that the business can meet its obligations.
- Distinguish between cash and profit: **profit is an accounting measure of income minus expenses over a period, while cash flow is the actual movement of money.** A business can be profitable but "cash poor" if customers are slow to pay (see Chapter 29 for the distinction between cash and profit in financial statements).

### Constructing a Cash Flow Forecast
A cash flow forecast is built month by month using this structure:

```
Opening balance (cash at start of month)
+ Total cash inflows (for the month)
− Total cash outflows (for the month)
= Net cash flow
Opening balance + Net cash flow = Closing balance
(Closing balance of one month = Opening balance of the next month)
```

**Formula:**
Net Cash Flow = Total Cash Inflows − Total Cash Outflows
Closing Balance = Opening Balance + Net Cash Flow

### Worked Example: "Brew & Bake" Café (illustrative figures for a fictional small business)
Brew & Bake is a new café. Its owner has forecast the following for its first three months of trading:

| | January (£) | February (£) | March (£) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opening balance** | 2,000 | ? | ? |
| **Cash inflows** | | | |
| Cash sales | 6,000 | 7,500 | 9,000 |
| Owner's capital injected | 3,000 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total inflows** | 9,000 | 7,500 | 9,000 |
| **Cash outflows** | | | |
| Rent | 1,200 | 1,200 | 1,200 |
| Wages | 2,500 | 2,500 | 2,800 |
| Stock/ingredients | 3,000 | 3,500 | 4,000 |
| Utilities | 400 | 400 | 450 |
| Equipment purchase | 2,500 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total outflows** | 9,600 | 7,600 | 8,450 |
| **Net cash flow** | -600 | -100 | 550 |
| **Closing balance** | 1,400 | 1,300 | 1,850 |

Step-by-step for January:
- Total inflows = 6,000 + 3,000 = £9,000
- Total outflows = 1,200 + 2,500 + 3,000 + 400 + 2,500 = £9,600
- Net cash flow = 9,000 − 9,600 = **−£600**
- Closing balance = 2,000 + (−600) = **£1,400**

February's opening balance = January's closing balance = £1,400:
- Net cash flow = 7,500 − 7,600 = **−£100**
- Closing balance = 1,400 − 100 = **£1,300**

March's opening balance = £1,300:
- Net cash flow = 9,000 − 8,450 = **£550**
- Closing balance = 1,300 + 550 = **£1,850**

**Interpretation:** Despite two months of negative net cash flow, Brew & Bake's opening cash cushion (£2,000, boosted by owner's capital) prevented it from running out of cash. By March, rising sales turn net cash flow positive and the closing balance begins to recover. If the owner had not injected £3,000 of capital in January, the business would have run out of cash and been unable to pay outflows in full.

### Causes of Cash-Flow Problems
- Poor sales/seasonal demand (e.g. an ice-cream business in winter).
- Overtrading — expanding too fast without matching finance.
- Allowing customers too much time to pay (poor credit control) while having to pay suppliers quickly.
- Unexpected costs (e.g. equipment breakdown, price rises).
- Holding too much unsold stock/inventory, tying up cash.
- Taking on too much debt with high repayments due at once.

### Consequences of Cash-Flow Problems
- Inability to pay suppliers, staff, rent — damaging relationships and reputation.
- Reliance on expensive short-term finance (e.g. overdrafts) increasing costs.
- In severe cases, insolvency and business closure — even for a profitable business.

### Solutions to Cash-Flow Problems
- Arrange an overdraft or short-term loan in advance.
- Improve credit control: chase late payments, offer discounts for early payment, reduce credit period offered to customers.
- Negotiate longer payment terms (more trade credit) with suppliers.
- Delay non-essential spending (e.g. postpone equipment purchases).
- Use debt factoring to receive cash from invoices sooner.
- Reduce/manage stock levels more efficiently.

**Common misconception**: students frequently confuse cash flow with profit, treating a forecast showing negative net cash flow as automatically meaning the business is "failing" or "unprofitable" — it may simply reflect timing differences (e.g. buying stock in bulk before a busy season). Also, many students forget the closing balance of one month becomes the opening balance of the next — a very common exam calculation error.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Carillion plc, United Kingdom, construction and facilities management (large business).
**Situation:** Carillion collapsed into liquidation in January 2018 despite reporting profits in prior years; the company faced a severe cash-flow crisis driven by large debts, delayed payments on contracts, and an inability to meet its short-term obligations to suppliers and pension schemes.
**Link to theory:** Demonstrates that a business can appear profitable yet still suffer fatal cash-flow problems, and illustrates the real-world consequences of poor cash-flow management and overtrading on large contracts.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the extent to which better cash flow forecasting could have prevented Carillion's collapse." (AO3)

**Company/context:** A Grameen Bank-style microfinance borrower, Bangladesh (small/emerging-market example).
**Situation:** Grameen Bank pioneered microfinance lending to small entrepreneurs (often women running small stalls or craft businesses) who previously lacked access to formal credit, helping them manage cash-flow gaps between production/purchase of materials and receiving sales income.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how access to short-term finance (a form of the solutions covered in this chapter) helps small businesses in developing economies bridge cash-flow gaps that would otherwise threaten survival.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the importance of access to short-term finance for the survival of small businesses in developing economies." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business often has a much smaller cash buffer and less access to varied finance, so a single late-paying customer or unexpected cost can quickly create a survival-threatening cash-flow crisis; forecasts for small businesses are therefore often simple, prepared manually, and reviewed monthly. A large business typically has dedicated finance/treasury teams producing detailed, rolling cash-flow forecasts across multiple departments and countries, with access to a wider range of short-term finance (e.g. commercial paper, large overdraft facilities) to manage temporary shortfalls, but the scale of potential cash-flow problems (as with Carillion) can also be far larger and more complex to resolve.
