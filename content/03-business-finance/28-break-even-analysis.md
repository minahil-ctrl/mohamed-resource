## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Break-even point | The level of output at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither a profit nor a loss | A café breaks even when it sells 150 coffees a day |
| Contribution | Selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit; the amount each unit sale contributes towards fixed costs and then profit | Selling price £10 − variable cost £6 = £4 contribution per unit |
| Contribution per unit | Same as contribution; used to calculate break-even output | £4 contribution per unit for a product selling at £10 |
| Margin of safety | The amount by which actual (or budgeted) sales exceed the break-even point | Sales of 200 units with break-even at 150 units gives a margin of safety of 50 units |
| Total revenue | Selling price per unit multiplied by the quantity sold | £10 × 200 units = £2,000 total revenue |
| Target profit | A specific level of profit a business aims to achieve, used to calculate the output required beyond break-even | A business wanting to earn £5,000 profit needs to calculate the required output |
| Break-even chart | A graph showing fixed costs, total costs, and total revenue against output, used to identify the break-even point visually | A break-even chart showing the point where the revenue line crosses the total cost line |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
Before launching a new product, from a small bakery adding a new pastry to Tesla deciding whether a new factory line is viable, every business asks the same question: "How many units do we need to sell before we stop losing money?" Break-even analysis answers that question directly, making it one of the most practical and frequently examined numerical tools in IGCSE Business — and one that real entrepreneurs use before committing a single dollar.

## Core Content

### The Concept of Break-Even
The **break-even point** is the level of output (or sales) at which total revenue exactly equals total costs — the business makes zero profit and zero loss. Below this point, the business makes a loss; above it, the business makes a profit. This builds directly on the fixed and variable cost concepts from Chapter 27.

### Contribution
**Contribution** is the amount each unit sold contributes towards covering fixed costs (and, once fixed costs are covered, towards profit). It is calculated as:

**Formula:** Contribution per unit = Selling Price per unit (SP) − Variable Cost per unit (VC)

Contribution is not the same as profit — contribution only becomes profit once total fixed costs have been fully covered by the accumulated contribution from all units sold.

### Calculating the Break-Even Point

**Formula:** Break-Even Output (units) = Total Fixed Costs (TFC) ÷ Contribution per Unit (SP − VC)

### Worked Example: "Amara's Smoothie Bar" (illustrative figures for a fictional small business)
Amara is launching a smoothie bar. Her figures are:
- Selling price per smoothie: **£4.00**
- Variable cost per smoothie (fruit, cup, straw): **£1.50**
- Total fixed costs per month (rent, insurance, equipment lease): **£1,000**

**Step 1 — Calculate contribution per unit:**
Contribution = SP − VC = £4.00 − £1.50 = **£2.50 per smoothie**

**Step 2 — Calculate break-even output:**
Break-even output = TFC ÷ Contribution per unit = £1,000 ÷ £2.50 = **400 smoothies per month**

**Interpretation:** Amara must sell 400 smoothies a month just to cover her costs. Any smoothie sold beyond 400 contributes £2.50 directly to profit.

### Margin of Safety
The **margin of safety** measures how far actual/budgeted sales exceed the break-even point — it shows how much sales could fall before the business starts making a loss.

**Formula:** Margin of Safety = Actual (or Budgeted) Sales − Break-Even Output

**Worked example continued:** If Amara budgets to sell 550 smoothies in a typical month:
Margin of Safety = 550 − 400 = **150 smoothies**

**Interpretation:** Sales could fall by up to 150 smoothies (from 550 to 400) before Amara's business would start making a loss. A larger margin of safety indicates lower risk.

### Calculating Output Needed for a Target Profit

**Formula:** Required Output = (Total Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per Unit

**Worked example continued:** Suppose Amara wants to earn a target profit of £750 per month.
Required Output = (£1,000 + £750) ÷ £2.50 = £1,750 ÷ £2.50 = **700 smoothies**

### Constructing a Break-Even Chart
A break-even chart plots output on the x-axis and £ (costs/revenue) on the y-axis, with three lines: fixed costs (horizontal), total costs (starting at the fixed cost level, rising at the rate of variable cost per unit), and total revenue (starting at the origin, rising at the rate of selling price per unit). The point where the total revenue line crosses the total cost line is the break-even point. The full labelled specification is given in the Diagrams Required section below — this chart should always be built to scale from calculated data, not sketched freely, in a slide deck or exam answer.

Using Amara's figures at, say, up to 600 units:
- Total Cost at 600 units = TFC + (VC × Q) = £1,000 + (£1.50 × 600) = £1,000 + £900 = **£1,900**
- Total Revenue at 600 units = SP × Q = £4.00 × 600 = **£2,400**
- At 600 units, Total Revenue (£2,400) > Total Cost (£1,900), confirming the business is profitable beyond the break-even point of 400 units, consistent with the calculation above.

### Uses and Limitations of Break-Even Analysis
**Uses:**
- Helps decide whether a new product/business idea is viable before committing money.
- Supports finance applications — lenders/investors want to see break-even calculations.
- Helps set sales targets and assess the impact of pricing or cost changes.
- Shows the margin of safety, helping assess risk.

**Limitations:**
- Assumes all output is sold (ignores unsold stock).
- Assumes costs and selling price stay constant, but in reality both can change (e.g. bulk discounts on materials, price changes).
- Assumes fixed costs remain fixed at all output levels, which may not be true if output increases substantially (e.g. needing a bigger premises).
- It is only a simplified model and does not account for external factors like competitor action or changing consumer tastes.

**Common misconception**: students often forget that break-even output changes if selling price, variable cost, OR fixed costs change — always re-calculate rather than assuming the original break-even figure still applies. Also, "contribution" is frequently confused with "profit" — contribution is a per-unit concept used to cover fixed costs, whereas profit is what remains after ALL costs (fixed and variable) have been covered.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Tesla, Inc., USA, electric vehicle manufacturing (large business).
**Situation:** Tesla operated at a loss for many years after its 2010 IPO as it built out expensive "Gigafactory" production capacity (very high fixed costs), before finally reaching sustained profitability from 2020 onward once vehicle production and sales volumes grew large enough to cover its fixed costs and generate profit — a real-world illustration of the break-even principle at enormous scale.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a business with very high fixed costs (factories, R&D) needs a very high break-even output before it becomes profitable, and how rising output (once achieved) can turn years of losses into profit once break-even is passed.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss why a business like Tesla might be willing to operate below its break-even point for several years." (AO3)

**Company/context:** A small independent coffee shop chain, Rwanda (small/emerging-market example, illustrative of common local start-ups reliant on Rwanda's specialty coffee sector).
**Situation:** Small coffee shops opening in Kigali typically face significant fixed costs (rent, equipment) relative to their sales volumes in the early months of trading, meaning many must carefully calculate their break-even point before opening to judge whether local demand can realistically support the fixed costs of the premises.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the practical, everyday relevance of break-even analysis for small businesses deciding whether a new venture is financially viable before committing limited start-up capital.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the importance of break-even analysis for a small business owner deciding whether to open a new coffee shop." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically uses break-even analysis informally, often on a single product or single outlet, to decide whether a new venture is worth the risk before committing limited start-up capital, and its margin of safety may be small due to limited sales volume. A large business often applies break-even analysis across multiple products, factories, or markets simultaneously, and while its fixed costs (e.g. factories, R&D) are far larger in absolute terms, its much greater sales volume usually provides a larger margin of safety and a proportionally lower risk of failing to break even, though the scale of losses if it fails to do so (as with Tesla in its early years) can be immense.
