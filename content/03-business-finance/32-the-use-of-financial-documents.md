# Chapter 32: The Use of Financial Documents

**Unit:** Unit 3 — Business Finance
**Specification reference:** 4BS1 Topic 3.8 — The use of financial documents
**Applies to:** Both (Paper 1 — Small Business; Paper 2 — Large Business)
**Tracker code:** 3.8

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the range of stakeholders who use a business's financial statements and ratios.
- Explain why each stakeholder group is interested in financial information and what specific questions they seek to answer.
- Apply understanding of stakeholder interests to judge which financial document or ratio is most relevant to a given stakeholder's decision.
- Analyse how the interests of different stakeholders in financial information can align or conflict.
- Evaluate the overall limitations of financial statement and ratio analysis as a source of information for decision-making.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder | Any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities and performance of a business | Owners, employees, lenders, government, suppliers |
| Financial statements | The formal documents summarising a business's financial performance and position, principally the statement of comprehensive income and the statement of financial position | A company's published annual accounts |
| Internal stakeholder | A stakeholder who is part of the business itself | Owners, managers, employees |
| External stakeholder | A stakeholder outside the business who is affected by or interested in it | Lenders, suppliers, government, investors |
| Creditworthiness | An assessment of how likely a business is to be able to repay money it owes | A bank assessing creditworthiness before approving a loan |
| Solvency | A business's ability to meet its financial obligations (pay its debts) as they fall due | A business found to be solvent after liquidity ratio analysis |
| Annual report | A yearly published document (required for many companies) containing financial statements, ratios, and a review of business performance | A public limited company's annual report sent to shareholders |
| Audit | An independent examination of a business's financial statements to check they give a true and fair view of its financial position | A firm of accountants auditing a company's year-end accounts |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
When Adidas publishes its annual report, that single document is read very differently by very different people on the very same day: a shareholder scans it to judge whether to buy more shares, an employee checks it to gauge job security and the chance of a pay rise, a supplier reviews it to decide whether to keep extending credit, and a tax authority uses it to verify the company has correctly calculated the tax it owes. The statement of comprehensive income (Chapter 29), the statement of financial position (Chapter 30), and the ratios calculated from them (Chapter 31) are not produced for one audience — they are a shared resource read through many different lenses, each shaping a real decision.

## Core Content

### Why Financial Documents Have Multiple Users
Financial statements and the ratios derived from them are not created purely for a business's own internal record-keeping. Because a business's performance affects a wide range of people and organisations connected to it, financial information is read and interpreted by a broad set of **stakeholders**, each asking different questions and each using different parts of the documents to answer them. Understanding *who* uses financial information and *why* is essential to understanding *why* the documents (and the ratios in Chapter 31) are structured the way they are.

### Owners
Sole traders, partners, and shareholders who own a business have a direct financial stake in its performance. Owners use financial statements to:
- Assess whether the business is profitable and growing (statement of comprehensive income, profitability ratios).
- Decide whether to withdraw profit (drawings/dividends) or reinvest it.
- Judge whether their investment is achieving an adequate return, often using ROCE (Chapter 31) to compare against alternative uses of their capital.
- Decide whether to invest further capital, sell their stake, or exit the business entirely.

### Managers
Managers, who may or may not also be owners, use financial information for day-to-day and strategic decision-making:
- Monitoring whether costs are under control (comparing gross and net profit margins over time).
- Setting and reviewing budgets and targets based on past performance.
- Deciding on pricing, cost-cutting, or investment in new equipment.
- Assessing liquidity (current ratio, acid test ratio) to ensure the business can meet short-term obligations such as wages and supplier payments without a cash crisis.
- Reporting performance to owners/shareholders and justifying management decisions.

### Employees (and Trade Unions)
Employees have a direct personal interest in the financial health of their employer:
- Judging job security — a business reporting falling profit or a weakening liquidity position may be more likely to make redundancies.
- Assessing the likelihood of pay rises, bonuses, or improved working conditions being affordable.
- Trade unions representing employees may use financial statements during pay negotiations to argue that a profitable business can afford to increase wages.

### Lenders (Banks and Other Creditors)
Banks and other financial institutions considering lending money, or reviewing an existing loan, use financial statements to assess **creditworthiness** and **solvency**:
- Checking liquidity ratios (current ratio, acid test ratio) to judge whether the business can service loan repayments alongside its other short-term debts.
- Reviewing the statement of financial position to see the existing level of borrowing (gearing) and available assets that could serve as security/collateral.
- Examining profitability trends to judge whether future cash flow is likely to support repayments.
- A bank is unlikely to extend further credit to a business already showing a weak acid test ratio and falling net profit margin, since this signals rising risk of default.

### Suppliers
Suppliers who sell goods or materials to a business, often on trade credit, need to judge whether the business will be able to pay its invoices on time:
- Liquidity ratios are especially relevant here, since suppliers are typically owed money as a **current liability (trade payable)** of the buying business and are directly affected by its short-term cash position.
- A supplier reviewing a weak or declining current ratio may reduce the credit period offered, demand payment in advance, or refuse to supply on credit at all.

### Government and Tax Authorities
Governments and tax authorities use financial statements primarily to:
- Calculate the tax owed on a business's profit (e.g. corporation tax, calculated from net profit).
- Verify that a business is complying with tax and accounting regulations, sometimes prompting an **audit**.
- Use aggregated business performance data for wider economic policy purposes, such as monitoring the health of a particular industry sector.

### Potential Investors
Individuals or institutions considering buying shares in a business (particularly relevant to public limited companies) use financial statements and ratios to judge whether an investment is likely to be worthwhile:
- Reviewing profitability ratios (gross profit margin, net profit margin, ROCE) and trends over several years to judge future earnings potential.
- Comparing ratios against competitors (interfirm comparison, Chapter 31) to judge relative attractiveness within a sector.
- Assessing the statement of financial position for financial stability (e.g. level of debt relative to equity) before committing funds, since a highly indebted company carries greater investment risk.

### Competitors
Rival businesses may also examine a company's published financial statements (where publicly available, as is required for most limited companies) to benchmark their own performance, identify a competitor's relative strengths and weaknesses, and inform their own strategic decisions — an application of the interfirm comparison introduced in Chapter 31.

### How Stakeholder Interests Can Align or Conflict
Stakeholder interests in financial information frequently **align**: a profitable, liquid business generally benefits owners (higher returns), employees (greater job security), lenders and suppliers (lower default risk), and government (more tax revenue) simultaneously. However, interests can also **conflict**:
- Owners/shareholders may want profit paid out as dividends, while lenders and employees may prefer profit retained in the business to strengthen its financial position and secure jobs.
- Managers may wish to reinvest heavily in growth (reducing short-term net profit and ROCE), while shareholders focused on short-term returns may prefer profit maximisation now.
- A business's efforts to minimise tax liability (within legal limits) may conflict with a government's interest in maximising tax revenue.

Recognising these tensions is itself an exam-relevant skill, since higher-mark evaluation questions often ask students to weigh up competing stakeholder perspectives on the same set of financial results.

### Common Misconceptions and Exam Pitfalls
- Students often name stakeholders generically ("the public," "society") without explaining a *specific, financially relevant* reason that group would consult financial statements — always link the stakeholder to a precise question they are trying to answer (e.g. "can this business repay a loan?", not simply "they are interested in the business").
- Students frequently forget that **the same document** (e.g. the statement of financial position) can be used by multiple stakeholders for entirely different purposes — a strong answer identifies which specific figure or ratio a given stakeholder would focus on, not just that they "look at the accounts."
- A common error is assuming all stakeholders want the same outcome from a business's financial performance — as shown above, interests can genuinely conflict, and recognising this is essential for AO3/AO4 marks.
- Students sometimes overlook internal stakeholders (employees, managers) in favour of only external ones (banks, investors) — a complete answer should consider both.

### Limitations of Financial Statement and Ratio Analysis for Stakeholders
- Financial statements are **historical** — by the time a stakeholder reads published accounts, several months may have passed, and the business's real-time position (see Chapter 30's point on the statement of financial position being only a snapshot) may already differ.
- **Accounting policies vary** between businesses (e.g. depreciation methods, stock valuation methods), making direct comparisons between companies potentially misleading unless adjustments are made.
- Financial statements say **nothing about non-financial performance** — factors such as staff morale, environmental impact, product quality, or customer satisfaction, which may be highly relevant to a stakeholder's decision, are not captured.
- **Window dressing** — the (legal but sometimes misleading) practice of presenting accounts in the most favourable light possible within accounting rules — can distort the picture a stakeholder receives.
- Ratios and statements provide **no information about future plans, competitor actions, or market conditions**, all of which may be more important than historical figures for certain stakeholder decisions (e.g. a potential investor should also consider a business's future strategy, not just its past ratios).
- Small, unincorporated businesses (sole traders, partnerships) are **not legally required to publish** detailed financial statements, meaning some stakeholders (e.g. potential investors, competitors) may have very limited access to reliable financial information for such businesses in the first place.

Links to Chapter 29 (Statement of Comprehensive Income) and Chapter 30 (Statement of Financial Position), which supply the raw data referenced throughout this chapter, and Chapter 31 (Ratio Analysis), whose calculated ratios are the specific tools most stakeholders described here actually use in practice.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Adidas AG, Germany, sportswear and footwear multinational (large business).
**Situation:** As a publicly listed company, Adidas is legally required to publish detailed audited annual financial statements, which are read and analysed by a wide range of stakeholders simultaneously — institutional shareholders assessing returns, financial analysts issuing buy/sell recommendations, employees and trade unions monitoring the company's ability to sustain wages and jobs, and tax authorities in multiple countries verifying reported profit.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a single set of published financial statements from one large multinational serves a genuinely wide range of stakeholder groups simultaneously, each reading the same figures for a different purpose.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss the extent to which the interests of shareholders and employees in a large company's financial statements might come into conflict." (AO3)

**Company/context:** A small family-run restaurant, Vietnam (small/emerging-market example, illustrative of common local small businesses).
**Situation:** A small, unincorporated family restaurant in Hanoi is not legally required to publish detailed financial statements to the public, but its owners must still prepare basic financial records for the local tax authority, and if the restaurant seeks a bank loan to expand or renovate, the bank will require sight of its financial statements before agreeing to lend.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how even a small, informally-run business is still subject to certain stakeholder demands for financial information (tax authorities, lenders), despite lacking the extensive public reporting obligations of a large listed company.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the importance of accurate financial record-keeping for a small business owner who hopes to secure a bank loan to expand." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business typically shares financial information with a narrow circle of stakeholders — mainly the owner(s), a tax authority, and perhaps a bank if borrowing is involved — and has limited or no legal obligation to publish its accounts publicly, meaning potential investors or competitors may struggle to access reliable financial information about it at all. A large business, especially a public limited company, must publish detailed, audited financial statements that are legally required to be made available to a very wide range of stakeholders, including thousands of individual and institutional shareholders, financial analysts, journalists, and regulators, meaning its financial performance is subject to continuous public scrutiny and can influence its share price and reputation almost immediately after results are announced.
