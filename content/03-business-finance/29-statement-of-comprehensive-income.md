## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Statement of comprehensive income | A financial statement showing a business's revenue, costs, and profit (or loss) over a trading period, usually one year | A retailer's statement of comprehensive income for the year ended 31 December |
| Revenue (sales/turnover) | The total income from selling goods or services before any costs are deducted | A shop with revenue of £200,000 in a year |
| Cost of sales (cost of goods sold) | The direct cost of producing or purchasing the goods sold during the period | Cost of the stock actually sold, including purchase price and direct production costs |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus cost of sales | £200,000 revenue − £120,000 cost of sales = £80,000 gross profit |
| Expenses (operating expenses) | Indirect costs of running the business, not directly tied to production, e.g. rent, salaries, marketing, utilities | Office rent and administrative salaries |
| Net profit (profit for the year) | Gross profit minus expenses (and other income/costs, before tax) | £80,000 gross profit − £50,000 expenses = £30,000 net profit |
| Gross profit margin | Gross profit expressed as a percentage of revenue | 40% gross profit margin |
| Net profit margin | Net profit expressed as a percentage of revenue | 15% net profit margin |
| Trading account | The section of the statement showing revenue, cost of sales, and gross profit | The "trading" part of the statement of comprehensive income |
| Profit and loss account | The section of the statement showing gross profit, expenses, and net profit | The "profit and loss" part of the statement of comprehensive income |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
When Amazon or a local corner shop wants to know "did we actually make money this year, and how much?", the answer comes from one document: the statement of comprehensive income. Investors, banks, tax authorities, and owners all read this statement before deciding whether to invest, lend, or simply keep the business running — making it arguably the single most important financial document a business produces each year.

## Core Content

### Purpose of the Statement of Comprehensive Income
The statement of comprehensive income (sometimes still called the profit and loss account, or income statement) shows a business's financial performance over a period of time — typically a financial year. Unlike the statement of financial position (Chapter 30), which is a snapshot at one point in time, the statement of comprehensive income covers a period and answers the question: "How much profit or loss did the business make?"

It is used by:
- **Owners/managers**: to assess performance and inform decisions (e.g. pricing, cost control).
- **Investors/shareholders**: to judge whether the business is a good investment.
- **Lenders (banks)**: to assess whether the business can repay loans.
- **Government (tax authorities)**: to calculate tax owed on profit.

### Structure and Formulae
The statement has two main sections:

**1. Trading Account section:**
Revenue (Sales)
− Cost of Sales
= **Gross Profit**

**Formula:** Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Sales

**2. Profit and Loss Account section:**
Gross Profit
− Expenses
= **Net Profit (Profit for the Year)**

**Formula:** Net Profit = Gross Profit − Expenses

### Worked Example: "Zainab's Boutique" (illustrative figures for a fictional small business) — Statement of Comprehensive Income for the year ended 31 December

| | £ | £ |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | | 150,000 |
| Cost of Sales | | (85,000) |
| **Gross Profit** | | **65,000** |
| Expenses: | | |
| Rent | 12,000 | |
| Wages and salaries | 22,000 | |
| Utilities | 4,000 | |
| Marketing | 3,000 | |
| Other operating expenses | 2,500 | |
| Total Expenses | | (43,500) |
| **Net Profit (Profit for the Year)** | | **21,500** |

**Step-by-step:**
1. Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Sales = £150,000 − £85,000 = **£65,000**
2. Total Expenses = 12,000 + 22,000 + 4,000 + 3,000 + 2,500 = **£43,500**
3. Net Profit = Gross Profit − Total Expenses = £65,000 − £43,500 = **£21,500**

**Interpretation:** Zainab's Boutique earned £65,000 gross profit from trading (i.e. from the difference between what it charged customers and what the goods cost to buy/produce), but after paying for running the business (rent, wages, utilities, marketing), only £21,500 of that remained as net profit. This net profit belongs to the owner (in a sole trader/partnership) or is available for tax, dividends, and reinvestment (in a limited company).

### Gross Profit vs Net Profit — What Each Reveals
- **Gross profit** reflects how efficiently a business buys/produces and prices its goods — a low gross profit might indicate the cost of sales is too high relative to selling prices, or that prices are set too low.
- **Net profit** reflects the overall efficiency of running the whole business, including overhead control — a business could have a healthy gross profit but a poor net profit if its expenses (e.g. rent, excessive staffing) are too high.

Analysing both figures together — and comparing them to previous years or to competitors — reveals much more about performance than either figure alone. (This links directly to Chapter 31, Ratio Analysis, where gross and net profit margins are calculated precisely for this purpose.)

### Common Misconceptions and Exam Pitfalls
- Students often confuse **revenue** (total sales income) with **profit** (what's left after costs) — revenue is never itself "profit."
- Students often forget that cost of sales relates only to the goods actually sold in the period, not all purchases made (a business may have bought more stock than it sold).
- Students sometimes add expenses to cost of sales incorrectly, or vice versa — cost of sales relates to production/purchase of goods, expenses relate to running the business.
- Remember: profit is not the same as cash (see Chapter 26) — a business can show a healthy net profit on its statement of comprehensive income while still suffering a cash-flow crisis if customers have not yet paid for goods sold on credit.

### Limitations of the Statement of Comprehensive Income
- It is a historical document — it shows what has already happened, not what will happen.
- It can be affected by accounting choices (e.g. how depreciation is calculated), meaning two similar businesses might report different profits using different (both legitimate) methods.
- It does not show cash position — a profitable business can still run out of cash.
- Figures alone mean little without comparison (to previous years, competitors, or industry averages) — this is why ratio analysis (Chapter 31) is essential alongside it.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Amazon.com, Inc., USA, e-commerce and technology (large business).
**Situation:** For many years after its 1997 IPO, Amazon reported very thin net profit margins (and losses in some years) despite enormous and rapidly growing revenue, as it deliberately reinvested most of its gross profit back into expenses such as warehousing, technology infrastructure, and expansion rather than maximising short-term net profit.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates the distinction between revenue, gross profit, and net profit, and shows that a business can have very high revenue and healthy gross profit while reporting minimal net profit due to high reinvestment in expenses.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss why a large business like Amazon might deliberately choose strategies that reduce net profit in the short term." (AO3)

**Company/context:** Jollibee Foods Corporation, Philippines, fast-food restaurant chain (large business with strong emerging-market and small-franchise relevance).
**Situation:** Jollibee has grown from a single ice cream parlour in Manila into one of the largest fast-food chains in Asia, consistently publishing statements of comprehensive income showing steadily rising revenue and net profit as it expanded its store network across the Philippines and internationally.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a growing business's statement of comprehensive income can be used over multiple years to track improving performance and support further expansion decisions.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the extent to which rising net profit alone indicates that a business like Jollibee is being managed successfully." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business (e.g. a sole trader like Zainab's Boutique) usually prepares a simple statement of comprehensive income, often for its own use and for tax purposes, with net profit belonging directly to the owner and no requirement to publish the statement publicly. A large business, particularly a public limited company, must prepare a much more detailed and audited statement of comprehensive income, publish it publicly (e.g. in an annual report), and answer to shareholders and financial analysts who scrutinise gross and net profit margins closely — decisions about net profit (such as how much to pay out as dividends versus reinvest) are also far more complex and strategically significant at this scale.
