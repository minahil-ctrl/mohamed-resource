## Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Example in context |
|---|---|---|
| Statement of financial position (balance sheet) | A financial statement showing a business's assets, liabilities, and equity at a single point in time | A company's statement of financial position as at 31 December |
| Asset | Anything of value owned or controlled by a business that is expected to bring future economic benefit | Machinery, cash, stock |
| Liability | An amount owed by a business to an outside party | A bank loan, money owed to suppliers |
| Non-current (fixed) asset | An asset expected to be used in the business for more than one year, not intended for resale | Premises, machinery, vehicles |
| Current asset | An asset expected to be turned into cash, sold, or used up within one year | Inventory (stock), trade receivables, cash |
| Non-current liability | A debt or obligation not due for repayment within one year | A five-year bank loan |
| Current liability | A debt or obligation due for repayment within one year | Trade payables, an overdraft |
| Equity (capital) | The owner's or shareholders' financial stake in the business; assets minus liabilities | Share capital and retained profit in a limited company |
| Accounting equation | The rule that assets must always equal liabilities plus equity | Assets £100,000 = Liabilities £40,000 + Equity £60,000 |
| Working capital | The finance available for day-to-day operations; current assets minus current liabilities | £30,000 current assets − £18,000 current liabilities = £12,000 working capital |
| Trade receivables (debtors) | Money owed to the business by customers who bought on credit | A customer who has 30 days to pay an invoice |
| Trade payables (creditors) | Money the business owes to suppliers for goods/services bought on credit | An unpaid supplier invoice due in 60 days |
| Inventory (stock) | Goods held by a business for production or resale that have not yet been sold | Unsold finished goods in a warehouse |
| Depreciation | The reduction in the recorded value of a non-current asset over time due to wear, age, or obsolescence | A delivery van losing value each year it is used |
| Retained earnings (reserves) | Accumulated profit kept in the business rather than distributed to owners/shareholders | Profit reinvested into the business over several years |

## Why This Matters (Hook / Real-World Framing)
If the statement of comprehensive income (Chapter 29) answers "how much did we earn this year?", the statement of financial position answers a different and equally vital question: "what does this business actually own, and what does it owe, right now?" When a bank considers lending to a business, when an investor considers buying shares, or when a supplier considers extending credit, one of the very first documents they request is the statement of financial position — because it reveals, in a single snapshot, whether a business stands on solid financial ground or is dangerously overloaded with debt.

## Core Content

### The Nature of a Statement of Financial Position
Unlike the statement of comprehensive income, which covers a *period* of time (usually a year), the statement of financial position is a **snapshot at a single point in time** — typically the last day of the financial year. It lists everything the business owns (assets) and everything it owes (liabilities), and shows how the difference between the two is financed (equity/capital). Because it is a snapshot, the figures on a statement of financial position can look quite different just a few weeks later as cash is received, stock is sold, or bills are paid — this is an important limitation to keep in mind and is returned to later in this chapter.

### The Accounting Equation
Every statement of financial position is built on one unbreakable rule, known as the **accounting equation**:

**Formula:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity (Capital)

This equation must always balance — hence the traditional name "balance sheet." The logic is that everything a business owns (its assets) must have been paid for by one of two sources: money borrowed from outsiders (liabilities) or money invested by the owner(s) (equity). The equation can also be rearranged to isolate equity:

**Formula:** Equity = Assets − Liabilities

This rearranged form is useful for calculating the owner's stake when only asset and liability totals are known.

### Classifying Assets
Assets are divided into two categories based on how quickly they are expected to be converted into cash or used up:

**Non-current assets (fixed assets)** are items a business intends to keep and use for more than one year to help generate revenue, rather than resell. Common examples include:
- Land and premises (buildings)
- Machinery and equipment
- Motor vehicles
- Fixtures and fittings

Non-current assets are typically shown net of accumulated **depreciation** — the recorded reduction in an asset's value over its useful life, reflecting wear and tear or obsolescence. This links to Chapter 29's discussion of accounting choices affecting reported figures: two firms depreciating identical machinery using different methods will report different non-current asset values even though the physical machinery is identical.

**Current assets** are items expected to be converted into cash, sold, or consumed within one year. They are conventionally listed in order of increasing liquidity (how easily/quickly they can be turned into cash), from least to most liquid:
- **Inventory (stock)** — raw materials, work-in-progress, or unsold finished goods
- **Trade receivables (debtors)** — money owed by customers who purchased on credit
- **Cash (and cash equivalents)** — money in hand or in the bank

### Classifying Liabilities
Liabilities are similarly split by how soon they must be repaid:

**Non-current liabilities** are debts not due for repayment within the next year, such as:
- Long-term bank loans (e.g. a five-year loan)
- Mortgages on business premises
- Long-term lease obligations

**Current liabilities** are debts due for repayment within the next year, such as:
- **Trade payables (creditors)** — money owed to suppliers for goods/services bought on credit
- Bank overdraft
- Short-term loans
- Taxation owed but not yet paid

### Equity (Capital)
**Equity** represents the owner's or shareholders' financial stake in the business — what would theoretically be left over for them if all assets were sold and all liabilities repaid. For a sole trader, this is simply the owner's capital invested plus retained profits minus any drawings. For a limited company, equity is typically shown as:
- **Share capital** — money raised from issuing shares
- **Retained earnings (reserves)** — accumulated profit kept in the business rather than paid out as dividends

### Working Capital
**Working capital** is the finance available to a business for its day-to-day running costs — paying suppliers, wages, and other short-term obligations. It is calculated as:

**Formula:** Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

A positive working capital figure indicates a business can comfortably meet its short-term debts as they fall due; a negative figure is a warning sign of potential liquidity problems, even if the business is profitable overall (linking back to Chapter 29's point that profit and cash position are not the same thing, and forward to Chapter 31's liquidity ratios, which examine this relationship precisely).

### Worked Example: "Farida's Furniture Workshop" (illustrative figures for a fictional small business) — Statement of Financial Position as at 31 December

| | £ | £ |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-Current Assets** | | |
| Premises | 90,000 | |
| Machinery and equipment | 25,000 | |
| Motor vehicle | 8,000 | |
| **Total Non-Current Assets** | | **123,000** |
| **Current Assets** | | |
| Inventory | 12,000 | |
| Trade receivables | 6,000 | |
| Cash | 4,000 | |
| **Total Current Assets** | | **22,000** |
| **Total Assets** | | **145,000** |
| **Current Liabilities** | | |
| Trade payables | 9,000 | |
| Bank overdraft | 2,000 | |
| **Total Current Liabilities** | | **11,000** |
| **Non-Current Liabilities** | | |
| Bank loan (repayable in 4 years) | 34,000 | |
| **Total Liabilities** | | **45,000** |
| **Net Assets (Total Assets − Total Liabilities)** | | **100,000** |
| **Equity** | | |
| Owner's capital | | 100,000 |

**Step-by-step:**
1. Total Non-Current Assets = 90,000 + 25,000 + 8,000 = **£123,000**
2. Total Current Assets = 12,000 + 6,000 + 4,000 = **£22,000**
3. Total Assets = 123,000 + 22,000 = **£145,000**
4. Total Liabilities = Current Liabilities (11,000) + Non-Current Liabilities (34,000) = **£45,000**
5. Net Assets = Total Assets − Total Liabilities = 145,000 − 45,000 = **£100,000**
6. Check the accounting equation: Assets (£145,000) = Liabilities (£45,000) + Equity (£100,000) → 145,000 = 145,000 ✓ **The statement balances.**
7. Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities = 22,000 − 11,000 = **£11,000**

**Interpretation:** Farida's Workshop owns £145,000 of assets in total, £45,000 of which has been financed by borrowing (bank overdraft and loan) and £100,000 by Farida's own capital investment and retained profit. Its working capital of £11,000 suggests it can comfortably cover short-term debts, though this should be checked more precisely using the liquidity ratios covered in Chapter 31.

### Common Misconceptions and Exam Pitfalls
- Students often forget that the statement of financial position is a **snapshot at one moment**, not a record of activity over a period — do not confuse it with the statement of comprehensive income (Chapter 29), which covers a period of time.
- The accounting equation must **always** balance — if a student's constructed statement does not balance, they have made an arithmetic or classification error and must find it before submitting an answer.
- Depreciation is frequently miscalculated or omitted entirely when listing non-current assets — non-current assets should generally be recorded at their value after depreciation, not their original purchase cost.
- Students sometimes misclassify a liability's term — e.g. treating a five-year loan as a current liability. Always check the repayment timeframe: within one year = current; more than one year = non-current.
- "Capital" and "cash" are often confused by students — capital is the owner's financial stake in the business (part of equity), whereas cash is simply one type of current asset.

### Limitations of the Statement of Financial Position
- It is only a **snapshot** — the figures can change significantly within days or weeks of the date shown, so it may not reflect the business's current position by the time it is read.
- **Asset valuation is often subjective** — non-current assets may be recorded at historical cost less depreciation, which may not reflect their true current market value (e.g. premises that have risen sharply in value).
- It does **not show profitability** — a business can have a strong statement of financial position (many valuable assets) while still making losses, which is why it must be read alongside the statement of comprehensive income (Chapter 29).
- It **ignores intangible factors** that may be highly valuable, such as brand reputation, staff skill, or customer loyalty, unless formally recorded as intangible assets (e.g. goodwill, patents).
- Figures are more meaningful when **compared** — to previous years or to competitors — than viewed in isolation, which is why ratio analysis (Chapter 31) is essential alongside the raw statement.

Links to Chapter 29 (Statement of Comprehensive Income), which supplies the retained profit figure feeding into equity, and to Chapter 31 (Ratio Analysis), where the figures from this statement (current assets, current liabilities, capital employed) are used to calculate liquidity and profitability ratios.

## Case Studies

**Company/context:** Apple Inc., USA, consumer technology (large business).
**Situation:** Apple has for many years maintained an extremely large balance of current assets — particularly cash and short-term investments — on its statement of financial position, giving it enormous financial flexibility to fund research and development, acquisitions, share buybacks, and dividends without needing to borrow heavily.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a large, cash-rich statement of financial position reflects financial strength and reduces reliance on external liabilities, and how analysts study the balance between current assets and liabilities to assess a company's financial security.
**Discussion question:** "Discuss why a highly profitable company like Apple might choose to hold very large amounts of cash rather than distributing it all to shareholders." (AO3)

**Company/context:** A small independent bakery chain, Kenya (small/emerging-market example, illustrative of common local start-ups).
**Situation:** Small bakeries in Nairobi typically hold most of their assets as non-current assets (ovens, shop fittings, delivery vehicles) financed substantially by bank loans in the early years of trading, meaning their statement of financial position often shows relatively high liabilities relative to equity until enough profit has been retained to reduce dependence on borrowed finance.
**Link to theory:** Illustrates how a small business's statement of financial position evolves over time as loans are repaid and equity grows through retained profit, and why lenders examine this statement closely before extending further credit.
**Discussion question:** "Evaluate the importance of the statement of financial position to a bank deciding whether to lend further money to a small, growing bakery business." (AO4)

## Large vs Small Business Application
A small business, such as a sole trader, typically has a simple statement of financial position with equity consisting largely of the owner's personal capital and retained profit, often financed by a single bank loan or overdraft, and it has no legal obligation to publish the statement beyond tax reporting requirements. A large business, particularly a public limited company, must prepare a far more complex and audited statement of financial position — including categories such as share capital, multiple reserve accounts, and potentially significant intangible assets (e.g. brand value, goodwill from acquisitions) — and must publish it publicly, where it is closely scrutinised by shareholders, financial analysts, and credit rating agencies who use it to judge the company's financial stability and borrowing capacity.
